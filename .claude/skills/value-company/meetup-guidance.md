# Meetup guidance

This file collects the rules Professor Damodaran gave in the live meetup Q&A sessions that change how an analyst makes a judgment call, reads a number, or interprets a company's behavior. It is regenerated when a new meetup transcript lands; where a later meetup refines an earlier one, only the later version is kept and the change is noted.

Meetups covered: 1 (2026-09-08), 2 (2026-09-22), 3 (2026-10-06), 4 (2026-10-20), 5 (2026-11-03)

## control — Who controls the company

- **Infer the controlling owner's end game from who appoints the board and holds the votes, then check whether the investment, financing and payout choices fit it.** [also: returns, leverage, payout] Petrobras' board was government-appointed and the government wanted the largest oil producer, so it invested below its hurdle rate, borrowed because it could not issue voting shares, and paid dividends it could not afford. Revenue or market-share goals work the same way (Japanese companies in the 1970s and 1980s). Quote: "Pick a different end game and you're going to run a business very differently." — Meetup 1, "Case study: Petrobras — every principle inverted"
- **Check what share of the votes the people running the company hold and whether outside shareholders can outvote them; if they cannot, assume strategy and capex answer to no one outside.** Founders at Facebook and Google hold small economic stakes but control votes through share classes that investors accepted at the IPO; Nvidia, Tesla and SpaceX are one share, one vote. Quote: "Then they exercise power the old-fashioned way: they outvote you." — Meetup 4, "Assessing AI with corporate finance, starting with governance"
- **Where founders hold control, expect the share price to be the only brake on a bad course, and put that in the risk assessment.** Smart, successful founders resist change hardest; the Zuckerberg metaverse spending only reversed when the share price fell, and Alphabet's non-search bets went unchallenged. This extends the meetup 4 point about investors disarming themselves at the IPO. Quote: "My concern is that seven really smart people in a room are the worst people to change course." — Meetup 5, "Why smart founders resist changing course"
- **When insiders control a public company, name what would trigger change (succession, an owner dispute, a block sale) instead of assuming shareholder pressure.** Buying the stock is still fine if the price reflects the behavior; control only changes hands through those events. Quote: "I would argue that in this company, change will come with time, as the owners age or get into a fight with each other." — Meetup 5, "Bad companies, good investments, and how change comes"
- **Read the board by whether it speaks; a board you never hear from is probably captive, and a board with no findable record is itself a finding.** For US filers, the proxy statement (DEF 14A) lists director conflicts, and almost nobody reads it. Quote: "Sometimes the best clue about corporate governance is a board that does not bark." — Meetup 1, "Q&A: boards, conflicts of interest, and the dog that didn't bark"
- **Treat a public benefit corporation or "stakeholder" language as weaker accountability, not as a stated goal.** Shareholders hold the residual claim and no contract, which is why they get priority; with many stakeholders, management can always point to another one, and with dual-class votes on top nothing short of a crime constrains it. Quote: "With multiple stakeholders, you've been handed an excuse so big that nobody can be held accountable" — Meetup 5, "Public benefit corporations and stakeholder wealth maximization"
- **Read sustainability and ESG claims as a cost or risk-management choice, and look for what the company gives up; do not credit them as value.** [also: value-vs-price] Doing the right thing is justified by long-term liability risk (the Roundup litigation), not by virtue, and selling "dirty" assets for ESG reasons just moves them to private owners. Quote: "Being good always requires giving something up." — Meetup 1, "ESG and sustainability: looking good vs. doing good"
- **Discount what management and promoters say by what they gain, especially before an IPO.** [also: value-vs-price] Nvidia, the AI labs and their bankers each have a game to play; the same applies to suppliers who fund their own customers. Quote: "I can hear you, but you have a dog in this fight. I can't take you at face value." — Meetup 3, "Why AI companies want disruption to happen fast"

## hurdle — Hurdle rate and cost of capital

- **Set the hurdle rate from the market's price of risk for the business, life-cycle stage and countries, not from what management says it wants.** Investors set it, so you can estimate it for an opaque or private company from public peers; risk also differs by stage (young firms have no settled model) and by country. Quote: "Hurdle rates are not something that is company-specific. They come from the business you're in." — Meetup 1, "Q&A: hurdle rates for a business you can't see inside"
- **Build the cost of debt from today's long-term risk-free rate plus a default spread, not from the company's past borrowing rate or its rating alone.** Add the tax benefit afterward; a rating is a shortcut that imports the agency's error, so assess default risk from debt load and earnings history. Quote: "it's not the rate at which you borrowed in the past, so don't look at your books" — Meetup 2, "Building a cost of capital"
- **Use a risk-free rate that is default-free in the currency of the analysis, so strip the default spread out of government bonds.** For euros use the lowest core AAA yield (the Dutch bond can beat the German one); for the dollar he now sits about 22 basis points under the Treasury rate; for unstable currencies estimate in dollars and convert at the end with differential inflation, because local-currency rates are too noisy. Quote: "I have to take the 2.1% out of the 13% to get a risk-free rate in reais." — Meetup 2, "The dollar risk-free rate after the US downgrade"
- **Weight the equity risk premium by where the company earns its revenue, not where it is incorporated, and use the implied premium rather than the historical average.** The historical premium assumes the past repeats. Quote: "Equity risk premiums reflect where you do business, not where you're incorporated." — Meetup 2, "Building a cost of capital"
- **Do not pad the hurdle rate; a rate above what the market is pricing makes a company lose business.** Stocks earned 9.5% a year since 1928 and the implied cost of equity in October 2026 is about 9%, so a 15–20% hurdle rejects projects that rivals take. Quote: "And if your competitors are doing the right thing, they're going to eat away your market share." — Meetup 5, "What a hurdle rate that is too high does"
- **Strip stacked premiums (small-cap, liquidity, company-specific) and VC target rates before using a rate.** Appraisers apply a liquidity premium and then a value discount for the same reason, which double counts; VCs target 25–50% and earn far less. Quote: "over the last 25 years VCs have made 12.9% a year, about half a percent less than the S&P 500." — Meetup 5, "VC target rates vs. what VCs earn"
- **For an owner with all capital in one business, compute the market-based hurdle first, add an owner-specific charge on top, and keep both numbers.** [also: value-vs-price] A business that can earn 25–30% has a barrier to entry, not a higher hurdle; you need the true rate if margins ever squeeze, and being one position in a diversified portfolio lets you take more risk than being all-in. This refines meetup 1, where the hurdle came from the market alone. Quote: "your hurdle rate can't be more than 14% or 15%, no matter how much you dance." — Meetup 4, "Before the session: a hurdle rate for a family business"
- **Do not use a risk-free rate for hedged cash flows; some risk cannot be hedged or insured, and hedging at a fair price adds no value.** Currency and interest-rate risk are easy to hedge; war and nationalization are not, so they stay in the discount rate; commodity firms that hedged did worse than those that did not. Quote: "You'll almost never be using a risk-free rate." — Meetup 3, "Q&A: hedging risk in a mining valuation"
- **Keep failure risk out of the discount rate and model it as a probability of not surviving.** [also: leverage] Even a distressed company's cost of capital stays near 11–12%, while its failure risk can be 30–50%; size that risk by cash on hand against contractual commitments. Quote: "If your concern is that Anthropic won't make it, that doesn't belong in the cost of capital." — Meetup 5, "Failure risk at Anthropic: compute commitments and cash"

## returns — Returns vs. cost of capital

- **Compare what a company says its projects earn with its realized return on capital against its cost of capital, and expect the realized number to be below.** Every ex-ante project analysis shows a positive NPV, yet in 2025 only 29% of companies earned more than their cost of capital. This updates meetups 1 and 2 (60–65% of projects below the hurdle) with the 2025 company-level figure. Quote: "Every project analysis says companies pick projects that earn more than the cost of capital, but afterward 70% of companies fail on that simple metric." — Meetup 5, "Every NPV is positive, yet 71% of companies earn less than their cost of capital"
- **Judge an investment on cash flows, not earnings.** Earnings can be made to do gymnastics and timing matters; it is also the investment decision, not the debt mix or dividend policy, that makes a company great. Quote: "Because cash talks and earnings just walks." — Meetup 2, "Investment returns and why companies take bad investments"
- **When management justifies an investment as strategic, with potential or synergy, ask where that sits in the cash flows and whether the decision came first.** Bad investments are rationalized with buzzwords after the choice is made, and because that is human nature you need a system to counter it. Quote: "In most businesses, people decide first and do the analysis afterwards." — Meetup 2, "Investment returns and why companies take bad investments"
- **Do not read a below-cost-of-capital return as a path to bankruptcy; expect the company to survive and be sold below the capital invested.** A company earning $70 million on $1 billion at a 10% cost of capital can cover its interest indefinitely; a buyer fixes the return by cutting the price. [also: value-vs-price] Quote: "They'll find willing buyers, but those buyers will pay much less than what was originally invested to create the company." — Meetup 5, "Every NPV is positive, yet 71% of companies earn less than their cost of capital"
- **Treat growth by a company earning below its cost of capital as worse than the same return at a standstill.** [also: lifecycle] Airlines are the example: the business is bad and management still expands it. Quote: "Not only are you destroying value, you can't even use the excuse that it's just a bad business." — Meetup 5, "Bad companies, good investments, and how change comes"
- **Check a company's old forecasts against actuals; if they missed in the same direction, call it bias.** Errors in both directions average out across a portfolio; 25 misses out of 25 do not. The same test applies to a company's acquisition and capex case files. Quote: "you have a bias, and you should work on reducing it in your capital budgeting." — Meetup 5, "Focus on the fragile assumption, and think in portfolios"
- **Test a claimed moat by whether a well-funded entrant could recreate it quickly, and by data on revenue drop-offs, not by a list of moat types.** [also: lifecycle] Proprietary data built over years across many clients beats single-client data; he plans to study which software businesses lost the most revenue to find where moats hold. Quote: "Is it infrastructure that takes years to create, or infrastructure that you could create overnight if you had enough capital?" — Meetup 2, "The eight software moats"

## leverage — Debt vs. optimum

- **Credit debt with value only through tax savings; where there is no tax benefit, look for another motive.** Borrowing raises both the cost of equity and the cost of debt, so without a tax benefit it only adds truncation risk; preferred stock at banks and debt in jurisdictions without a tax shield exist for other reasons. Quote: "It's the tax savings that create the benefit of debt." — Meetup 3, "Debt creates value only through tax savings"
- **Read borrowing by a private owner without a tax benefit as a way to keep control.** [also: control] Venture debt is the extreme case: founders accept failure risk before they will sell equity. Quote: "Now do you see why some businesses choose to borrow money even when it doesn't make sense? They don't want to give up ownership." — Meetup 3, "Why private businesses borrow anyway"
- **Compare the actual debt ratio with that company's own optimum, and expect most companies to be under-levered; do not read low debt as strength or as a stated goal.** Only about 5% of companies sit at their optimum, and about 80% are under-levered; under-levered is the cheaper failure for society than over-levered. Quote: "They're underlevered, about 80%. They'd rather not grow than go out and raise equity." — Meetup 3, "The trade-off, and why most companies are underlevered"
- **Check whether cheap debt is subsidized (state backing, implicit guarantee) before treating high leverage as management's choice.** Korean companies had 80% debt against a 30% optimum because lenders priced in the government's backing; when 80% of companies stumble together the backing fails. Quote: "The banks said, okay, if you're backing the debt, we'll lend at below-market rates." — Meetup 3, "South Korea in the 1990s"
- **For asset-backed leverage, test how much of a fall in asset value the equity can absorb and who would buy the assets under stress.** A 10% fall in value wipes out an owner with 10% equity, and a lender cannot sell a data center to anyone if everyone is in trouble. Quote: "If everybody is in trouble, who's lining up to buy real estate the size of 100 football fields?" — Meetup 3, "Real estate leverage and data centers"
- **Size a possible write-off in years of free cash flow, then ask whether it was funded with equity or debt.** An equity-funded write-off hurts shareholders; a debt-funded one spills to lenders and banks, and CoreWeave is the debt-funded case. [also: debt-design] Quote: "$300 billion is two years of its free cash flow." — Meetup 4, "If AI doesn't work out: write-offs vs. debt"

## debt-design — Debt design

- **Treat lease, content, compute and other contractual commitments as debt when you judge a company's leverage.** [also: leverage] Any contractual payment is debt; Anthropic has about $400–500 billion of contractual compute commitments and no profits to cover them, except for a cash cushion. Quote: "We treat them as debt. What do we do with content commitments at Netflix? We capitalize them and treat them as debt." — Meetup 5, "Failure risk at Anthropic: compute commitments and cash"
- **Fund assets with no revenue yet with equity; debt capacity follows existing cash flow.** You cannot refinance a project until it produces cash flow, and the hyperscalers funded about 80% of their data centers with equity from cash cows. Quote: "My problem is not that you funded the data center. It's that it should have been funded with equity." — Meetup 3, "Who is financing the AI capex"
- **Trace where a customer's cash comes from; revenue from a customer that the supplier itself funds or that is renting to other insiders is not demand.** [also: returns, value-vs-price] Nvidia invests in and lends to its builders, and much of xAI's revenue is data-center rental to other AI firms. Quote: "That's not revenue from AI. That's companies playing internal games and making it look like they're making money." — Meetup 2, "Who captures the value: the AI "factory""
- **For a bank, read the stability of its deposits and its duration match, not just its capital ratio.** [also: leverage] Deposit stickiness is the free funding behind the whole model; Silicon Valley Bank lost half its deposits in two days; regulators count preferred stock as equity (which is why banks issue it despite no tax benefit), and banks will need more equity. Quote: "I think this is a huge potential threat to banking, because deposits are getting less sticky." — Meetup 3, "Sticky deposits and disruption"
- **Split a convertible into a straight bond and a conversion option, and do not read a large non-cash charge from a rising equity value as cash burn.** The option's value surges when the company's value surges, so the charge reflects success; strip non-cash items (here $34 billion of a $42 billion operating loss) to see the cash loss. Quote: "It doesn't pay anything. That's why it's a non-cash charge." — Meetup 5, "Q&A: why the convertible bond produced a loss"

## payout — Cash returned vs. free cash flow to equity

- **Read dividends as a symptom of maturity, and expect the form (dividend or buyback) to follow who the shareholders are.** [also: lifecycle] Dividends are taxed in the year they are paid, so wealthier holders prefer buybacks; the end game of investing is to harvest cash. Quote: "the fact that they were old made them pay dividends" — Meetup 1, "Dividends vs. buybacks and the tax wedge"
- **Flag payouts the company cannot afford, especially while it is investing at negative cash flow.** [also: control, returns] At Petrobras the non-voting preferred shares carried a fixed dividend, so the owner's wish for control locked in the payout. Quote: "we should pay dividends even though we can't afford to." — Meetup 1, "Case study: Petrobras — every principle inverted"
- **Take a firm that is debating whether to return cash or reinvent itself as a sign that it is aging.** [also: lifecycle] For private owners the money is theirs to decide with, but give them a realistic picture of the commoditized market they would be entering. Quote: "historically when you face this choice, it's because you're aging." — Meetup 2, "Q&A: judging a product bet when software faces AI disruption"

## value-vs-price — Value vs. price

- **State the end game as value, the present value of cash flows at the hurdle rate, over any time horizon.** Negative cash flows for years are acceptable if later cash flows repay them; profits in the next year are not the goal. Quote: "Value is completely agnostic about time horizon." — Meetup 1, "The end game: from profits to cash flows to value"
- **Do not treat market price as value without testing the efficiency assumption and the transfer from lenders.** Maximizing price is a shortcut; leveraged buyouts show it, because most of their gain comes from debt, tax and lenders rather than a better business. Quote: "That's true only if markets are efficient and investors are rational" — Meetup 1, "Why stock price is a flawed shortcut — the LBO aside"
- **Separate the quality of the company from the quality of the investment; price decides.** A $70 million earner on $1 billion of assets at a 10% cost of capital is a good buy at $600 million, while Nvidia earns 35% on capital and is probably not a good investment at its price. Quote: "Bad companies can be good investments at the right price." — Meetup 5, "Bad companies, good investments, and how change comes"
- **Reverse-engineer the revenue a price needs and compare it with the pool that revenue must come from.** At $3 trillion of capex the revenue needed is $7–8 trillion, against $62 trillion of all operating expenses; meetup 4 adds that total employee pay of $26 trillion caps what agents can earn, and a vendor's revenue is capped at its customers' costs. Quote: "I reverse-engineered what revenues would need to be to justify $3 trillion." — Meetup 2, "Who captures the value: the AI "factory""
- **Count net value (gains minus value destroyed elsewhere) and do not hold everything else constant.** Real GDP grew 3.37% a year over the last 40 years against 4.15% in 1946–1986, so most technology gains moved between firms; put a win scenario and a lose scenario side by side and trace both. Quote: "we have to focus on net value, not gross value, net of the value destroyed at other businesses because AI succeeds." — Meetup 4, "Value transfer, not value creation"
- **Expect the value from a disruption to go to the firms that use the new infrastructure, not the ones that build it.** [also: lifecycle] The dot-com winner was Amazon, not the telecom and router builders. Quote: "the people who build the factory can't be the ones who collect the value from the factory." — Meetup 2, "Who captures the value: the AI "factory""

## lifecycle — Life-cycle stage

- **Classify stage by whether the business model has settled, not by company size.** A small company can be mature and a large one can be young; a company with no settled way of making money is riskier whatever its market value. Quote: "young companies are riskier often because your business model hasn't taken form yet." — Meetup 1, "Risk varies by business, life cycle, and geography"
- **Treat a mature company's acquisitions or reinvention as a possible attempt not to act its age, and require the numbers to prove otherwise.** [also: returns] Consultants and bankers sell the cure, and acquisitions can become an addiction; a few succeed, but they are the exception. Quote: "more money is wasted by businesses trying to not act their age than by any other single corporate action." — Meetup 1, "The corporate life cycle: acting your age"
- **Expect incumbents to delay and soft-pedal products that cannibalize their cash cow.** [also: returns] Their old moats may not carry over, and margins of 85–90% gross are unlikely to survive commoditization, so cost structure becomes the advantage. Quote: "Disruption never comes from the establishment. It comes from people who have nothing to lose." — Meetup 2, "Q&A: judging a product bet when software faces AI disruption"

## reporting — How to write and present the analysis

- **Start the write-up with the company's end game, then take each decision in turn and say where the company departs from what it should do.** The incentives (for example CEO pay tied to profit or market share) can explain why. Quote: "Pick a company and take it through the entire process, starting with: what's the end game for this company?" — Meetup 1, "The final project"
- **Describe the company as it is, including ugly findings, and make best judgments where data is missing.** Peloton is the example of a horror story with no rosy version; any analyst faces the same data gaps. Quote: "This is not a fairy tale you're going to tell about a company." — Meetup 1, "The final project"
- **Open the analysis with governance.** [also: control] Even if the capex looks foolhardy, who holds power determines whether it can be stopped. Quote: "So I'd start with governance, because even if you think the capex is foolhardy, you can't stop a runaway train here." — Meetup 4, "The circle of spenders and receivers"
- **Argue from the one or two assumptions that drive the value and concede the side points.** [also: hurdle, value-vs-price] He conceded that Anthropic is high-risk and defended the cost-of-capital number, and named revenue as his most fragile assumption; the distractions pile up otherwise. Quote: "So go in with your ammunition based on what you think are your biggest and most fragile assumptions." — Meetup 5, "Focus on the fragile assumption, and think in portfolios"
- **Show the process and the reason behind each number, in plain language, not only the answer.** Outcome-only answers cannot be checked, and professions hide behind complexity. Quote: "we're so caught up with outcomes that we've lost sight of process." — Meetup 4, "Outcomes vs. process"
- **When reading a filing, skip the risk-factor section and read share classes, restricted stock and convertibles.** [also: control, debt-design] The risk section is boilerplate; the equity structure and the reason for the convertible tell you who controls the company and what the losses mean. Quote: "You can skip the entire risk section; there's zero information in it." — Meetup 5, "Reading assignments"
