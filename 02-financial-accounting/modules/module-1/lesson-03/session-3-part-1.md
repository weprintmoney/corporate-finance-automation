# Lesson 3 — The Income Statement (Part 1)

The next financial statement will discuss is the income statement, also referred to as the statement of operations. Where the balance sheet was a moment in time showing what assets I own, liabilities I owe, and what equity I contributed, the income statement on the other hand measures an organization's performance over a specific period of time. Actually we're measuring the increase in the owner's equity account of the balance sheet. So it is historical, reporting what has already happened and it is rule driven, which provides comparability across companies. So how does it work? Two primary elements: revenues, also referred to as sales or sales revenue.

These are earned from selling your goods or services. When we sell a product, we record an increase in assets that we receive along with a corresponding increase in owner's equity. With the cruel based accounting, companies don't actually have to receive the cash immediately to record revenue. Instead, they can receive a promise for cash in the future, called accounts receivable. As long as it's from a legitimate creditworthy source where you're confident you will get paid. This is a big difference between accrual-based accounting and cash-based accounting. The next element are expenses. These represent the costs incurred in the process of running your normal day-to-day operations, how much I paid for the product, I sell salaries I pay my employees, rent, and so on.

This represents a decrease in owner's equity. Now accrual-based accounting requires that we match expenses with the related revenues. And that we match expenses with the time period they relate to, not necessarily when the cash is paid. So if my employees work the month of February, I will record that salary expense in February, even if I'm not paying them until March because I want to measure my performance for that specific period of time, February. The result of accrual accounting is an income statement that better measures the profitability of a company during a specific period of time.

This tells you what it really cost me to run my business for that month of February. Now the difference in revenues minus expenses equals income ,also known as profit or earnings. Let's go back to our sheet equation and incorporate this new information. We'll expand our equation to show the two components to stockholders equity. Now remember equity is comprised of original contributions or investments by owners plus the earnings of the business. We call these retained earnings. Well now we know that retained earnings can be broken down further into revenues minus expenses. Now a company has two choices with their earnings.

They can retain them and reinvest them in the business. Maybe they want to expand their operations, open new stores, invest in new equipment, or they can distribute the earnings to the owners. This is called dividends. Let's take a look at the fully expanded equation. The retained earnings portion of equity is revenues earned minus expenses incurred minus any dividends that are distributed. Let's consider a simple example with my coffee shop. If you were to pay me $10 for coffee and pastries, I would record an increase in my asset of $10 cash and I record an increase in my owner's equity of $10.

Am I balanced? Yeah! So when I sell you those goods, I now no longer have the inventory. You took the coffee and pastries. I have to record the decrease in this account. Let's assume the coffee and pastries cost me $4. I will record a decrease in inventory of $4 and a decrease in owner's equity. My revenues minus my expenses would be $6. What if I have more expenses than revenues? We call this a net loss. Now as you can see with Whole Foods on the balance sheet those revenues and expenses are buried in the retained earnings value.

So we take all of that detail and create the income statement. Next we'll examine the income statement in more detail.
