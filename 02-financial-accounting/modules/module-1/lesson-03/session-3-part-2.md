# Lesson 3 — The Income Statement (Part 2)

Now let's review the Whole Foods income statement in more detail and you're going to notice there are several meaningful subtotals. First, there's gross profit simply the revenue they earn selling their products minus the cost paid for those products. So for my coffee shop example I earn $6 of gross profit. Now this is before I consider any other expenses. How much am I making on selling my actual product? Am I pricing it correctly? We use gross profit to also calculate gross profit margin by dividing the gross profit by revenue. So in my coffee example it would be $6 divided by $10 or 60% margin.

This margin has to be sufficient to support all of my remaining expenses and leave a little extra for the investors. And you're going to learn that different industries have different margin expectations. For example, a retail industry must have higher margins to support paying retail rent versus a wholesale industry. For Whole Foods the profit margin is about 34% well this is a really meaningful metric to assess Whole Foods and compare them to their competitors to their industry, to their own performance in the past, to project into the future. Actually if you review the annual report you'll notice significant discussion of gross profit throughout the footnotes.

Moving on you'll notice the next subtotal is operating income. This is revenues minus all operating expenses this tells us how much of their revenue will become profit after you factor in their recurring day-to-day operating costs. So what do they include selling general and administrative expenses, pre-opening expenses for their stores, relocation expenses. Well you may wonder, what do these numbers really mean? Well lucky for you you have all of the detail provided in the footnote disclosures. The footnotes are full of all the juicy details companies provide descriptions of what each line item represents how they're calculated they even give you detailed reconciliations.

Take the time to review those disclosures you're going to find it's like reading a story. Finally, we get to net income the bottom line revenues minus all expenses. Everything including taxes interest the final number. Now remember net income is not the same as net cash collected, why is that? Well in accrual accounting some sales and some expenses are on credit. So they'll record them now but the cash will be paid or collected later. Now these are the accounting reporting requirements but depending on your goals you may want to include or exclude specific items. For example, if you're valuing a company and you want to measure earnings you may exclude expenses that aren't directly linked to daily operations.

Such as lease expenses or research and development expenses. The beauty of accounting is that the data is available as long as you understand it you can dissect it as necessary. Now after net income companies provide the earnings per share ratio or EPS. And that's the only ratio that's reported on the face of a financial statement. This shows us the portion of the company's net income that's allocated to each outstanding share of ownership. I also want to point out that companies can choose their fiscal year or you want to think of it as their accounting year to report their results.

Now their fiscal year is not always the calendar year. Companies want to end at the low point in their annual business activity and December 31st can be a really busy time for many businesses. In many countries it's holiday shopping so people are buying and returning goods there's just too much action going on to stop and report results. And you'll notice Whole Foods is reporting the 12 months ending in September. That's their fiscal year as long as they're consistent from year to year they pick the most meaningful 12 months to report. Now let's go back and think about these basic elements we've discussed and how it would differ across industries.

For example, with Whole Foods revenue will be earned and then recorded when they sell groceries to customers. So they can record revenue an increase in owner's equity at the point of sale. So you pay for that Apple cha-ching for Whole Foods. And that's the default rule in accounting companies must deliver the goods or services and receive a credible promise for payment before they can record revenue. But what about the difference between selling a product immediately versus a construction company that's building a bridge that might take 10 years to complete. Are we gonna make them wait 10 years to record revenue?

No, there's special rules for these types of circumstances they allow a company to recognize a portion of revenue as it's completed. Or think about the company Apple that sells an iPhone made up of the actual phone or hardware plus the software with future updates. In accounting this is called multiple deliverables or bundling. Different industries will have different revenue recognition considerations there is a lot going on behind the scenes when you buy a product.Accounting goes wild!
