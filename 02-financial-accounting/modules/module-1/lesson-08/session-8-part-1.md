# Lesson 8 — Accounting for Leases

Music Now that we have discussed the accounting rules for assets and liabilities, I'd like to turn our attention to an area that affects both of these categories and is undergoing significant change. Lease transactions. Leases represent contracts that companies enter into that provide the right to use some type of tangible asset for a period of time in exchange for payments. Think of it as a consumer deciding whether to buy or lease a car. Now in the past, the way the contract was structured could have significant financial reporting implications. Let me back up and give a little history.

Companies who were actually considering taking out a loan to purchase an asset would avoid this because they didn't want to have the loan. They would want to have the loan liability on their balance sheet. Why do you think they would care? Well, they didn't want to be weighed down by the liability. They didn't want their financial ratios to deteriorate, such as current ratio or interest coverage ratio. Many companies have bank covenants or agreements that prohibit them from taking on more debt. Lots of reasons. So they would come up with creative ways to structure the transaction so it wouldn't be considered a purchase.

Instead, they would enter into what was called an option. This would allow them to account for the lease payments as rental expense on the income statement. In other words, there was no asset and no liability on their balance sheet. We call this off-balance sheet financing. Wouldn't it surprise you to look at the balance sheet of a major airline and not see any airplanes in the assets category? This was really happening. Now, the accounting world did have a distinction between these types of operating leases and what we called capital leases. Capital leases are the types of leases that are not listed on the balance sheet.

Capital leases are the types of leases that are not listed on the balance sheet. So if certain criteria were met that showed that the lessee was assuming substantially all rights and risks for the asset, for example, if they were leasing the asset for most of the useful life of the asset, or if the present value of all future lease payments was substantially the total value of the asset, these companies did have to account for the lease transaction as a capital lease. This required that they record an asset, and more importantly, they would record the liability for future payments and recognize the related interest expense.

But it still wasn't enough. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission estimated that there was still over a trillion dollars of off-balance sheet activity related to operating leases. So companies' balance sheets did not properly reflect future lease liabilities. Imagine, you're trying to value a company and you're trying to assess their ability to pay their bills, and these obligations weren't included in the liabilities. So the rulemaking authorities, the U.S. Financial Accounting Standards Board and the International Financial Reporting Standards Board, got together back in 2006 to change their rules. And although it took about a decade, they now each have new lease standards in effect for 2019.

The most substantial change is that lessees, the company leasing the assets, are required to reflect virtually all property, plant and equipment leases on their balance sheets. There's still a distinction. Now, some are called operating leases and others financing leases. But if the lease term is greater than 12 months, the assets and liabilities will now be on the balance sheet, and there will be more footnote disclosures. So how does it work? How do you value an asset and a liability that will be paid for over time, and possibly long periods of time? You'll see some of the familiar patterns emerge again, similar to what we saw with other long-term liabilities, such as bonds and notes.

For leases, a company is usually paying over time, so there's implicitly interest included in their lease payments. Nobody's going to let you take your time to pay without getting paid interest for it. Assuming you've reviewed the prerequisite time value reading, we can now consider an example. Your company enters into a lease contract, agreeing to make annual payments of $40,000 for three years, and you calculate the implicit interest rate at 5%. The present value of those future payments would be $108,929. Now, this present value is obviously less than the present value of the future payments. Now, this present value is obviously less than the present value of the future payments.

So we record the leased asset *[transcription unclear]* as our other property, plant, and equipment, and depreciate or reduce the value of the asset over the period we benefit.

Remember, this results in systematically reducing the asset value each year and allocating that portion to expense. Now, for leased assets, we actually call it amortization expense, similar to the label we use for intangibles, even though these are tangible assets. This expense will appear on the income statement as a non-cash expense. For the related lease liability, we want to properly recognize that each $40,000 payment includes both paying down the 108,929 liability and recognizing a portion as interest expense. By the end of the three-year lease term, we will fully reduce the liability on our balance sheet, and we would have properly matched the interest expense to each of the three years we benefited from the lease.

Now, in a comprehensive accounting course, we would walk through these exact calculations for how much of the $40,000 payment reduces the liability and how much represents interest expense. But for our purposes, this is sufficient. Now, for readers of financial reports, this long-awaited change in the lease account will be a key factor in determining whether or not the company will be able to pay down the $40,000 payment. The new financial accounting rules means that the assets and liabilities related to leases will now be included on the balance sheet. The interest expense and amortization expense will be included on the income statement, and the disclosures will provide much more detail all of this effective 2019.

And since most companies provide several years of comparative and annual reports, all of this leads to more meaningful information for readers of financial statements.
