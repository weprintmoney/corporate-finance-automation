# Lesson 5 — Statement of Cash Flows

So far, we've been looking at one company and taking their historical information, we're twisting, we're turning we're magnifying it and looking at it on different dimensions. Remember the balance sheet showed us that moment of time and then we take parts of the balance sheet and magnify. So finally this brings us to the final financial statement that is required: the statement of cash flows. Now remember, everything we've discussed so far is accrual accounting; trying to focus on the substance of a transaction, not necessarily when cash trades hands. So we record revenues when they're earned and expenses when incurred.

Really meaningful information about a company's performance but it doesn't tell you what's going on with cash. If a company cannot effectively manage cash, they're gonna have a really tough time staying in business. If you're lending to a company or you're valuing a company, you want to know how are they managing cash. Some people will even argue that this is the most important financial statement. Let's return to the Wholefoods balance sheet. As we see here they report cash at the end of 2015 of 237 million. One year later, we see cash has increased to 351 million dollars.

What caused this hundred and fourteen million dollar change? Lucky for you, you don't have to look any further than the statement of cash flows. It provides a detailed explanation of that change and it shows exactly the cash coming in the door and out the door in three meaningful categories: operating activities, investing activities, and financing activities. Operating activities are those that relate to their normal recurring operations to run their business; selling their product, paying their employees should sound a little bit familiar. Remember the income statement also represented the results of operating activities but that was based on accrual-based information.

It didn't tell us the cash activity. As you see here, Whole Foods can start with their accrual-based net income and adjust it to show only the cash impact and we see here that their operating activities resulted in an overall cash inflow of over a billion dollars, a billion 116. And that is what we would expect for a company that has a strong customer base that's been in operations for many years. That tells us that at the end of the day, they're collecting more cash from their customers than it costs them for their operating expenses, like paying their rent, and their salaries, and so on.

The next category shows us the cash impact from their investing activities, things like buying new equipment, or buildings, or land. In this case, we see Whole Foods has a cash outflow of 895 million dollars and you can see exactly why. In fact, the top two lines show us they're investing in new locations and in property plant and equipment. Finally, the last category provides details on their financing activities, sources of funding things like borrowing money, selling stock to investors, paying dividends. This statement summarizes all of that activity and explains the 114 million dollar change in cash over a period of time.

You see at the bottom, they actually reconcile the two values on the balance sheet. Again, remember the accounting rules dictate what activities are included in those three categories. So there's comparability across periods and across companies. Companies actually provide three years of comparable information. Companies even go a step further and they will disclose more information about the free cash flow that focuses on the cash provided by operating activities minus capital expenditures. In other words, can the company generate enough operating cash flows to fund the capital expenditures they require? Can they pay for those new stores and the new equipment and the land that they need?

The disclosures are full of meaningful information. Now let's put it all together. Let's take a look at a simple example and see how all of the financial statements are linked. Here, you see the balance sheet provides that snapshot, that moment in time. Now we're going to take parts of it and magnify it. Why did cash change? Boom statement of cash flows. Why did stockholders equity change? Boom statement of stockholders equity. And finally, we see how the income statement fills in that gap and explains the change in retained earnings and in stockholders equity. All of this data is available for you to dissect and interpret.

Now you try it for yourself.
