# Lesson 2 — The Balance Sheet (Part 1)

All right so let's begin our discussions of the four required financial statements that public companies provide. Now when I say public this specifically refers to those companies that the public meaning all of us can invest in and become shareholders or owners in by buying shares of their stock. We hold those companies to a different standard than private companies, and we should right ? If they're coming to the public to solicit investments or ownership and they're providing accounting information and they say hey look how great we're doing come invest in us give us your money. Well that accounting information better be relevant, reliable, and comparable.

Let's start with the balance sheet and stay with me I am now going to unveil the backdrop for the entire accounting system if you remember anything about accounting this is it, the accounting equation. Assets equals liabilities plus stockholders equity. Let's define these elements: assets probably heard that term before these represent probable future economic benefits owned by a company economic resources that will help generate future cash inflows what comes to mind hopefully cash that's a pretty good asset equipment land it's what they own and think about it for yourself. If you purchase a car so you can drive to work to earn your salary this car is an asset an economic resource that will help you generate future income in the form of your salary.

Let's move to the right side of the equation the elements that follow the equal sign this side of the equation represents the sources of financing or funding for a company companies need money and they either borrow or they get money from investors or shareholders which is called equity. Liabilities these are economic obligations to outsiders or claims that outsiders have against assets. Think about if you get a loan from a bank it's what a company owes. Finally, stockholders equity this is also called shareholders equity owner's equity we have a lot of synonyms in accounting by the way.

This category is comprised of two components first are the contributions or investments provided by owners we call this contributed capital, paid in capital, common stock lots of synonyms. Second, are the earnings of the company that are retained and reinvested into the business. The balance sheet really could be called the statement of financial position. It shows a company at a specific moment in time a snapshot. Now every economic transaction of a company will impact the accounting equation and the equation must always balance. Let's think about it with a real example, I have decided I want to start a business I love coffee and I want to open up a little coffee stand.

I think I have a great idea for something new so I'm going to open up Shahadah coffee stand alright maybe I'll come up with a more creative name later but just go with this. Let's take a look at how my business transactions impact the accounting equation. I need to get things started so I'm going to contribute or invest $5,000 of my money to open the coffee stand. So I go to the bank and I open an account in the name of my new business. Take a look you'll see that the cash account will now have a balance of $5,000 and my stockholders equity will also have a balance of $5,000.

That side represents my ownership in the business. The equation is balanced. All right let's keep going I need to actually get the product or the coffee that I'm going to sell my customers. Right now the product that we sell our customers has a very specific label in accounting we call it inventory. And this is an asset because it will help generate future cash inflow when I sell it. So I'm going to spend $1,000 of my cash purchasing the actual coffee supplies: the coffee, the sugar, the little stirring sticks, the cups. Well now I need to adjust my cash account to properly reflect that I only have four thousand dollars left.

Right? I spent a thousand and I'm going to create a new asset account called inventory. And properly record the thousand dollar value. So everything happened in the assets category and we're still at five thousand dollars. And of course my equation is still balanced. Well now I need to buy some equipment I need the coffee maker and cash registers so I spend three thousand dollars but instead of spending cash the store agrees to let me pay next month. So I have a new asset equipment and an accompanying liability or a future obligation. I'm going to call this new liability, accounts payable.

Now I'm realizing that I need more money to get things moving for my business. I go to the bank and they agree to lend me five thousand dollars and I'm gonna pay them back in a few months. Now I'm gonna record this liability in a different account called, loan payable. I'm going to keep things separate so I can keep track. I also now have the increase in my cash account. Bringing my total cash balance to $9,000. I can stop and summarize all of this activity all of the increases and all of the decreases and create a balance sheet.

Look this shows my financial position at this moment in time. There it is just like magic now a comprehensive accounting course would get into much more detail about the mechanics behind this but for our purposes this is sufficient. Now I'd like you to take a moment to review the balance sheet of a company of your choosing. Do the assets equal the liabilities plus stockholders equity? I promise you they will.
