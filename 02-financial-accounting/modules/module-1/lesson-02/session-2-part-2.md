# Lesson 2 — The Balance Sheet (Part 2)

All right let's continue our discussion of the financial statements that public companies are required to provide. In the U.S. companies submit their annual financial information called a 10k to the Securities and Exchange Commission, this is the regulatory government agency that oversees the U.S. securities market. They also have to submit quarterly reports called 10-qs and other countries have similar requirements. Let's begin with the balance sheet and examine the 2016 balance sheet for the public company Whole Foods this is a grocery store chain. Now first I want to point out that Whole Foods actually provides two years of balance sheets so you can have comparable information or benchmarks.

You should also notice that at the top they tell us the numbers are presented in millions meaning you can add six zeros to every number you see. Now the first thing I hope you're looking for is that for 2016 the assets of six billion three hundred and forty-one million are equal to the total liabilities plus stockholders equity we're good there. I also want to highlight that companies classify their assets into current versus non current and they do the same for their liabilities. Current by definition means that they will use these assets or convert them to cash within the next twelve months.

So why is that important? Why do you think that most companies classify their balance sheets? Well, in looking at Whole Foods I see that they have a billion 3 41 of current liabilities coming due in the next twelve months. So the next obvious question is: are they ready do they have sufficient current assets to meet this obligation? Where are you going to look? Obviously they can't pay their bills with a big piece of land they have to pay their bills with money so we will look at their current assets to determine if they're prepared. The balance sheet even goes a step further and lists those current assets in order of liquidity.

How soon a company will convert the asset to cash or use it so cash is always listed first as it's already liquid they do the same for liabilities. How soon a company's bills are due it makes for a much more meaningful report for investors so you can assess their standing. And do you notice how they provide detailed accounts within each categories. So you can see their assets include cash, short-term investments, and so on. Now all companies provide footnote disclosures that accompany the balance sheet and these provide much more detail on the rules they follow. For example, let's take a look at the merchandise inventory asset line-item.

They tell us they have five hundred and seventeen million dollars of inventory for 2016. Well that's just a number, what does that mean? Let's take a look at the footnote disclosure so we can get behind that number and learn more. You see here that they tell us what inventory valuation method is used and for what percentage of their inventory. In accounting you'll learn later we have several acceptable inventory valuation methods that companies can use and there's rules on how those are calculated what impact the choice they make has on their financial reporting. But overall what you'll find is that the financial statements aren't just full of numbers they provide valuable data about a company's position and explanations for the choices that management makes and all companies are providing comparable formatting comparable details.

It allows you to take all of this information and use it for your own purposes.
