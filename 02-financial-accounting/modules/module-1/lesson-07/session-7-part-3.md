# Lesson 7 — KPMG: Understanding Debt

You know that companies finance all asset purchases with either debt or equity. Debt can take many different forms. One is a loan. A company goes to a bank, negotiates a contract to borrow a set amount of money, and pays it off over time with interest. Loans are a very common form of financing for businesses and people. Owing a lot of money through a loan to one entity gives that entity a sizable amount of leverage over the company. Some loans are secured with assets, collateral that can be repossessed if the borrower fails to make payments. Another way for companies to borrow money is to issue bonds.

Bonds trade on an exchange just like stock. Unlike a loan, however, the bondholders don't individually have sway over the company. This can be good from a decision-making standpoint. But if the company gets into trouble and has a hard time making money, there's no one with whom to renegotiate terms like there is in a bank loan. A bond states three things. Its par value, also called its face value, its coupon rate, which is stated as a percentage of par, and its maturity date. These three things provide the information about the cash flows the company is promising to the bondholder.

The face value is the lump sum that the borrower is promising to pay at the maturity date. The coupon is the cash that the borrower is promising to pay at the maturity date. It's called a coupon because before electronic records, bonds literally had detachable coupons around them that bondholders would remove and send in to collect their interest payment.

The coupon is stated as a percent of face value and is typically paid annually or semiannually. Here's the Blue Hat Company, headquartered in Boise, Idaho, and maker of fine hats for professional accountants. You are the Chief Financial Officer. You've decided to issue bonds bonds to finance an expansion into England. First, you need to decide whether to issue the bonds in US dollars or in British pounds sterling. Whichever you choose, that's the currency you'll use when you make payments to the bond holders. You decide to issue dollar denominated bonds to avoid the risk of unfavorable currency changes over time.

You arrange for an issue of 10-year bonds, each with a $10,000 par value and a coupon of 5% payable semi-annually on June 30th and December 31st. This means that every six months for the next 10 years, Blue Hat will pay each bondholder $250 and will also pay the bondholders the $10,000 par value at the end of the 10-year period. How much would a bond holder be willing to lend Blue Hat in exchange for those promises? How much cash will Blue Hat raise from this bond issue? You need to know how much you'll have to work with to finance the expansion.

Lenders will determine how much compensation they want for lending to Blue Hat and will bid for the bonds accordingly. Their bid price will be a function of their opportunity costs determined through their net present value calculations and how risky they think lending the money to Blue Hat will be. Their required compensation will be Blue Hat's effective interest rate. Blue Hat is promising to pay bondholders $250 every six months for 10 years and $10,000 at the end of 10 years for a total of $15,000 in cash. Blue Hat could decide to pay off the bonds sooner and avoid future coupon payments.

When Blue Hat issues a bond, you will record a liability of $10,000. If lenders think Blue Hat is riskier than other investment options, they might demand an effective interest rate higher than the coupon rate and pay less than par value less than $10,000 for each bond. If bondholders pay exactly face value, then the interest expense over the entire 10-year life of the bond, the money paid in excess of the money borrowed, is $5,000, the same as the 5% coupon. If bondholders buy the bonds at a discount, if they demand a higher effective interest rate than the coupon, Blue hats effective interest expense over the life of the bond is greater than $5,000. Blue hat is promising the same $15,000 in payments, but receives less money from the bond issuance. In other words, they are able to borrow less money.

If bondholders buy the bonds at a premium, in other words, if they bid prices to a lower effective interest rate than the coupon, the effective interest expense over the life of the bond is less than $5,000. Blue hat is promising the same $15,000 in payments, but receives more money from the bond issuance. In other words, they are able to borrow more money. It is important to note that discounts aren't necessarily bad and premiums aren't necessarily good. They're simply a reflection of market demands at the time the bonds are issued. Understanding how the market will price the bonds will help you make more accurate plans for your UK expansion and any other financing you do in the future.
