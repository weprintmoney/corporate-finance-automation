# Lesson 6 — Measurements of Assets (Part 1)

Alright, so now that we've covered the basic elements of the balance sheet, let's get into more detail. First, I have a confession before we begin, and some of you will be really surprised to hear this, but accountants are conservative. No, I'm not talking politically or socially, I mean financially. Outside investors are relying on our financial reporting, so we don't want to be overly optimistic or misleading. So you'll see a conservative pattern in the rules. So how do accountants value assets? Let's start with current assets, defined as those that will be used or convert to cash within the next 12 months.

Let's take a look at Estee Lauder's balance sheet and focus on those current assets that require special consideration. Accountants. Accounts receivable. These represent amounts that customers owe for inventory that was already delivered. So for Estee Lauder, their customers could be department stores around the world. They ship them cosmetics and they send them a bill that gives the customer, say, 30 days to pay. Until Estee Lauder receives that cash, they record a different asset in exchange for delivering their goods. Accounts receivable. But do you notice that little word net after accounts receivable? Actually, no. You'll see this word sprinkled throughout the financial statements of every company.

Net means that you've subtracted something from a number. So for accounts receivable, this means that there will always be a few customers that end up not paying their bills. Either they go bankrupt or there's some sort of dispute. Personally, I call these deadbeats. But companies will reduce their account receivable value by an estimated uncollectible amount for a given period. This amount becomes an expense for the specific period. Bad debt expense or uncollectible account expense. It shows up on the income statement as part of operating expenses. Let's keep going and discuss inventory. The product a company sells to their customers.

So imagine a company sells bicycles. They purchase, let's say, 100 bikes for a total of $10,000. They would record an asset called inventory. Let's take a look. Let's take a look at our balance sheet equation. See, I told you it would always balance. So if a customer pays $12,000 for the entire lot of bikes, you should recall that we match the revenue earned with the cost of goods sold expense. The difference represents the gross profit for the sale. So note that when they sell their product, that inventory asset converts to the cost of goods sold expense.

But the special case is that the cost of goods sold expense is not the cost of goods sold The special case is that the cost of goods sold expense is not the cost of goods sold So you say 10 bikes at $90, and another 10 bikes at $93, and another 10 at $102.

How do they identify which bikes are sold? And it matters. Whichever bikes they sell impacts the expense they recognize on their income statement. Well, if you're selling millions of bikes, it would be so cumbersome to keep track of exactly which bikes were sold and which bikes are remaining as an asset. Well, in accounting, we are really reasonable, and we like to provide choices. So we have different inventory valuation methods that allow companies to choose how they will allocate that original asset value, meaning whether it's the $90 bike, the $102 bike, to cost of goods sold expense.

You're probably surprised that it's not based on the original value. It's based on the actual bike that's sold. There's tons of rules and calculations behind the methods, but just note that whatever method they choose, the companies have to be consistent in applying it, and they have to disclose it. And by the way, we see conservatism in this as well. We record inventory at the price we paid, as we've discussed, historical costs, unless it declines below that cost in the market. Then we have to reduce the asset value to the lower value. So, for example, if I purchased one bike for $90, and now the price to purchase it decreased to $75, I have to decrease the asset on my records to the lower of cost or market.

Actually, that's the name of the rule, lower of cost or market, or if you're in the know, LCM. I mention this point because when you're reading financial data, it's important to note that assets are listed at historical costs or less. Not at the lowest. That's the rule. Not actual market value if it's higher. Now that we've reviewed the current or short-term assets, let's turn our attention to non-current assets.
