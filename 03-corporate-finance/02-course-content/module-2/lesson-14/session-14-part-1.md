# Session 14 Part 1 — From Hurdle Rates to Cash Flows

So now that we've estimated hurdle rates for your company, let's move on to the other half of the investment principle. Let me remind you again of what the principle says: "You want to invest in projects that earn a return that is greater than your minimum acceptable hurdle rate," right? So let's talk about returns.

## Show Me the Money: Cash Flows vs. Accounting Earnings

If there's a theme that drives how I think about returns, it's that line from Jerry Maguire. Remember "Show me the money."? It's not show me the accounting income, it's not show me the earnings, it's show me the money. And the key theme that drives how we think about returns in finance is we think in terms of cash flows.

To understand what the distinction is, let's talk about why accounting earnings and cash flows can lead to very different endgames or very different numbers. I'm not an accountant, but when I look at accounting from the outside, there are two broad principles that govern accounting.

The first is the principle of accrual accounting. What does accrual accounting require you to do? It requires you to record transactions as they happen. So if you sell something on December 30th, you have to show these revenues this year even though you haven't been paid for it yet. And when you show something as revenues, you have to show all the expenses associated with those revenues even though you might not have paid for them yet either. So accrual accounting requires transaction-based recording.

The second broad principle that drives accounting is accountants like to categorize expenses. Specifically, expenses can be categorized as operating expenses, capital expenses, or financing expenses. Operating expenses are expenses designed to create benefits this year. So raw material, labor, those are operating expenses. Capital expenses are expenses that create benefits over many years, land, building, equipment, classic examples. And financing expenses are expenses associated with the use of debt.

Each has its place in an accounting statement. Operating expenses show up in your income statement right below revenues to get to operating income. Capital expenses don't get shown in the year that they're made, but they get spread out over time as depreciation. And financing expenses show up below the operating income line, to get from operating income to net income. Everything has its place, but it's precisely those two principles that lead accounting earnings to be different from cash flows.

## Three Adjustments from Earnings to Cash Flows

In fact, if you give me the accounting earnings for a company or a project to get the cash flows, there are three adjustments I need to make, and only three.

The first thing I need to do is add back non-cash expenses, expenses like depreciation. Why? Because even though you've reduced my income for those expenses, I haven't paid anybody for those expenses. So I'm going to add back depreciation and amortization.

Second, I'm going to subtract out capital expenditures. Why? Because even though you tell me it's not an expense, I've got to pay for that land, pay for that building. It's a cash flow.

And finally, I'm going to subtract out change in working capital. You think, "What does that do?" It takes my accrual earnings and makes them into cash earnings, and think of why. Remember how I described accrual accounting? If you sell something on December 30th, I said you have to show it or record it this year as revenues even though you haven't been paid yet. That's going to show up in your balance sheet as accounts receivable. And I said you have to show expenses even though you might not have paid for them yet. Now, that's going to show up as accounts payable. And if you're using stuff that you bought last year or you're going to carry over into the next year, that shows up as inventory. Working capital is a residue of accrual accounting, and the change in working capital makes your accrual earnings into cash earnings.

So let me summarize. To get from earnings to cash flows, you add back depreciation, you subtract CapEx, you subtract that change in working capital.

Now, if you think about it, your accounting earnings and your cash flows can be very different numbers. You could have a company with positive earnings and negative cash flows, that's especially true for growth companies, or you can have a company with negative earnings and positive cash flows, more uncommon, but it can happen. So when your earnings are different from your cash flows, here's what we argue for in finance, trust the cash flows. Why? Because you cannot spend earnings.

Here's a second broad principle we're going to draw on when you think about returns. We care about incremental cash flows. What does that mean? We care about cash flows that are created by a project, not cash flows that would exist anyway. And finally, these cash flows have to be time-weighted. Sounds fancy, but you'd rather get the cash flows earlier rather than later, right? In fact, when you discount cash flows, you're time-weighting the cash flows. A good measure of investment return will be cash flow-based, be based on incremental cash flows, and we time-weight those cash flows. As we look at different measures of return, I'd like you to think about whether these different measures pass this test.

## The Disney Rio Theme Park Illustration

So let's take a hypothetical project so that we can use it to illustrate this process. The project I'm going to describe is a project that many of you might be able to relate to. It's two theme parks that Disney's planning to open in Rio. So hypothetical.

The first theme park is a replica of Magic Kingdom, and every Disney theme park is built around the Magic Kingdom. I'm going to assume it's going to take a year to build and be available for operations at the start of the second year. The second theme park is going to be a variation of Epcot, the theme park that's at Disney World in Orlando. And that's going to take longer to build, three years, and be available for operations at the start of the fourth year.

I'm going to be a number cruncher, and I'm going to give you all the numbers in U.S. dollars. That's cheating, right? After all, if this is a theme park or these are theme parks in Rio, the cash flows will be in nominal reals. But I'm going to assume that as a Disney manager, you're more used to seeing numbers in U.S. dollars. A little later we'll come back and ask the question, would the analysis be different if I did everything in nominal reals?

## Project Assumptions and Investment Outlays

So here's the first step. I'm going to give you the assumptions that are going to underline the numbers you're going to see. The first assumption is that Disney has already spent a half a billion dollars researching this theme park. And if it decides not to open these theme parks, that money's not coming back. We're going to come back and talk about what happened to that half a billion.

Here are the additional investments they will need to make if they decide to open these theme parks. The cost of constructing Magic Kingdom, I'm going to assume, is going to be $3 billion, with $2 billion spent right away and a billion a year from now. The cost of constructing Epcot is going to be a billion and a half, with a billion spent at the end of the second year, and a half a billion at the end of the third year. Notice how my expenditures to build this theme park are spread out over time? These investments are all going to be treated as capital expenditures and depreciated over time.

## Forecasting Revenues and Expenses

Now, to estimate the revenues, the earnings, and the cash flows in this theme park, I'm going to draw on what I already know. I already have theme parks on the ground, Euro Disney, Tokyo Disney, Disneyland, Disney World. I use their history as a basis for forecasting out revenues and earnings in the theme park.

So if you take a look at my projections, here's what I'm projecting. No revenues in year one, why? Because I'm still constructing. Starting in year two, you see Magic Kingdom kick in. In year four, you see Epcot kick in. The collected revenues in theme parks are estimated based on how quickly my existing theme parks started to show revenues. I also estimate revenues in the resorts that I plan to build at the Rio theme park. Those revenues are estimated to be 25% of my theme park revenues. Why 25%? Because that's what I make in Orlando on my existing theme parks and resorts.

Then I estimate expenses. My direct expenses for the theme parks, I assume, will be 60% of my revenues, and I estimate that expense. And again, that 60% is based from the existing theme parks. For the resorts, I assume my direct expense will be 75%. You think, "What do you mean by direct expenses?" These are the expenses associated with operating the theme parks, the actual operations. I subtract those out.

Then come to accounting expenses. The first, of course, is depreciation and amortization. Those big upfront costs get written off over time, that's my depreciation. And the second accounting expense is called the allocation of G&A. You think, "What is that?" That's Disney's general and administrative cost being allocated to this project. Why am I doing this? Because Disney's a big company with cost that need a place to go. This is perhaps true for every large company, is expenses get allocated to projects. I'm going to allocate that expense.

Just as an aside, I should also tell you that two thirds of these allocated expenses that I'm showing on this page have nothing to do with this theme park. In other words, if I don't take these theme parks, they'll go somewhere else in the company. They're not going to go away. That's going to become relevant later in the process, but for the moment, I'm going to treat them as expenses.

## Building the Operating Income Table

So I've everything I need. I've my expected revenues, I subtract out the direct expenses, I subtract out the depreciation, I subtract out the allocated G&A. I come up with my operating income from this theme park.

Notice in year one, I have a $50 million loss. And then if you look at just below the $50 million losses, you'll notice that I'm claiming up plus $18 million. You think, "What is that?" Basically, that's the taxes I'm going to save because I have a $50 million loss. I'm in effect assuming that when I have a loss on a project as Disney, I'll find profits elsewhere to offset it against. In year two, I still lose money. I still claim a tax savings, I come up with an after-tax operating income. I do this every year for the next 10 years. I come up with operating income each year, and come up with the after-tax operating income.

Now, I'm going to ask you to think about whether you want to take these projects or not, or these theme parks or not. But to make that judgment, if I just showed you these accounting earnings, you can see very quickly why making a decision based just on accounting earnings is going to be really tough to do. These numbers, after all, change over time. Depending on the year that you look at, the project loses money or makes a lot of money, and it's not scaled to anything on the project. In other words, in year 10, the $421 million dollars looks like a lot of money in terms of after-tax operating income, but relative to what? If you invested $4 billion or $10 billion on this project, that's going to make a difference in whether you take this project or not. But for the moment, this is a good start in terms of looking at the project, at least we have estimated accounting earnings from the project.

Now, this part of the project might be difficult for you to replicate in your company. To get numbers on internal projects, you need access to the company's data. But for the moment, if you want to make up a hypothetical project for the company, go ahead. So if you looking at the Home Depot, think of what the earnings would look like for a typical store. That's a good place to start thinking about how your company creates value or doesn't create value.
