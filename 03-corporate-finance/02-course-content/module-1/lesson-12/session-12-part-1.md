# Session 12 Part 1 — Cost of Debt and What Counts as Debt

## From Cost of Equity to Cost of Capital

The last few sessions, we've talked about how to estimate the cost of equity for a company. For publicly traded companies, we said that cost of equity should be based on just the risk that you cannot diversify away. With private businesses, we brought in the rest of the risk. But remember, equity is only one way of funding a business. What if you use debt? In this session, we're going to look at bringing in debt into the hurdle rate.

So, let us start off with a very simple question. If you're a company and you have to raise money, should you use equity or debt? Many companies use both. And if you use both, then to get to a cost to capital, I need not just a cost of equity, but also a cost to debt. So in this session, I'd like to start off by looking at what debt is and how to attach a cost to that debt.

## Three Criteria for Classifying Debt

So, let's start with the easier of those two questions, or at least in theory, what should be easier. What is debt? That might sound like a strange question. You're saying, "Why not just look at the balance sheet? Don't accountants tell us how much debt you owe?" Well, if it were that easy, we could compute cost of capital much more quickly than we do.

So, I'm going to lay out three criteria I look for to classify something as debt. The first is, debt gives rise to a contractual commitment. You remember when you issue equity, people might buy shares in your company, hoping, expecting you to pay a dividend? But if you don't pay a dividend, you cannot get sued. There is no contractual obligation to pay a dividend. But if you borrow money and you agree to make interest payments, that's a contractual obligation. So, first feature of debt is as a contractual obligation you have to make.

Second, imagine that word when you meet that obligation by making that interest payment, it is tax deductible.

Third, if you fail to meet that contractual obligation, bad things happen to you. What bad things? You might be shut down as a company. You might go bankrupt. Lenders might get your entire business.

Fixed payments. Tax deductible. Loss of control. Those are the features I would look for in order to categorize something as debt.

## Applying the Criteria to the Balance Sheet

So, let's apply those three criteria to a balance sheet to see what would be included in debt and what should not be. If you take on bank loans or corporate bonds, obviously, it is debt. Why? Because you have a contractual obligation to make those coupon payments or interest payments, and you're going to include that in debt. And that's going to be true whether you're short-term debt or long-term debt. All interest-bearing debt is, obviously, debt.

Let's look at something else in the balance sheet. What about accounts payable, supplier credit? Those show up as liabilities, right? I'm not going to include them in debt, and here's why. They don't include an explicit interest payment. Notice what I said, "Explicit." There is an implicit interest payment because when you use supplier credit, you usually give up discounts. If you're willing to convert those implicit payments, those lost discounts into explicit payments as interest expenses, you can trade accounts payable and supplier credit as debt. But usually, that's too difficult to do, so we're not going to include accounts payable and supplier credit as debt.

I'm not also going to include underfunded pension and healthcare obligations, which increasingly show up in balance sheets in the U.S.' debt, because they have no teeth in the U.S. Put differently, there is no contractual obligation you enter into to cover those underfunded pension obligations. Until that becomes true, I'm not going to include that in debt.

Now, here's where it gets interesting. There are items that are not on the balance sheet that meet the criteria for debt. Most importantly, if you're a retailer or a restaurant company and you lease your premises, remember what that lease agreement looks like, 10 years, half a million dollars a year, a contractual obligation. That payment is tax deductible. And if you fail to make that payment, you lose that store or restaurant. To me, all leases look like that whether they're classified as operating or capital leases in accounting.

So, all interest-bearing obligations are, obviously, debt. All lease commitments are debt. So, what is debt? It's not just the debt you show on your balance sheet because in some companies, your biggest debt might be off the balance sheet.

## Defining Cost of Debt: Long-Term and Today

Now, let's talk about what the cost of debt is. The cost of debt is the rate at which you can borrow money, long-term, today. Keywords are "Long-term" and "Today."

Let's start with the long-term. If you look at a typical company, you'll see debt, six-month debt, one-year debt, two-year debt, short-term, as well as long-term debt. It's a mess. Here's what I'm going to assume. If you have short-term debt that the rolled-over cost of that short-term debt is equal to the long-term debt. So, I'm going to take all of your debt, short-term as well as long-term, and treat it as if it were long-term debt.

You're saying, "What's it today?" When I ask you what your cost of debt is, you might be inclined, perhaps even tempted, to give me your debt from two years ago, five years ago and point to the interest payments you're making on that debt. That's called a book interest rate. I'm not interested in that rate. I need to know the rate at which you can borrow money today.

And if you think about what's going to go into it, it's very simple. You're going to start with the risk free rate and you're going to add a default spread, a credit spread, based on how much default risk lenders see in you. So, the cost of debt boils down to estimating what that default spread should be.

## Three Scenarios for Estimating the Default Spread

So, I'm going to give you three scenarios, starting with the easiest one ending with the most difficult one.

Here's your easiest scenario for estimating the cost of debt. You've been asked to estimate the cost of debt for a company that has issued bonds, and those bonds are traded and are simple bonds. Simple bonds, in what sense? You haven't loaded them up with special features. Here's all you need to do. Look up the yield to maturity on that bond, a current market interest rate on the bond, and you could use that as your cost of debt.

That works in about 1 out of 100 companies. Why? Because even companies that have bonds outstanding, either those bonds are loaded up with special features making the interest rate difficult to read, or the bonds might not be traded. But here's the advantage you have when you're a company issuing bonds. You have a bond rating, right? A bond rating is what a ratings agency has attached to you as its measure of default risk. To the extent that you trust the ratings agency, you can take that bond rating, which ranges from AAA all the way down to D, and convert it into a default spread because there are publicly traded bonds in each ratings class. That's pretty simple too. If you got a default spread, you come up with the cost of debt.

You're saying, "But what if I don't have a rating or bonds?" Ninety percent of all companies globally, have neither bonds outstanding nor a bond rating. How do you come up with the cost of debt for those companies? Well, you got to act like a ratings agency and attach a rating to these companies. Sounds complicated, but it's actually much simpler than it looks. I call it a synthetic rating. And the advantage of a synthetic rating is I can use that synthetic rating to come up with a default spread, add that default spread to the risk free rate and come up with the cost of debt.

So, cost of debt is all about estimating the default spread.

## Currency Consistency and Wrap-Up

One final point. Remember that currency choice you made when you estimated your cost of equity? You decided to estimate your cost of equity in U.S. dollars, or pesos, or reais. You got to stick with that currency when it gets to cost of debt. You cannot switch currencies even if your company borrows in a different currency because in one cost to capital, there can be only one currency. So, your cost of debt has to be estimated in exactly the same currency as your cost of equity.

So, if your company has no debt, you're home free. You have nothing to do. But if your company does have debt, your job is to assess both how much debt it has and what that cost of debt is. Look for the easy way out first. See if you can find a bond rating for your company. And if you can find a bond rating, BBB, BB, A, use that bond rating to come up with a default spread, add that default spread on to the same risk free rate used to build up your cost of equity, and you've got your cost of debt.
