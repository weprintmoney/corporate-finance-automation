# Session 3 Part 1 — Alternatives to Maximizing Stock Prices

Continuing with the discussion of maximizing stock prices as the objective. Remember in the last session we talked about the utopian world from which the subject was born and what can go wrong in the real world. Let's continue with that discussion.

So when you look at the objective in corporate finance as maximizing the value of the business, being narrowed down to maximizing the value of equity, and then from that to maximizing stock prices, we talked about all of the flaws in the real world that can make it a dangerous assumption. We talked about how you can maximize stock prices by ripping off bondholders, how you can maximize stock prices by lying to markets, or perhaps how you can maximize stock prices by creating social costs.

## Three Choices If You Reject Maximizing Stock Prices

So what do I do now? What if I don't want to use as objective, what can I use instead? So let's look at the choices you face if you don't buy into maximizing stock prices as your objective.

The first choice is to pick a different corporate governance mechanism. Hey Singh, what are you talking about? Well, if you as shareholders are not going to keep an eye on managers and fire them when they're not doing a good job, somebody has to do it for you, right? So we'll talk about other corporate governance mechanisms that have developed around the world to keep managers in line.

The second way in which you can deal with the fact that you don't like maximizing stock prices as an objective, is to pick a different objective: maximizing revenue growth, maximizing earnings, maximizing market share. We'll talk about those alternative objectives and ask, "Are those objectives better than maximizing stock prices? Do they offer a lower downside?"

The third and final way of dealing with the flaws we've talked about is to continue to focus on maximizing stock prices, but do the best we can to minimize the flaws. Perhaps we can put in constraints into the process: constraints that keep managers in line even if shareholders have very little power, constraints that keep companies from ripping off bondholders, constraints from markets wandering away too far away from the correct value, and finally, constraints and social costs that might keep you from going out and doing something that hurt society.

## Alternative Corporate Governance: Keiretsu and the German System

So let's take the first of these choices. Maybe there's a different way to get rid of bad managers. Let me take you back a long time. At the end of the Second World War, Germany and Japan were in shambles, they had no economy. And over the next 40 years, Germany and Japan rebuilt themselves in true economic miracles to become the second and third largest economies in the world. How did they do it? They didn't follow the U.S. rule book of shareholder power, instead they relied on a different mechanism.

Let's take the Japanese first. They created Keiretsu. Keiretsu are companies held together by cross-holdings, maybe 50 companies that own shares in each other, and they keep an eye on each other. And here's how it work. If one of the 50 companies was badly managed and badly run, the remaining 49 companies would get together and replace the manager of the 50th company. Genius you say, right?

The Germans had a similar system of cross-holdings with the bank in the middle. So Douche would be the largest single stockholder than Daimler, Daimler would be the largest single stockholder than Alliance, and Alliance would be the largest single stockholder than Douche and they would all keep an eye on each other.

Notice what these two share in common, their leader's systems. Essentially, at the base of these systems is the assumption that shareholders don't know enough to make these big decisions, that it's better left to managers who might have gone to better schools, who are better educated, more informed than shareholders were. And let's face it, these leader's systems worked really well in Germany and Japan. They did not create the side course you saw on the U.S. from hostile acquisitions and companies coming apart. And in the mid-80s if you argue that the German and the Japanese system were better than the U.S. system, they've done it with a good basis.

## Why Leader Systems Fail at Systemic Problems

But here's what went wrong. You get to the 1990s, you saw the downside of both systems. Remember that Japanese example that I gave you of 50 companies bound together by cross-holdings, where one of the 50 companies is badly managed and badly run and the remaining 49 get together and replaced the manager of the 50th company? Well, let me change the facts a little bit. Let's assume that it's a one company doing stupid things and being badly managed, 49 of the 50 companies have made bad real estate loans. Ask yourself a question, will the one manager who did the right thing be able to replace the other 49, or will the other 49 close ranks and get rid of the one person who did the right thing? I think the latter is going to happen.

In fact, that's exactly what happened in the 1990s. Japanese companies faced with bad real estate loans rather, than face up to them hid from them. Twenty five years later you could argue that the Japanese economy is still paying a price for that blindness.

These systems, these alternative corporate governance systems, these top-down systems where experts or leaders decide what's good, have a significant problem. If there's a systemic problem, a problem that many companies face, these systems are not very good at dealing with systemic problems. Markets deal with systemic problems much better. So if you have to make a choice between a market-based system, a stockholder-based system and then the leader system, think about the trade-off, and that trade-off might lead you back to a market-based system.

## Alternative Objectives Are Intermediate Objectives

Now let's think about the other way you can deal with not liking maximizing stock prices as an objective. You can pick a different objective. Maximize revenues, maximize revenue growth, maximize market share. And if you have consultants, God help you, because consultants come with their acronyms. Maximize CFROI, maximize EBA.

What all of these objectives offer is, in my view, a false choice. They say, "Look, that maximizing stock prices is such a flawed difficult objective. Use this instead." I have no problem with using these objectives as long as you remember that they're intermediate objectives.

What do I mean by that? Maximizing market share by itself is a dumb objective, and here's why. You could get 100% market share of a market by selling below cost. But do you want that? You have 100% market share but you're going to be losing money forever. So when you think about maximizing market share, it's usually because you think you can use that market share to charge higher prices and make more profits in the future, in effect you're maximizing market share because eventually you think will lead to a higher value.

Intermediate objectives are okay but they take on a life of their own. So if your view is, "Let's replace maximizing stock prices with something simpler, revenue growth, earnings growth or market share," think about the consequences.

## Reading a Company's Objective Function

Now when you look at your company, it's nice to know what the objective is that drives the decision making. That's not always easy to find out, but read their annual reports, read what the managers in the company say about themselves. They will give you clues on where the focus is. If all they talk about is doubling revenues or tripling earnings, they're telling you what their objective function is. Whatever that objective is, think about all the bad stuff that can happen by focusing on that objective and keep track of what the company does, because odds are somewhere along the way, you're going to see the consequences play out.
