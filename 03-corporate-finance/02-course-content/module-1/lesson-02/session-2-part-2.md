# Session 2 Part 2 — Why the Utopian World Breaks Down: Weak Shareholder Control and Managerial Self-Interest

So now that we've set up the objective of maximizing stock prices, the narrow objective in the Utopian world, let's talk about what can go wrong. In fact, the real start is what cannot go wrong because everything that can go wrong will go wrong. So let's look at each of the linkages again, and let's look at the real world, not the Utopian world. Remember in the Utopian world, I assume that stockholders have complete power of managers. In the real world, that's not true. And here's why.

## The Annual Meeting Fails to Discipline Managers

The two mechanisms I describe for keeping managers in check — the annual meeting and the Board of Directors — are not very effective. Let's start with the annual meeting. At the annual meeting, it's difficult to create change often because most shareholders don't show up at annual meetings. In fact, if you're a shareholder in a publicly traded company, you get a proxy. It used to be in the mail, but perhaps online today, and that proxy allows you to vote at the meeting without actually being there. That said, most stockholders don't return their proxies. You're saying, so what? Well, the proxies that don't get returned, and this is true in much of the world, are counted as votes for incumbent management. Think of what I've just done. I've created a system tilted in favor of incumbent managers, because if you don't show up to vote, I counted as a vote for incumbent managers. That's why at annual meetings it's very difficult to get a vote against incumbent managers. A 35% no vote against managers at an annual meeting might be 75% of people actually voted. But because people don't bother to vote or turn in their proxies, incumbent managers start at an advantage.

## The Board of Directors Fails to Protect Shareholders: The 1997 Disney Case

You're saying, what about the board of directors? Well, what about it? In theory, the Board of Directors is there to protect shareholder interests. But if they do their job, take a look at the board of directors of any company and ask yourself a question: how do these people get on the board? Obviously, these are not names pulled out of a phone book. They're actually names that were picked by someone. Who's that someone? In many cases, that someone is the CEO, the top management of the company.

I talked about using real companies to illustrate much of what we're going to do in the class, and I'm going to get started. I'm going to take you back to 1997 and talk about one of the six companies that's part of my lab experiments, Disney. This is what the Board of Directors looked like for Disney in 1997. In my view, it's one of the worst boards created at a large publicly traded company, and let me back up that statement.

My first problem with this board is it has 17 members. You're saying so what? That's a big board. In fact, it's too big a board. Studies routinely indicate that once you get past nine or ten members on a board, it becomes counterproductive.

Second, at least in 1997, about eight of the 17 members on this board were insiders. They either work for Disney or used to work for Disney. You're saying so what? Remember, it's the job of the board to take a look at management and make sure that they're acting in the shareholders' best interest. And if half the board is constructed of people or part of the management, you're not going to get a very objective board.

The third problem with this board is the chairman of the board is a man called Michael Eisner. Well, Michael Eisner also happened to be the CEO of the board. Now, this makes no sense to me. Michael Eisner chairs the board that keeps an eye on him, and asks the question, is he doing a good job? How can you be objective?

And here's the final point. Even the outsiders on the board are conflicted. Reveta Bowers, the first name you see on the list, for instance, the principal of an elementary school in Los Angeles — that school happened to be the school that Michael Eisner's kids went to. That's a conflict of interest. In fact, every one of the non-insiders on this board has some connection, some conflict of interest that's going to get in the way of being objective.

## Managerial Self-Interest at Shareholder Expense

So if you go back again, you can see already that neither the annual meeting nor the Board of Directors does the job of keeping managers in line. So what happens? Given a choice between their interest and what's in the best interest of stockholders, managers often put their interest over stockholder interests. And here are a few examples.

Ever heard of Greenmail? This is what a target company in an acquisition, a hostile acquisition, does sometimes to get away from the acquisition. The managers of the target company collect cash, usually stockholder money, and use it to pay off the acquirer to make sure that the hostile acquisition doesn't go through. You're saying why? But remember the word hostile. A hostile acquisition is usually followed with managers losing their jobs. Managers are protecting their jobs using stockholder money — clear conflict of interest. Greenmail.

Here's a second example: a golden parachute. A golden parachute is a clause put into a compensation contract, where if a company is taken over, the top management who are going to be displaced are paid out 3 million, 5 million, or $15 million. Again, this is shareholder money being used to protect managers from the consequences of the acquisition. That's a golden parachute.

Then you have poison pills. Poison pills are clauses put into corporate charters that make hostile acquisitions more difficult. They raise the spectrum, but they have a common objective. It makes it much more difficult for somebody to acquire the firm, and because it makes it much more difficult, it actually reduces the likelihood that shareholders will get that premium. Again, it protects managers at the expense of shareholders.

In fact, you can extend the concept to what are called shark repellents, which are more extensive versions of changes to corporate charter. Here's one example. Normally, to take over a company, you need to buy 51% of the outstanding shares, but with a shark repellent, you could make that 80% of shares. Again, you're protecting managerial interests at the expense of shareholder interests.

In fact, when you look at acquisitions from the other side as the acquiring company, many acquisitions where companies overpay, you could argue that the real rationale, the motivation for doing the acquisition was because it served managerial interests.

## Every Utopian Assumption Breaks Down

So those are all ways in which managers put their interest over stockholder interests. So you can already see why the assumptions we made in the Utopian world break down. Every single assumption we made — that managers are controlled by shareholders, that lenders are protected, that markets are efficient, and are no social costs — is a flawed assumption. That said, though, we have to live in the world we're in, not the world we wish we were in. So we'll talk about ways of dealing with companies in the real world.
