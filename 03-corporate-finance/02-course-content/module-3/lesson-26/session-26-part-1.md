# Session 26 Part 1 — Assessing Dividend Policy and Free Cash Flow to Equity

In these last few sessions, we've looked at how companies collectively set dividends. We've looked at how to measure dividends, the payout ratio or a dividend yield, and we've also looked at three schools of thought on dividends that rains a spectrum from dividends are good, to dividends are bad, to dividends don't matter. So let's now talk about ways of assessing how much a company can actually return to its stockholders that is more concrete.

## The Trust Framework and Three-Step Process

The word that comes to mind when I think about dividends is trust. Let me explain. When a company has cash flows it can afford to pay out in dividends and it does not, it's holding your cash. You being the stockholder, it's holding your cash for you, right? If you trust managers with that cash, then you're going to give them a lot of slack when it comes to dividends.

So I'm going to start with three-step process for assessing dividend policy. The first step in this process, I'm going to ask the question, how much could your company have returned to its stockholders during the last year, the last 5 years, the last 10 years? The second question I'm going to ask is, how much did it actually return? And we're going to find out very quickly that most companies don't return what they can afford to. Some return more, most return less. And the third question I'm going to ask is, if your company has held back cash and hasn't returned what it can do, do you trust the managers of this company with your cash? And to address that question of trust, I'm going to look at your history. How well have you picked projects? How well have you done for me in terms of stock appreciation and returns?

## How Much Companies Actually Return: Lab Company Examples

So let's get this process started. Before we look at how much a company can afford to return, let's take the easier question, the second question first. Let's look at how much companies actually return.

I took my companies, the further companies, that publicly-traded companies that are part of my lab experiments, and I looked at how much each of these companies has returned over the last five years in the form of dividends and in the form of buybacks. If you take a look at Disney, the bulk of the cash the company has returned over the last five years has taken the form of buybacks. With Vale, a segment of the cash return has taken the form of buybacks. With Tata Motors and Deutsche Bank, all of the cash return took the form of dividends. And of course, Baidu has returned no cash at all. So you've five companies, five very different ways of returning cash to stockholders, and we'll come back and address why those differences might exist.

## Free Cash Flow to Equity: The Potential Dividend

Now, let's look at the first question, the more critical question. How much could these companies have returned over this period? To answer this question, I'm going to come up with a number that I call the free cash flow to equity. Sounds fancy, right? But think of it as potential dividend, and here's how I'm going to estimate it.

This is the cash left over after you've met every conceivable need. And to compute this number, I'm going to start with your net income. Why your net income? Because that's what the accountants measure as the income to equity investors. So if you're the equity investor in a company, the accountant tells you that you've made your net income. Of course, it's accounting earnings, so I'm going to add back the non-cash expenses I have, depreciation and amortization, and I'm going to come up with the cash flow that equity investors have.

From that cash flow, I've got to cover every conceivable need. And here are some of my needs. If I have capital expenditures: land, building, factories, that I've got to invest in, I've got to pay for them. If my working capital increases, inventory or accounts receivable increases, I've got to set aside cash to cover that. If I have debt payments to make, I've got to set aside cash to cover that.

After I've met all these needs, there is a way I might be able to get a cash flow from outside, and that is by borrowing money. Now, this might sound strange, but when I borrow money, cash comes into the company. It actually increases cash available to pay out as potential dividends.

So in some, here's what I have. I have net income plus depreciation minus all of the investments I've got to make to create future growth minus debt payments plus new debt issuances. What I'm left with at the end is my free cash flow to equity.

Now, think about it. If you start with a positive net income, could you end up with a negative free cash flow to equity? Absolutely. If you're a growth company with huge capital expenditures and you don't borrow much money, you can end up with a negative free cash flow to equity and dividend policy just got really simple. If you have a negative free cash flow to equity, guess how much you can pay in dividends. The answer is nothing. If you're already in a hole, you need to stop digging. If you have positive free cash flow to equity, it's cash that you could pay out in dividends, but you're not contractually required to. Managers can choose to pay out all of that cash as dividends. They can pay out less or they can pay out more, and that becomes the basis for assessing dividend policy.

## Disney Case Study

You ready? Let's try this on Disney. I went back and I collected the numbers I need to compute free cash flow to equity for the last five years. I started with net income, I added back depreciation and amortization, I subtracted out capital expenditures, I subtracted out change in working capital.

You think, "Where would I find these numbers?" The place you will find them is in the statement of cash flows. It'll be on different sections of that statement, but you can find them all there. So net income plus depreciation minus CapEx minus change in working capital.

I'm going to compute an intermediate free cash flow to equity, I'm calling it intermediate because this is the free cash flow to equity before the cash inflows and outflows from repaying debt and issuing new debt. My free cash flow to equity, if I don't count the debt cash flows for Disney over this five-year period, would have been just over 17 billion. Disney actually paid out slightly more than that in dividends and buybacks. It returned about $19.7 billion over the 5 years, but here's how it funded the rest. Over this period, if you look at how much Disney borrowed and how much it repaid, it added about $9.8 billion of debt over this period. There comes the extra 2.5 billion that you needed to cover your dividends and your buybacks.

So let's summarize. If I look at the free cash flow to equity from just operations, before debt issuances and repayments, Disney has about 17 billion. Disney returned more than that, 19.9 billion as dividends and buybacks, but it covered much of that requirement or all of that by borrowing additional money. So at this stage, if you ask me to summarize what Disney is doing, it is paying more than its free cash flow to equity from operations and increasing its debt to cover the difference.

## The Shortcut: Equity Portion of Reinvestment

Now, to give you a sense of how to compute that free cash flow to equity quickly, let's take an example, an extreme example, where computing the free cash flow to equity becomes simple. If you're willing to assume that a portion of your reinvestment, the capital expenditures and working capital that you have to cover, a fixed portion always comes from debt. You can actually simplify the free cash flow to equity calculation, and here's how you do it. You'd start with the net income first, but instead of subtracting out what you need to reinvest fully, you'll subtract out only the equity portion of that reinvestment.

So let me make this more explicit. When I look at reinvestment in a company, I break it out into two components. One component, I call net CapEx. It's a difference between capital expenditures and depreciation, and think of that as what you're investing to increase your long-term assets. The other is a change in working capital. When I'm using the shortcut, I start with the net income and I subtract from it only the equity portion of my net CapEx and only the equity portion of my change in working capital.

So let me use some numbers to illustrate this process. Let's assume I have a company with a $100 million in net income and $60 million in reinvestment, reinvestment composed of net CapEx and change in working capital. Let's assume that 40% of that reinvestment is going to come from debt, and you feel pretty secure in that assumption. To get the free cash flow to equity, I will start with the net income of a 100 and subtract out only the equity portion of the 60 million which is 60% of 60 million which is 36 million. I get a free cash flow to equity of 64 million.

The shortcut works only if you are willing to assume that a constant proportion of reinvestment is always funded with that. In fact, this calculation of free cash flow to equity will converge on the longer calculation if you work through the numbers.

So one of the key numbers to compute if you're trying to assess whether your company is paying out more or less than it should is what it can pay out, the potential dividend. Computing it is not difficult to do if you're willing to dive into the numbers. You need to go to the statement of cash flows, if you can find it, compute what the company's net income and reinvestment are, and if you have debt cash inflows and debt cash outflows from repayments and issuances, get those as well. That'll give you a measure of what your company could have paid out in dividends last year, the last five years, or the last decade.
