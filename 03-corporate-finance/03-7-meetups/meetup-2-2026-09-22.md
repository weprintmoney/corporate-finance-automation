## Building a cost of capital

**Damodaran:** To get a hurdle rate, let's go back to business. A business can raise capital two ways: equity capital and debt capital. Debt capital is just borrowed money, and for most businesses it takes the form of bank borrowing. What would the rate be? The rule in corporate finance is that it's not the rate at which you borrowed in the past, so don't look at your books. It's not the lowest rate you can borrow at today, because that can be a short-term rate. It's the rate at which you can borrow money today, long term. I use 10 years for pragmatic reasons, because my risk-free rates are often 10-year rates. Nothing stops you from using 20 or 30 if you're an infrastructure company.

To get a cost of debt, I start with the risk-free rate in that currency. That's why we spent time on risk-free rates. It's the base you build on. On top of that you add a default spread, a credit spread, because lenders worry about being paid back, and the more they worry, the more they charge. Banks have had to decide how much to charge for hundreds of years. Your history matters, your capacity to pay matters. If your company is rated, use the rating as a shortcut, with the consequence that if the ratings agency is wrong, your cost of debt will be wrong. So the pre-tax cost of debt is the risk-free rate plus a default spread. Then there's a quirk in the tax law in much of the world: if you borrow money, interest expenses are tax deductible, but if you raise equity you don't get an equivalent advantage. So the tax benefit lowers your cost of borrowing. That's the cost of debt, the easier half of the equation.

Now the cost of equity. Start with the same risk-free rate. Then look at what equities collectively are priced at in the market, because you want the price of risk in the market. That's what an equity risk premium is. If I ask you what the equity risk premium is for US stocks, you're tempted to look backwards. Historically stocks have made 5.3% more than bonds, so use that. But the problem with looking backwards is that you're assuming the past will look like the future, and that's a dangerous assumption in 2026. The alternative I offer is to let the market tell you. The implied equity risk premium is my attempt to back out the price of risk in the equity market. It's 4.5%, or whatever it works out to right now. I'm not saying it's right or wrong. It's the price of risk. I'm a price taker when I invest in equity. That's the price of risk for an average-risk stock. What if I have a riskier stock?

The metric that captures that is not your risk-free rate or your risk premium. It's your beta. Betas are relative risk measures, and that's the best way to read them. Risk-free rate, beta, and risk premium together give you a cost of equity. A twist on the equity risk premium: if I'm a US company getting 80% of my revenues in Ukraine, I have to bring that into the equity risk premium. If you're a Ukrainian company getting 80% of your revenues from Germany, I have to bring that in as well. Equity risk premiums reflect where you do business, not where you're incorporated.

Keep it simple. You have the cost of debt, the cost of equity, and weights on the two.

## Why project cost of capital uses company weights

**Damodaran:** Those weights, especially if you're looking at a project's cost of capital, should not be based on how you fund the project. Why, when you look at the hurdle rate for a project, should you not look at the mix of debt and equity used on that project?

**Valentyn:** We have to assess the project separately and its risk.

**Damodaran:** Wouldn't it be consistent, then, to look at its debt and equity? Think about it. Can I fund a project entirely with debt if I'm an ongoing company? If I stick with project-specific weights, its cost of capital becomes the after-tax cost of debt, a really low number, because I funded it entirely with debt. But if I keep funding every project with debt, I go bankrupt. So if I fund one project disproportionately with debt, what has to be true for the next project? I'll fund it disproportionately with equity. If I let project weights drive the cost of capital, it swings depending on how each project is funded, and that isn't fair to the projects funded with equity.

The exception is a very big standalone project, such as in an infrastructure company.

**Valentyn:** A special purpose vehicle.

**Damodaran:** A special purpose vehicle that borrows money on its own. That's the exception. In most cases we use company weights for individual projects.

## Investment returns and why companies take bad investments

**Damodaran:** All of this gets the hurdle rate for the project, the benchmark I need to beat. The other half of investment analysis is measuring the returns I will make. Going through the rules of corporate finance, what am I looking at to measure project returns? Cash flows as opposed to earnings.

**Valentyn:** Why?

**Damodaran:** Because cash talks and earnings just walks. You can make earnings do all kinds of gymnastics. Does timing matter? Obviously I'd rather get cash flows earlier. That's what discounting does. And everything I say about this project should be in the cash flows. No side stuff, no garnishing allowed. The return should exceed the hurdle rate.

That's where you are fundamentally in this class. And I'll be quite honest: this is at the heart of what makes companies great, taking great investments. You may ask, what about financing and dividends? No company ever became great by optimizing its debt mix or finessing its dividend policy. You need to start with great investments, because everything else becomes a side story.

We know how to do this, but we also know a lot of companies take bad investments. By my judgment, 60% of the projects that are taken have negative net present value: they earn returns less than the hurdle rate. And this is not after the fact. You can't blame luck, because it can't be 60%. These are things we know going in. So how do companies justify taking bad investments?

**Valentyn:** Because of the big potential, or something.

**Damodaran:** The potential? Why isn't it in the cash flows? In what form is it potential?

**Valentyn:** Or synergy.

**Damodaran:** Synergy is a cash flow as well. But I think you're onto something. In most businesses, people decide first and do the analysis afterwards. That's not the way it's supposed to be. **Damodaran:** Lots of potential. AI. And you hope it turns the decision. That's not a frailty. It's human nature, and because it's human nature, you have to design systems that counter it.

It will be interesting to see how much human inertia plays a role in the shift.

You did well as an AI company, but what happens to the economy? What happens to your own revenues when you can't sell to anybody because nobody has income? People are in silos. ## Who captures the value: the AI "factory"

**Valentyn:** There's a thought from Alex Karp, the Palantir CEO.

**Damodaran:** He's clearly on the spectrum, because you watch him and say, oh my God, you must have been one heck of a disruptive kid. My wife teaches fifth grade, and she saw him and said, I have kids like that in my class. They can't sit still, they squat on stools. But what did he say?

**Valentyn:** He's a punk, and punks always do disruptions. He thinks it won't be the AI model companies that harvest the profit in the end, because the price per token, even for the most capable models, will decrease over time. The companies that will harvest the profits are the ones with the huge data centers, and companies like Palantir that use these models in specific ways for society, businesses, and the economy.

**Damodaran:** There are core questions. First, will there be value creation or value transfer? The optimists talk about a more productive, wealthier world, with GDP going up. I'm skeptical, because over the last 40 years, with all the tech disruption, real GDP has grown more slowly than in the previous 40 years. It's not the top line that shifts. It's where the wealth goes. So it's more about wealth transfer, and the question is who collects the spoils.

I don't think it will be the infrastructure companies, because that's not what history tells us. Take the internet. Who built the infrastructure? The telecom companies, Cisco with its routing equipment, AOL with its early access. But the biggest winner from the dot-com boom and bust was none of those. It was Amazon. The PC disruption: name me one successful PC company that's still around. They all soared, then collapsed. Dell is still around, but it's not a PC company anymore. The history of disruption is that the people who build the factory can't be the ones who collect the value from the factory. Ultimately it's the people who use the factory to make products and services. The question is who those people will be. Right now the LLM companies are first in line, but there will be others who use the AI factory to produce products and services.

The way I describe it, and I'll say it again: we've built the most expensive factory in history, at $3 trillion, and adding about $1 trillion every year. We have no idea what the factory will make, whether people want it, or how much they'll pay. It's an act of faith.

**Valentyn:** We have a first signal on that. I believe Perplexity and Nvidia now want to introduce a laptop with a basic AI model embedded in the chip, doing the LLM processing locally. For this basic model you don't need any subscription or even an internet connection.

**Damodaran:** It will be interesting to see, because if you can do that, it becomes a commoditized, almost free version of AI. That could kill the business.

**Charlcye:** We have that, but it doesn't kill the business, because the powerful inference you get from Claude or ChatGPT is basically a mega market.

**Damodaran:** Yes, there's a premium AI market, which is what Anthropic aspires to be in: charge huge amounts. And then there's mass-market AI. Look at this stupid AI thing on my Zoom. I've never clicked it. Everything has an AI button, and I have no idea what it does. A lot of AI is going to be essentially free or close to free. If those are the products and services that come out of this factory, we're screwed, because you can't get the $3 trillion back. It has to be premium products at high prices.

That's why, in my post on AI, I reverse-engineered what revenues would need to be to justify $3 trillion. I don't know enough about AI, but I can tell you what the revenues would need to be, and you tell me whether there are enough revenues in the universe. You very quickly get to $7 or $8 trillion in revenue. Collectively, all the operating expenses of every company in the world last year were $62 trillion. That's why, when I listen to Cathie Wood talking about SpaceX last week, saying the space launch business is a $10 trillion potential market, I think: who's launching satellites into space? Every one of us would have to go into our backyards and launch one. It's a completely absurd number. These experts need perspective, because they're talking from inside the business looking outside, and everything looks like potential.

Ultimately these premium products become things companies have to pay for, and the money has to come from revenues. If you're adding to expenses, what's going away? It can't be on top of existing expenses. That's why the discussion can't be put off, because it's very much a part of whether you're willing to pay [ed. — figure garbled in source, "too trillion"; possibly "$2 trillion" for Anthropic] and what these expenses displace and whether that's feasible.

**Charlcye:** I always hear it referred to as building the railroads: we know it's going to go bust, but it will live on.

**Damodaran:** The difference is that the railroads had tangible output. You were opening up the West. You were making the country ten times bigger and putting the US on the pathway to becoming the wealthiest nation in the world. And they came with a moat that was almost impossible to overcome. It wasn't like everybody said "I'll start a railroad too," because it took years of real estate investment and infrastructure. For anybody going west, unless you wanted a horse-drawn carriage, it was the railroad or nothing. There were no flights. Eventually railroads got disrupted, but for a time they were the only game in town. People argue data centers are like railroads. I don't see it, because I don't see what the West is. Where are you going with these data centers? What's the end product? Right now much of the revenue in the space is companies renting data centers to each other.

Almost all of xAI's revenues come from renting out data centers to Anthropic and to Alphabet. That's not revenue from AI. That's companies playing internal games and making it look like they're making money. The money has to come from frontier models and from products like Perplexity or any other company selling subscriptions and usage, because that's what pays for the factory. If you're Nvidia, you want people to keep building the factory, making it bigger and bigger. So there's an internal tension between what the architecture companies want and what the product companies want.

**Charlcye:** It feels like we thought the big foundation model companies would run out of money, but they found ways to do these deals with each other, and now they're IPOing.

**Damodaran:** And Nvidia will fund them, because it's in their interest to keep the status quo going and keep the factory getting bigger. They're the one company that collects money right away. They're not doing it on future promise and potential. They're selling you the stuff to build the factory. So the internal tensions between the AI players are going to rise to the surface, because you'll see conflicting interests play out in how big the factory gets. I'll hold on to the factory analogy because it illustrates why you have this internal financing and circular business. You have to keep the factory illusion going if you want to keep making the factory bigger.
