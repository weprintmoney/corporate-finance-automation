## The three corporate finance decisions

**Damodaran:** Hey, welcome, folks. We have three basic decisions. So as I go through this, tell me whether you can think of exceptions. Every business — that hot dog stand you see on the street, all the way to the largest company in the world.

The first is the investment decision. What's the investment decision? Every business has scarce resources. It has to decide what to invest those resources in, what projects to take, what acquisitions to make. If you're the hot dog stand, you know what your big investment decision might be? Whether you should add sausages to the menu or not. You think that's trivial? For that guy, it's a big investment decision. Or whether to get a new hot dog cart — that's a huge investment decision.

So the investment decision is about what kind of investments to make. And what's the core principle that governs that decision? It has to pass a minimum. You have to make sure the investments you take earn a return that is greater than some minimum acceptable hurdle rate.

You'll say, that's a buzzword. What does that even mean? The minimum acceptable hurdle rate should reflect the risk of the investment you're taking. Riskier investments should have higher hurdle rates than safer ones.

## Debt vs. equity: why equity costs more

**Damodaran:** But let's say that measurement problem is solved and we come back with 12%. The hurdle rate should reflect the risk. It should also reflect where you get your money.

There are only two ways any business can fund itself. It can use equity, and it can use debt. What's the defining difference between debt and equity? What makes debt different from equity?

**Charlcye:** Equity is your own money.

**Damodaran:** Equity is what?

**Charlcye:** Your own money.

**Damodaran:** It could be your own money. But can you lend yourself your own money? I mean, you can — as a business, you can lend yourself money.

The difference is the type of claim you get. When you borrow money, what do you expose yourself to? A contractual payment you have to make. It's called interest, but you can call it a lease payment. Any contractual payment is debt. With equity, you get whatever's left over.

So already you can lay the foundations. Which is going to be more expensive to you?

**Charlcye:** Equity.

**Damodaran:** Equity — because they get the residual. They're the last guys in line. This is common sense. Ninety-five percent of corporate finance is common sense. When in doubt, go back and ask yourself that question: if I'm the last person in line, am I going to settle for a lower return than the guy in front of me? The lender charges 11%; I'm going to charge 13% to 15%.

So your hurdle rate is going to reflect not just the business and the geography, but where you raise your money. You come up with a hurdle rate: 12%.

Now, which one is costly? Is debt costly, or is equity costly, for the business? Let's play it out. Debt and equity are not abstractions. Somebody has to supply the debt. Somebody has to supply the equity. So if I come to you and say, lend me money, and you have a contractual claim on me and you charge me 10% for that contractual claim — would you charge more if you had an equity claim instead?

**Student:** I would charge more.

**Damodaran:** There you go. That's it. The rest is just mechanics — we have to figure out how much more. Why do you have to charge more? Because you don't have a contractual claim. You have to wait until everybody else is paid before you get your claim.

## How markets clear a collective cost of equity

**Student:** The reason I'm saying that is — if I buy a stock, let's say I buy a single stock in Amazon, my expectation would be that my stock doubles or triples in one or two years. That is much—

**Damodaran:** If that's your expectation, don't buy Amazon.

**Student:** No, I'm just giving an example.

**Damodaran:** No, no — but there's got to be… You can have an expectation, but there's also a market expectation.

**Student:** Correct.

**Damodaran:** Amazon raises money from millions of investors like you. That collective expectation becomes the cost of equity. And that cost of equity will still be higher than what Amazon will be charged if it goes to a bank and borrows money today.

When you're a publicly traded company, you're raising money from millions of shareholders. It's not one person's requirement that defines your cost of equity — it's a collective requirement. And if you want a 100% cost of equity, you know what's going to happen? You will never buy Amazon shares, because they will never measure up.

So what will happen in markets is that people who demand really high returns will drop out. There's a selection bias. People buy shares because they think they can make their required return — almost by process of elimination. That's what public markets do.

So if somebody comes in and says, I want to make a 50% return on shares, I say: okay, go ahead, you're welcome to try. But every company you look at, you're going to say, I can't make 50% there. I can't make 50% there. I can't make 50% there. And eventually what are you going to end up doing? Holding on to cash, saying, I wish I'd done that. I wish I'd done that.

So markets have a way of clearing up expectations and ending up with a group of shareholders who collectively say, this is what I need to make. One of our challenges in corporate finance is: how do you come up with that collective judgment, when there are tens of thousands of people with very different expectations?

## The investment principle in practice

**Damodaran:** So you come up with a cost of debt and a cost of equity. As you said, we'll spend four weeks on that assessment. We're going to come up with the hurdle rate. And then we have a very simple principle: if your hurdle rate is 11%, for God's sake, make sure your investments make more than 11%.

Does that surprise you? It's common sense. It costs you 11% to raise money — don't go out and invest at less than that. And you know how much pushback I get from businesses on this? I'm going to show you evidence that 60% to 65% of businesses take investments that earn less than their hurdle rate.

We can talk about why. Maybe it's the capacity of human beings to delude themselves — hope, greed, overconfidence. But the core principle doesn't change. You need to make a return above your hurdle rate. That's your investment principle.

## The financing principle

**Damodaran:** The financing principle is about the mix of debt and equity. We talked about equity having a cost and debt having a cost. The financing principle says: find the right mix of debt and equity for you — not for everybody, but for you — that will minimize your hurdle rate. It's better to have a lower hurdle rate than a higher one. So we're going to spend time on that financing principle, on the mix of debt and equity.

## The dividend principle: harvesting the investment

**Damodaran:** And then there's a third principle. Why do farmers plant the farmland? What's the end game? Is it because they enjoy watching the crops grow? Yes, farmers do enjoy seeing those sprouts, and they enjoy seeing the fields of corn. But what's the end game? They want to harvest the crop.

Why do we invest in businesses? Oh, we love seeing businesses grow, and customers happy — creating all of that is nice. But the end game is that we need to be able to harvest our investments.

In the context of a private business, you take cash out of the business to do what? Buy a house, send your kids to college. In the context of a publicly traded company, it takes the form of cash returned to the shareholders.

There used to be only one way in which a public company could return cash, which was dividends. Until the 1980s, across the world, dividends were the only way companies did it. In the last 40 years — and it started in the US, but it's now spread across the world — a second way has opened up, which is buybacks. Either way, you give cash back to shareholders.

## Dividends vs. buybacks and the tax wedge

**Damodaran:** The only difference between dividends and buybacks is that with dividends, everybody gets a dividend, whether you want it or not. You say, why would I not want a dividend? Why might an investor not particularly want a dividend?

**Student:** Investment opportunities.

**Damodaran:** What is it? It means the company is mature. That's fine. But that's the end game — you can't fight it. That's like me complaining about being old. If you're a mature company, you're a mature company. So the fact that they pay dividends didn't make them old; the fact that they were old made them pay dividends. That is a reality.

We *want* our businesses to grow old. Because what's the alternative? A lot of companies don't make it. They never make it. So that doesn't bother me.

But when you receive a dividend — and I'm sorry, I'm going to get your name wrong over and over again — you're in Texas, so I can relate to you, because I file US taxes. When you receive dividends, what do you have to do in the year you receive them? This might not be true everywhere in the world, but in much of the world it is.

You get that statement from your broker. It says: look, we've already told the IRS, the tax guy, that you received the dividend. So you can't hide it. You'd better report it and pay taxes on it.

Now do you see why some investors might say, I'd prefer not to get dividends? Because when you get dividends, you get taxed. If you didn't need the cash anyway — you're saving this money for your pension — you're saying, why would I want to pay taxes on this?

So already you can see that one of the things that's going to drive whether you pay dividends or buy back stock is who your shareholders are. If your shareholders are wealthier, you might want to buy back stock rather than pay dividends, because that way you're not creating a tax burden. You don't have to pay taxes on a buyback — only if you sell your shares back. And the taxes you pay then are capital gains taxes, which tend to be lower in much of the world. And there are parts of the world where there is zero capital gains tax.

Investment principle, financing principle, dividend principle. Every business faces those choices. Corporate finance is about coming up with the mechanics and the measures to deliver on those principles.

## The end game: from profits to cash flows to value

**Damodaran:** But as you make these decisions, you need an end game. You have to tell me why you run a business. What's the end game here? That's what the first three to four sessions look at.

What is the end game for a business? Why do we start businesses? What's the end game?

**Student:** Profits.

**Student:** Value.

**Damodaran:** I'm sorry — somebody said "take projects." No, no, that's not the end game for a business, because I could take projects and go bankrupt. I can take projects and wipe out money. Did you say profits or projects? Okay. That's a good start.

But if I make that my end game — profits — I'm going to follow up. So whoever said profits, stay on. Was that you? No. Who said profits? Ashish. I'm going to put you on the spot.

Is it profits next year? Profits in the long term? Profits over the next ten years? You see already. If I say profits next year, would I run my business very differently than if I say profits over the next ten years? Yes. That's the ultimate short-term business. I'm going to maximize profits next year even if it means burning my clients, destroying my customers, because I don't care about repeat sales.

So if I want to run a business as a going concern, it can't be profits in the near term. It's got to be profits in the long term. But I have a problem. Who measures profits?

**Student:** Accountants do?

**Damodaran:** You think so? Can you have a business that shows big profits but has negative cash flows?

**Student:** Of course.

**Damodaran:** And which one do you get to spend — cash or profits? I tell people to go to Starbucks, order a venti cappuccino, and offer to pay the cashier with earnings. "Look, I have a good job, I have great earnings." Unless you have a really stupid cashier, he's going to say, I don't care, I need cash or a credit card.

**Student:** But I could securitize. We can use securitization.

**Damodaran:** You can try. And it works only if the earnings can be securitized — and usually earnings are not securitized. You know what gets securitized? Your receivables. Things that the person securitizing can convert to cash.

So, Ashish, I'm going to take your profits and replace it with cash flows, because cash is cash in hand. And I'm going to replace cash flows next year with cash flows over the long term.

You see the problem: how the heck am I going to compare cash flows in year one to cash flows in year ten? So time value comes in. That present value function — time value — is one of those core, very simplistic, mechanical tools in finance that gets used over and over again.

What's time value? A cash flow in year ten is not the same as cash in my pocket today. To make them comparable, what do I have to do? I have to bring the cash flow in year ten back to today by discounting it. At what rate?

**Charlcye:** At the discount rate.

**Damodaran:** Remember that hurdle rate we talked about? That's why we care about what your hurdle rate is, because that now becomes the discount rate.

And you know what happens when I discount my cash flows over time at the hurdle rate? What I get is a present value. In my valuation class, this is the value of the business.

The end game for a business is to maximize the value of that business. So when people say that's short-term, they're missing the point. Because what is the value of the business? Not profits next year. It's cash flows across time. I am perfectly okay with Anthropic having negative cash flows for the next four years, or the next five years, as long as they do what? Deliver really huge positive cash flows in the future that make up for the negative cash flows.

Value is completely agnostic about time horizon. You can be a really long-time-horizon firm or a really short-time-horizon firm. As long as you're maximizing value, that is the end game.

## Why stock price is a flawed shortcut — the LBO aside

**Damodaran:** In practice, though, people replace that with maximizing stock prices for a publicly traded company. Why is that a shortcut?

Because first, they're making an assumption that the market price is a good estimate of value. That's true only if markets are efficient and investors are rational — then of course a market price can become your standard for value.

It's also true only if you can't rip off your lenders. Because if I steal from my banker and make my equity investors more wealthy, I'm not building a better business. I'm just making my shareholders richer.

That's why I laugh when people who do LBOs talk about the value they create. You know where the bulk of the value created comes from — the value they then leverage the outcomes from?

**Charlcye:** Debt.

**Damodaran:** Debt. It comes from ripping off your lenders, or taking advantage of tax laws that give debt an advantage. It doesn't come from making the business more valuable. I'm not saying they're bad people, but stop talking and acting like you're making the economy bigger or companies more valuable. You're just moving wealth around.

## Why the end game matters: Japan and market share

**Damodaran:** But I stay on that objective function. In most corporate finance classes — the one I took when I was an MBA, and this is still true of many corporate finance classes — the objective is stated in the first ten minutes of the class and then you move on to the mechanics.

I think that's a huge mistake, and here's why. When people have disagreements about how businesses should make decisions, it's not because they don't understand net present value or what we do in corporate finance. It's because they disagree about what the end game for a business should be.

**Charlcye:** Sure.

**Damodaran:** I'll give you an example. Through much of the 1970s and 1980s, Japanese companies were among the most successful companies in the world. They grew, and Japan became a global economic power. You know what end game the Japanese companies defined for themselves? It wasn't to maximize value. It was to maximize revenues, to maximize market share.

And there are companies out there that state their objective is: look, we want to grow revenues, that's our end game. We want to become the biggest company in the sector, that's our end game. And if you make that your end game, all your decisions are going to be tailored to that end game. Pick a different end game and all of corporate finance gets rewritten.

## Case study: Petrobras — every principle inverted

**Damodaran:** If you get a chance, go on Google and type in my name and "Petrobras blog." This is from about 13 years ago. It remains one of my most-read blog posts of all time.

You familiar with Petrobras?

**Student:** Yes — the Brazilian oil company.

**Damodaran:** For a brief period it was the most valuable company in all of Latin America. It grew tremendously between 2004 and 2011, it soared, and it reached a market cap of 200 billion US dollars — by far the most valuable company in Latin America. And over the next three years its market cap went from 200 billion to about 30 billion. In case you're wondering, that's the wrong direction.

So what happened? I took a look at Petrobras, and here's what it looked like they were doing.

They were exploring for more reserves, investing over and over in investments that earned less than their cost of capital. They were taking projects that earned less than the hurdle rate. Second, they were finding mixes of debt and equity that were frankly insane — that were pushing up the hurdle rate. And third, while they were investing huge amounts and having negative cash flows, they were paying out huge amounts in dividends.

It's almost like they took the corporate finance rulebook and inverted every single principle. In other words, they said: we should invest in projects that earn less than the hurdle rate, we should find a mix of debt and equity that maximizes our hurdle rate, and we should pay dividends even though we can't afford to.

You say, why would you run a business as if you're insane?

**Student:** Political pressure?

**Damodaran:** Okay, let's back up. First, Petrobras is a publicly traded company. But it used to be a Brazilian government-owned company until about 25 years ago. It was privatized, taken public — but while it was taken public, like almost every Brazilian company of that generation it had two classes of shares: common shares and preferred shares.

Now, if you just trusted the English language, which of these two classes would you want to hold? Preferred, right? That's the better word. But in this case it's actually very Orwellian. The common shares had the voting rights to run the company. The preferred shares — you received a dividend, but you had basically no voting rights.

The common shares were held by six entities, three of which were government entities, and the board of directors for Petrobras was entirely appointed in Brasília by the Brazilian government. So you're the CEO of Petrobras — who do you answer to? Not the shareholders, not even the board, but the minister of petroleum, or whatever the ministry is, in Brasília.

And here's what that minister said — and they were public about this. They said: we want you to make Petrobras the largest oil company in the world.

So you're the CEO, you get the message. What does the largest oil company in the world have to do? Produce the most oil. How do you produce the most oil? You go look for oil all over the place, even if it's not economic — even if it costs you $80 a barrel, a hundred, you're going to get the oil out of the ground because you want to be the biggest. There you get the investment decision explained.

Now, normally to fund this you'd have to raise debt or equity. But the problem is the Brazilian government didn't want you to go out and issue more shares. They said no, no, you can't issue equity. So what did they have to do? They went out and borrowed money.

And here's the other component. Because those common shareholders — especially the government — wanted to maintain control, when they issued new shares, what kind of shares did they have to be? Those preferred shares, on which you paid a dividend without voting rights. So they were locked into an insane dividend policy.

Pick a different end game and you're going to run a business very differently.

## Why shareholders get priority — the contract argument

**Damodaran:** So here's what I do. I stay with that end game and I say, okay, give me your alternative. Because people don't like maximizing value. They say that's so narrow-minded. Why do you just focus on shareholder wealth?

Are there multiple stakeholders in businesses? Let's go down the list. You've got shareholders, you've got lenders. Let's keep going. Are employees stakeholders?

**Student:** Yeah.

**Damodaran:** Are customers stakeholders?

**Student:** Yeah.

**Damodaran:** Are suppliers stakeholders?

**Student:** Yeah.

**Damodaran:** What about society — do you think society has a stake in a business? Absolutely. So you've got multiple stakeholders. Why do we put shareholders at the top of the heap and focus on their interest? That's the biggest pushback I get.

And why do we give them the power to appoint the board? Because that's what we do — we give shareholders the power to pick the board and ultimately the management. Why do we give so much power to shareholders?

**Charlcye:** Oh — the manager…

**Student:** Because they are the owners, and they have taken the risk.

**Damodaran:** That's a very dangerous road we're traveling. Don't lenders take risk when they lend to you? Do employees take risk when they work for you? I would argue employees are more exposed, because their entire human capital is tied up in it.

So I don't want to open that door, because that would mean whoever is most exposed to risk should be the one running the business — which might shift depending on who we are as a business.

**Charlcye:** I think it's fair, price…

**Student:** But it's essential to capitalism.

**Damodaran:** No, that's not an excuse either, because then I'm going to say capitalism stinks, let's go to socialism. I'm opening a different door there. You can't use that.

I'm going to give you a clue. Think of every other claimholder in this process, and let's think about how the relationship works.

Lenders lend money, but they do it on a contract. What does the contract require? Interest gets paid. There are covenants on what you can or cannot do. They get a claim to sell your assets. So they're contractually protected.

Do employees have contracts with the business? Yeah. We can argue about how strong or weak the contracts are — maybe unions get created to make the contract stronger — but employees have a contract. Unless you're slave labor, which is a different thing; let's not open that door. Employees have a contract.

Do customers have a contract with a business? When you sell a product it comes with one. And again it varies across countries, but if I get a product that is faulty, I can sue the company. There's a contract.

Does society have a contract? Yeah — they have the government, which establishes the laws, the rules. They have laws, regulations, taxes, a whole set of contracts. Suppliers have one.

You see where I'm going. The only claimholder who does not have a contractual claim is shareholders. If I don't give them the capacity to run the company, who will ever want to be a shareholder? Because you're going to say: I get whatever is left over, but I don't get to run the firm. And guess what's going to be left over for you if you don't run the firm?

If every other stakeholder gets a piece of the pie, it'll be like being the twelfth person at a family Thanksgiving and you get the last slice of the pie. Trust me, there is no pie left over for you, because somebody piggish is going to take a really big slice and you're going to have nothing left.

We give shareholders priority not because they're special, but because if we didn't, nobody would want to risk their equity and start businesses. So this is the defense. If you don't like it, then basically throw the system out — you cannot have owner-run businesses. And that's a different debate. We've had that since Karl Marx came out. Maybe we do it differently. But that's a different argument. Once you've decided you want owner-run businesses, this is part of what you need for owner-run businesses to succeed.

## Externalities and the limits of shareholder wealth maximization

**Damodaran:** Does it come with side costs? Absolutely. In the process of maximizing shareholder wealth, can I do damage to other claimholders? Yeah, I can.

I can rip off lenders if they leave loopholes in the contract. I can take advantage of employees — maybe pay them below minimum wage and get away with it if the government is not enforcing it. I can make them work 18 hours a day. In fact, factories used to make workers work 16 hours a day, and use child labor, until laws got passed. I can sell substandard products to customers, or products that actually create health problems for them. I can rip off society — I can create costs that spill over onto everyone else. Economists call those externalities, because you can't trace them back to the company. If it's something that *can* be traced to the company, I can charge the company. But if it's something more opaque, you can't.

And that creates a whole other discussion, which is: should businesses go out of their way, even if they're not legally required to, to put protections in for all of these other stakeholders? ## The corporate life cycle: acting your age

**Damodaran:** But that's part of this discussion. Because if I make my end game maximizing sustainability — which is a terrible objective. What does "sustainable" mean to you? That you want a business that lasts for a longer time?
