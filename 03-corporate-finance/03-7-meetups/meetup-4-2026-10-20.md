## Before the session: a hurdle rate for a family business

**Student:** Professor, before the session starts, can I ask you a practical question about my family business? So the hurdle rate is hard for me to figure out. Obviously it needs to be better than Treasuries, and potentially a lot better than stocks, because it's riskier than a diversified portfolio and has a lot of idiosyncratic risk.

**Damodaran:** Let me ask you a question. Intuition is going to lead you to 20%, 25%, maybe even 30%. Do you think you can make that much return in your business?

**Student:** Yes, easily.

**Damodaran:** Then you've got padding you can use. That effectively means you have a competitive barrier to entry [ed. — transcribed as "comparative barrier"] that allows you to earn it. Most businesses can't.

**Student:** We have exclusivity arrangements with luxury brands, and they want us to reinvest in new inventory and merchandising, so we need to choose among that and other things we could do.

**Damodaran:** Do they also give you the pricing power to pass that cost on?

**Student:** Not necessarily, but the margins are already about 65%, so it's a high-margin business.

**Damodaran:** Then I think it's perfect. The truth is, your hurdle rate can't be more than 14% or 15%, no matter how much you dance. You just have a really good business. Having a big margin that lets you set an artificial number like 25% and get away with it doesn't make that your hurdle rate. So I would compute a true hurdle rate, reflecting what you could actually make as an investor elsewhere on an investment of similar risk, and file it away. Then say: we have all our money invested in this, so it's much riskier for us, and here's the extra amount we have to charge. You can use the total beta; it will push you toward a higher return. But be aware of both numbers. If your business ever gets squeezed, your margins drop or your brand-name clients start to do badly, you need to know your true hurdle rate.

**Student:** Okay. Thanks.

## Assessing AI with corporate finance, starting with governance

**Damodaran:** Welcome, folks. I'm going to get started. I'm sure that, like everybody else in markets and business, AI is on your mind, either as an investment or in your business. I recently posted that the skill set we need to assess AI is not valuation. It's corporate finance. If you think about AI as a business, which is what we should ultimately be doing, it's the biggest bet mankind has made on a new business in the last century. It behooves us to ask whether it makes sense as a business. So I'm going to take you through where we are with AI right now, using entirely corporate finance lenses. Some of this will be repetition, because I've talked about it in previous sessions.

Let's start with corporate governance, as the course did. We said that if you're an investor in a company, you have to think about where the power lies in that company and how much you as a shareholder can do to fix things if your interests and those of the people running the company diverge. We talked about boards of directors and the conventional mechanisms.

Let's keep it focused on the big AI players. There are two sets. First, the builders of AI: the hyperscalers, the four Mag 7 companies, Oracle. If you're an investor in these companies and you look at the hundreds of billions of dollars going into capex, you can have one of two feelings. One is that these people know what they're doing, they're smart, they must have a good reason, and everything will work out. But this is a bet they're making with your money. Let's be very clear: it's not company money or Mark Zuckerberg's money. It's your money and my money if we're shareholders. Say you don't think Meta and Google should be investing this much in AI capex. What are you going to do about it? That's a corporate governance question. How much power do you think shareholders, even big institutional investors, have at these companies?

**Student:** Almost nothing.

**Damodaran:** Almost nothing. Why?

**Student:** Because they've already given all the power to these prodigies.

**Damodaran:** In every publicly traded company, power comes from one of two things. One is that the people running the company own more than 51% of the shares. But at these companies the founders don't own 51%; it's not even close. Mark Zuckerberg owns 14% of Facebook. Sergey Brin and Larry Page own maybe 7% or 8% of Google. That was your time to take a stand and say: Mark, we're not buying your shares if you do that. Clearly, institutional investors didn't. Until 2004, companies with two classes of shares with different voting rights were almost unheard of in the US. Google broke the mold. What do you think the rationalization was that institutional investors used to justify investing in these second-class shares? Because that's what they are.

## Value transfer, not value creation

**Student:** Professor, a quick question. Aside from the circle, isn't this a transfer of value rather than value creation? Because if we lose jobs—

**Damodaran:** That's always been the case with technology. Describe the age of technology starting with the PC in the 1980s and running through now. It's been a golden age for technology. Look at the headlines: PCs, then the internet, then the smartphone, and all the amazing businesses that came out of them: Amazon, Uber, Airbnb. Do you know what global real GDP growth has been over the last 40 years? 3.37% a year. Do you know what it was from 1946 to 1986, what we'll call the primitive age? 4.15%. So where did all the value go? You know where Amazon gets its value: from brick and mortar. It's value transfer from other businesses.

I was listening to an AI optimist last week who said AI is going to triple global GDP. There will be no planet left if you triple global GDP. Climate change, all of that. This is the problem when you hold everything else constant and point to the additional value created at Anthropic, OpenAI and the AI agents. It's value transfer. Say Anthropic succeeds at getting AI agents to do what Accenture does. Accenture is a prime target for Anthropic: a business services company where employees mostly deliver cookbook solutions to companies. That's true of most consulting firms. You replace the consultants with agents. You're not creating more value at the top; GDP isn't going up. You're transferring wealth, first from the old consulting company, which loses its competitive advantage, maybe to younger consulting companies, and from the best paid of us, the consultants and bankers who get paid millions, to the companies developing the AI agents.

**Student:** To the agents.

**Damodaran:** Value transfer happens at lots of different levels, so I'd be very cautious about arguments that say the economy will prosper if we do this. That's the big productivity story with AI: it will make us more productive and create deflation and real GDP growth. Not so fast. Everything else has to change as AI changes. I can go along with the notion that AI can create valuable businesses. But we have to focus on net value, not gross value, net of the value destroyed at other businesses because AI succeeds. What I'd suggest is that you take two sheets of paper: one if AI wins, one if AI loses. Then trace through the full consequences of both. I'll tell you up front: both will be scary by the time you're done.

**Student:** It's very hard to define where AI wins and where it loses. It can't entirely lose, because we already use it.

**Damodaran:** You're right, there are intermediate outcomes, but it's still a continuum. Once you have the two ends nailed down, you can ask where on the continuum we as a society, forget Anthropic and OpenAI, are best served, because this has become a societal bet. It's too big. That's why I said the best-case solution for all of us might be that we do this slowly, spread out over time. That Anthropic goes public at a market cap below a trillion. When it started, the rumored number was $2 trillion. After last week's investor meeting in San Francisco, that number has drifted down toward $1.5 trillion. But if it goes for less than $1 trillion, it'll make people sit up. The companies that have leased data centers to Anthropic and OpenAI will say: I'm not building any more until we see how these guys do. That means a slowing of AI adoption, because the rest of the world has to catch up. The tech companies have taken off down this road, and the rest of us are struggling to keep up with the consequences. But it's in the best interest of OpenAI and Anthropic, and of Nvidia, to speed the process up and keep the cycle going, because that's how they get the maximum value.
