## Debt creates value only through tax savings

**Damodaran:** The entire basis for value creation from debt is that interest gives you tax savings, while cash flows to equity come out of after-tax cash flows. Without the tax savings, there is no benefit to borrowing money. None.

**Jose:** That's Miller-Modigliani, right?

**Damodaran:** Forget about Miller-Modigliani. I want this to be intuitive. We can give it a name later. Without tax savings, all you do when you borrow money is create pain for yourself. You increase default risk, and there is no benefit you're going to get. In cost of capital terms, if you set your tax rate to zero, your cost of capital will always go up when you borrow money. It's the tax savings that create the benefit of debt. So when you can afford to borrow money, you borrow. Why? Not because it's cheaper, but because it gives you tax savings. The taxpayer is subsidizing your company. I'm not making a moral or ethical judgment. It's perfectly legal. You're doing what the law allows you to do.

## Borrowing with no tax benefit: preferred stock and banks

**Valentyn:** But look at preferred stock. It's actually like debt, but without any tax benefits.

**Damodaran:** So you think it's a stupid way of raising financing, then? So why would a company do it? Why would companies issue debt without tax savings?

**Valentyn:** It's usually financial companies and banks, I believe.

**Damodaran:** Okay, let's isolate them. What is it about banks that induces them to do this really stupid thing? It is a stupid thing.

**Valentyn:** Tier 1 capital, I believe.

**Damodaran:** Because regulatory capital, which governs how banks are run, counts preferred stock as equity. It's stupid. I don't think regulators should do it. But 100 years ago, accountants said if it's called stock, it must be equity, which is the stupidest thing I can think of. What if I called my bank debt "bank stock"? Would you allow me to count it as equity? But that's what we do with preferred stock, and that's exactly why banks issue it. When you see deviations from the base principle that you shouldn't borrow money if there's no tax advantage, one reason is this regulatory overhang.

## The trade-off, and why most companies are underlevered

**Damodaran:** So we know the core trade-off. You've got tax benefits on one side and bankruptcy risk on the other. In a friction-free world, and I'll talk about what I mean by frictions, that trade-off is where you minimize your cost of capital. You might have come to the session where I optimize the capital structure and the optimal debt ratio comes out at 30%.

Every year I do this in my corporate finance class, and you have to do it with your company. There are 350 people in the class. They each pick a company, compute its optimal debt ratio, and then look at its actual debt ratio. Want to guess what percentage of companies are at their optimum?

**Valentyn:** About 10, 15%?

**Jose:** 5%.

**Damodaran:** 5% at best. Follow-up question: are more companies underlevered or overlevered? They're underlevered, about 80%. They'd rather not grow than go out and raise equity. Thank God for that. What happens to a market where 80% of companies are overlevered? It's on the verge of blowing up. It's one crisis away.

## South Korea in the 1990s

**Damodaran:** That's what happened in Korea in the late 1990s. The entire market blew up, because Korean companies collectively were overlevered. I remember looking at Korean companies in the late '90s and thinking, this is insanity. Every company I looked at had an optimal debt ratio of 30% and an actual debt ratio of 80%. Were Korean companies insane? What pushed the debt ratios up? Does anybody know the story?

**Valentyn:** Probably just very cheap debt or something.

**Damodaran:** They did get cheaper debt than they should have. But why were banks lending to these companies at rates lower than they should have been charging?

**Valentyn:** Government subsidies, probably.

**Damodaran:** It wasn't even government subsidies. The government implicitly sent a message. I'm not talking about North Korea, I'm talking about South Korea. South Korea wanted to grow and wanted its companies to have access to capital. So the government went to the banks and said, we will implicitly guarantee the loans you make to Hyundai and Samsung and the other leading companies. The banks said, okay, if you're backing the debt, we'll lend at below-market rates. And if you lend at below-market rates, what will companies do? They will overborrow.

The Korean government's rationale was that if one company got into trouble, it could cover the debt, maybe even three. But what created the Korean financial market crisis of 1996 [ed. — the crisis hit Korea in 1997, as part of the Asian financial crisis] was that 80% of companies got into trouble at the same time. The government didn't have enough money to back up the loans. The entire system cracked and came very close to tipping over.

Sometimes you see entire markets be overlevered. So if you're going to have a problem, at least make it an underlevered problem. In fact, you can argue for that from society's standpoint. There's no value lost to society when companies are underlevered. Do you see my rationale? What have they given up? The tax subsidies they would have gotten from taxpayers. Collectively, as a society, we don't lose when companies are underlevered, but we do lose when they're overlevered.

## "Isn't debt just cheaper than equity?"

**Horton:** Professor, I had a question on the previous point. I kind of disagree that it's only because of the tax savings. If a company can take out debt at 6% and do good projects with it, versus using its own equity, which requires a higher return, how is that not beneficial?

**Damodaran:** Does anybody want to take Horton's point? Because it's how real estate digs itself into a hole. Let's start with the base point. Is debt always cheaper than equity at any point in time? It has to be. Why? The lender is first in line and the equity investor is last in line. That's pure common sense. If the lender charges 6%, the equity investor will charge 8%, 9%, 10%, with the equity risk premium on top.

So you're saying, why wouldn't I borrow at 6% rather than raise equity at 9%? Horton, if I use that rationale, I'll be at 95% debt very quickly. What is it about the process that pushes back? Say I borrow money at 6%. Have I made the company riskier, at least the equity in the company? What happens to my cost of equity? It goes up. So even though 6 looks lower than 9 at first sight, when I borrow money the 9 becomes 9.6, 9.8, 10.

And when I borrow at 6, what happens to my default risk? It goes up. So the process of borrowing pushes up both the cost of equity and the cost of debt. In a world without taxes, that's why the cost of capital goes up when you borrow money. It looks completely illogical. I'm borrowing with the cheaper instrument, so how can my cost of capital go up? The answer is that your true cost of financing reflects the risk of your operations. Using more or less debt doesn't make your operations safer or riskier. So without a tax benefit, there's no value creation for the company from using debt. All you've increased is truncation risk, the risk that you won't make it. Horton, does that make sense?

**Horton:** Yeah, that makes sense. That's really interesting.

## Why banks hold regulatory capital

**Damodaran:** It's not just the Bundesbank. All banks are covered by regulatory capital requirements, and the question is what goes into your Tier 1 and Tier 2. That comes from Basel, from 50 years ago, when the banks got together, so they're all driven by the same principle. Tier 1 is basically equity capital, the strictest definition of capital. For that, you have to either have retained earnings or issue shares. Then there's a Tier 2 and a Tier 3, where you stretch the definition of capital, and with each tier it gets a little looser. But you have to meet all three. It's not pick one and you're fine.

Let's step back. Why are banks required to maintain regulatory capital?

**Jose:** To comply with…

**Damodaran:** That's too facile an answer. Why?

**Jose:** Because they have to earn margins and pay back the money to savers.

**Damodaran:** I think you're on to it. It's not just savers. Where do banks get the bulk of the capital they lend out? From depositors: checking accounts, savings accounts, fixed deposits. Let's go down to the core banking business. You collect money from deposits, lend it out as loans, and hope and pray the people who borrowed from you pay you back. Do they always pay you back? No. And if they fail, you've got a problem.

When you go into a bank and put money in a checking account, do they tell you there's a 90% chance you'll get it back? Or is it your money? It's your money. You're not investing in stocks. You put it in a checking account and expect to take it out tomorrow. Now do you see why banks are required to maintain regulatory capital? If they take a hit on their loans and try to pass it to depositors, you get a bank run. It happened during the Great Depression. Who can blame people with checking accounts? In those days they physically lined up outside the bank to get their money out. Therein lies a financial disaster. We had something similar with SVB recently.

After the Great Depression, the Federal Reserve put in regulatory capital requirements precisely to stop this. It worked really well until 2008, when it failed for the first time. Why did it fail then? What did banks do? They put themselves at risk.

**Jose:** I think that one was basically issuing…

**Damodaran:** Collectively they were lending at too low a rate. They were investing in mortgage-backed securities. There's nothing wrong with investing in mortgage-backed securities. There's something wrong with investing in ones that are truly BB-rated while acting like they're AA-rated. They blame the ratings agencies, but ultimately, when the risk came through in 2008, those investments blew up, and the federal government had to step in and bail out the banks.

**Jose:** So basically there was an asymmetry of information about risk.

**Damodaran:** This is the problem with regulatory capital. It's defined by rules. Banks thought they were fooling the regulators by buying things that looked like they passed the risk test but didn't. But they also fooled themselves. They took on too much risk, and it blew up.

## The Post Office problem
