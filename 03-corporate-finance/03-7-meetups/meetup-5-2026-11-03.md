## What a hurdle rate that is too high does

**Damodaran:** Take me through what happens if I use a 12%, 15% or 20% cost of equity in a discounted cash flow valuation or in capital budgeting, in a world where the market is pricing in a cost of equity of about 9%. The world is accepting 9%. I demand 15% as an investor. You're allowed to do whatever you want; it's your money. If you demand 15% or even 20% and start valuing companies, what are you going to find?

**Student:** It's underpriced.

**Damodaran:** I took every company in the S&P 500. There isn't a single company in the market that is cheap right now. So you're not going to hold equities. What are you going to hold instead? If you carry your outlandish cost of equity into real estate and say you need 20%, nothing looks cheap there either. So you either hold cash, which is fine if you're consistent, or you become a target for every scam on the face of the earth. Do you see why? If you demand 20%, who's going to beat it? A friend of a friend with an investment concoction that delivers 35% a year with no risk. That's Madoff on steroids. Or a YouTube celebrity who claims to have found a magical way of investing. It's a secret formula, usually with some options and currency derivatives thrown in. He can't tell you what it is, but you can make 30%. There is a cost to being too conservative.

As a CFO, what happens if you set your cost of capital too high? Many companies say they'll take the cost of capital and add a buffer. You can see the rationale: we don't want to make mistakes, so we'll use a higher cost of capital to prevent them. Say you use 15% when the world says you should accept 9%. If you're doing your capital budgeting right, what happens?

**Student:** You're not going to take any projects.

**Damodaran:** And if your competitors are doing the right thing, they're going to eat away your market share. You'll lose business to them and you'll shrink. That's exactly what happens at many private businesses. You get more conservative than you should be, you lose market share, and other people eat your business. And then when you get ready to sell, if you think your cost of capital is 15%, I would love to buy your business from you, because with a 15% cost of capital you'll push down your value.

I hold private company appraisers to account for this. They start with a risk-free rate plus something to get to a cost of equity or capital, the way we do. Then they go crazy and start adding premiums. A small-cap premium, which hasn't existed in 45 years: add 4%. A liquidity premium, which has a core of good sense, because private companies are illiquid: add 2% or 3% to the cost of capital, and then take another 25% off the value for the same reason. That's double counting. And when they get really desperate, they add the ultimate concoction: a company-specific risk premium, which should really be called the "I made this up" premium, another 6% or 7%. The end game is getting to a number you feel comfortable with.

The cost of capital is not the receptacle for your hopes, your fears and your fantasies. But in private companies that's what it becomes. For individual investors and businesses, there's a consequence to setting it too high, and I think many of them do. If you're an institutional investor, this borders on fraud.

## Q&A: why the convertible bond produced a loss

**Student:** I didn't really understand why they took the loss on the convertible bond.

**Damodaran:** Say Anthropic was worth about $50 billion two years ago, and it issues a convertible bond with a conversion price that corresponds to a $75 billion company value. You price the bond on the existing price, so it's about 90% debt and 10% conversion option struck at a $75 billion value. Now the company value goes to $1 trillion, which is what subsequent VC rounds made it. What happens to the value of the option, with the same strike price? It surges. So a convertible bond on which they raised $15 billion is now worth $105 billion in market terms because the conversion option paid off. They took that $90 billion and wrote it off, saying they didn't get any money for it. That's why it was a non-cash charge. They're writing off the surge in the conversion option that comes from the company's value going from $50 billion to $1.5 trillion.
