# Valuation and Pricing of Western Alliance Bancorporation

Author: Juan Carlos Hurtado Quimper Valuation as of: April 2023

# **About The Firm**

Western Alliance Bancorporation ("**WAL**") is a large bank holding company based in the United States. Its holdings include Western Alliance Bank, Alliance Bank of Arizona, Bank of Nevada, Bridge Bank, among other bank and (smaller) non-bank subsidiaries.

All in all, WAL's holdings offer a vast array of business banking and lending solutions, largely including Business & Industrial Lines of Credit ("**LOCs**"), Mortgage Warehouse Lending, and Commercial Real Estate ("**CRE**") Lending. The company also holds considerable exposure to residential real estate assets, through mortgage loans held for investment or sale, and residential mortgage-backed securities. WAL's banks are primarily focused on business banking and lending, and less than 1% of its loan portfolio (held for investment) involves consumer credit.

As a regional bank, WAL is a domestic company with no operations abroad. Virtually all revenue generated is within the United States.

# **Current Pricing & Performance**

WAL stock traded at **\$35.5/share** at the time of valuation, down over 30% since the beginning of the calendar year and down nearly 60% TTM, along with a variety of other US bank stocks which also showed negative performance within these timeframes.

The price slump across regional banks can be largely attributed to the 2023 Banking Crisis, which saw Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank placed under FDIC receivership and threw Silvergate Bank into a voluntary liquidation (not to mention UBS's proposed takeover of Credit Suisse). Investors reportedly feared collateral damage of these events infecting other regional banks, and the crisis sparked doubts on the strength of their balance sheets, particularly regarding their exposure to low-yielding longer-term securities in a rapidly rising rate environment and low regulatory capital balances 1 .

But independent of market sentiment, WAL's latest quarterly filing showed:

- Its net interest margin ("**NIM**") was 3.8%, up from 3.3% the year prior and only slightly lower than the industry average of 4.08% for all US banks with assets between \$50-99bn,

according to data from BankRegData, a bank financial data aggregator for over 4,000 US banks 2 . This corresponds with an industry-wide increase in NIM across banks over the past couple of quarters.

- Its deposits decreased by 11.1% from the previous year,
- Its Return on Equity stood at 19.0%, well above the industry average of 14% for banks within its asset size bracket, and
- Its Tier 1 Capital ratio was 10.1%, up from 9.8% the year prior but still below the industry average of 11.9% for the same group of US banks.

It seems that, at least on the surface, certain investor fears were not unfounded. The firm saw deposits decrease significantly, and a steep decrease in deposits (a "bank run") was one of the main factors behind the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank. Additionally, its Tier 1 Capital ratio was below its peers', which indicated that the firm's free cash flows to equity would need to decrease to retain earnings and increase the Tier 1. Nonetheless, the bank's ROE was well above its peers', and over the past 10 years it had successfully grown its assets by a 20%+ CAGR and maintained a NIM in line with its peers.

The question then becomes: did WAL's stock price decline represent an appropriate pricing correction? Or was the stock "over-punished" as a result of less overall confidence in the sector or an overreaction?

#### **Intrinsic Valuation: Discounted Cash Flow**

A 2-stage discounted cash flow model was used, based on Free Cash Flows to Equity and accounting for the reinvestment needs of the bank to meet its Tier 1 capital target.

A growth period of 5 years was chosen until the bank reaches a steady state. The shorter length of this period was due to the fact that WAL had already been undergoing high levels of growth (in assets and earnings) and could soon reach stability, despite facing current turmoil.

Since WAL already appears to be in a high-growth phase, with an ROE that surpasses its COE by a considerable margin, but also a regulatory capital margin below the industry average, we forecast that by the time it reaches a steady state, its ROE will gradually decrease to reach the industry average of 11%, and its Tier 1 ratio will gradually increase to reach 11.9%.

Additionally, the model assumes its current COE will also decrease by the time it reaches a steady state, given that the risk-free rate is likely to decrease (due to macroeconomic conditions), its beta is expected to approach its peer median of 1.1, and the ERP is likely to decrease as well as the economy regains confidence in US equities after recent turmoil. Nonetheless, the model is built on the assumption that WAL's ROE will continue to surpass its COE.

#### **Pricing: Comparables Analysis**

Basing our price analysis on the PBV Ratio (since it is an equity-focused multiple), we can see that at the time of valuation, WAL shares traded at a 0.76x PBV ratio, down from 1.88x the year prior.

I performed an analysis of 23 US banks shortlisted as comparables using the following criteria:

- **● Total Assets:** Under \$100bn.
- **● Geography:** Banks based in the US. Majority of revenue generated within the US.
- **● Company Type:** Public
- **● Business Lines:** Mostly provide **business banking** services (commercial and industrial financing, business LOCs, and or mortgage warehousing finance). Banks that operationally focus on consumer credit were excluded (such as Synchrony Financial, which has a larger interest margin but different risk profile due to the type of high-yield consumer financing products they offer) as well as non-bank financial services firms (such as Raymond James which is largely focused on investment banking, asset management, and other non-matching services).

This, in comparison to the aforementioned list of banks with assets of \$55-99bn, would likely render more appropriate comparables for WAL.

Upon first glance, the analysis revealed that WAL's **Tier 1 Capital Ratio** was 1%+ below the comparables' median and average of 11.3% and 11.5%. So we can immediately see that WAL may indeed need to increase its regulatory capital base to match its' peers' average.

WAL's **payout ratio** is ~12% below the average and median of approximately 30%. This may just be a factor of its need to retain capital to meet its Tier 1 capital requirements..

Nonetheless, WAL's **net interest margin**, defined as the difference between the average rates interest received and interest paid, was slightly above both the average and median. In addition, its ROE was also higher than the average and median, by approximately 7%.

All of this, combined with its P/BV ratio of 0.76 (which is 0.25 below the median), could indicate that the stock is undervalued. Using the median P/BV of 1.01, we arrive at a **\$48.2 price target**.

### **Pricing: Regression**

We also regressed each firm's P/BV ratio against its beta, its Tier 1 Capital Ratio, its ROE and its Net Interest Margin.

The Net Interest Margin is a good measure of a bank's profitability and its ability to balance the cost and duration of its liabilities with the returns on its assets. Banks have the ability to (and often) strategically alternate between fixed and variable rates for its assets and liabilities, depending on their short and long term interest rate forecasts and their overall outlook on the economy. Their NIM therefore can oftentimes represent their effectiveness at running their business.

The regressions yielded the following results:

$$P/BV = 0.84 - 1.04 * Beta + 7.65 * Tier1 Capital_{TOV} + 3.92 * ROE$$

# **Conclusion & Recommendation**

All three models arrive at the same conclusion, that the stock was, at the time of valuation, underpriced. Nonetheless, there are additional risk factors to consider. Most recently, analysts have begun to forecast decreasing values for commercial real estate assets within major metropolitan areas. Within CRE, office properties seem to be most at risk.

WAL is somewhat heavy on CRE lending. Based on its most recent 10-K (3 months prior to the time of valuation) CRE loans (owner- and non-owner occupied) represented 22% of the bank's HFI loan portfolio and 16% of its total assets. Per the 10-K, loans were originated at a 55% LTV (loan-to-value), which would allow for up to a 45% decrease in property value before the debt has to be marked down in case of foreclosure (assuming no further guaranties or cross-collateralized assets).

Fortunately, WAL claims that "substantially all of the Company's remaining CRE loans are secured by first liens", which gives it the highest positioning across each asset's capital stack over any mezzanine or subordinated position. Additionally, per the 10-Q from the time of valuation, CRE loans had decreased to 16% of its HFI portfolio and 10% of its assets.

Therefore, it is hard to assess the specific level of risk the write-offs associated with CRE loans pose for this bank. In any case, considering the proportion of these loans to WAL's assets I don't think this risk would meaningfully affect my analysis' recommendation in the near term.
