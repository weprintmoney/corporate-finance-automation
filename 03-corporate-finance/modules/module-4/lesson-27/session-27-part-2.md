# Session 27 Part 2 — Assessing Dividend Policy Across Companies

So to assess dividend policy for a company, not only do you have to assess how much the company could have paid in dividends, the free cash flow to equity, you also have to look at what it actually does return. You also have to get a measure of whether your company has good projects and whether it delivers solid return to its stockholders. That is what we did for Disney. But we can replicate what we did for Disney for all of the other companies in our sample.

## Vale: Paying Out More Than It Can Afford

So let's try this on Vale. Vale is a large Brazilian mining company, actually, a global mining company incorporated in Brazil. Let's look at what it pays out in dividends and what it could have paid out. In the period that I looked at, at Vale, its free cash flows to equity were actually negative, minus $1.9 billion if I don't count debt. And if I do count debt, it's about $1 billion. The company managed to return almost $42.8 billion in the form of dividends and buybacks. In fact, even if I use a target debt ratio, the free cash flow to equity I get is only $19 billion. It is beyond doubt that Vale during this time period was returning way more than it could in the form of dividends and buybacks than it could.

So, really, the question becomes, what do we do next? Clearly this is a problem, right? A company that pays out vast amount in dividends and buybacks with cash flows it doesn't have. So I'm going to try something. I'm going to be the CEO of Vale, and I'm going to put you in the role of a shareholder and I'm going to get up and ask for a change. A change in what? A change in my dividend policy. I'm going to make the case on those cash flows. I'm going to say, "Look I can't afford to pay what I'm paying in dividends, I just don't have the cash flow." Sounds reasonable, right?

But let me fill in the rest of the story. Part of the reason Vale has to pay the dividends that they do is they have two classes of shares. We talked about this at the very start of the class, voting shares and non-voting shares. One of the conditions that come with the non-voting shares that allows them to be non-voting is Vale's committed contractually to pay dividends on those shares. If it fails to pay those dividends on those preferred shares, it has to give up voting rights. So if you're a shareholder, odds are, you're a non-voting shareholder, you are the one receiving dividends, and what's Vale CEO asking, "Can I pay you less in dividends, right?" Before you agree, what would you like in return? Think about it. You demanded those large dividends because I told you, you could not vote. Now, I'm asking you to cut those dividends. I would assume that you're going to push back and say, "You can do that as long as you give me voting rights." In many companies the desire for control is so strong that companies will continue to pay dividends, a little like Vale does, and drive themselves into bankruptcy rather than give up control. So with the case of Vale, are they paying out too much in dividends? Absolutely. Can they do something about it? Only if they're willing to give up control.

## BP: The Dividend Addict Trap

So let's try this on a company I haven't talked about so far, BP, large oil company. And, in fact, this is way back in time between 1982 and '91. Between 1982 and 1991, BP reported free cash flows to equity on average each year of about $571 million a year. That's a pretty large cash flow, right? So let me ask you a question, it's a pretty obvious question. If you have free cash flows to equity of $571 million a year, how much can you afford to pay in dividends each year? Good answer is roughly $571 million, right? But here's what BP managed to do. In this 10-year period BP consistently managed to pay about $1.5 billion in dividends every year. You see, how does a company with $571 million in free cash flow to equity pay $1.5 in dividends? Each year for this period BP went out and borrowed $1 billion every year, not to take projects, not to drill more wells, but to pay in dividends.

Clearly, the company has a dysfunctional dividend policy. It's paying way too much in dividends. Now, the puzzling question is, "Hey, why didn't BP do something about it right away?" Now, here's the reason. Companies, once they get into a dysfunctional dividend policy, can't back away. As BP started paying bigger and bigger dividends in the early 1980s, it started attracting investors who like those dividends. So they bought BP stock because they liked big dividends. For lack of a better word, these were dividend addicts, they were addicted to dividends, and they're now stockholders in your company. Think of the problem you face. What are you going to do? Are you going to go in front of those stockholders who really love their dividends and tell them, "Hey look, we can't afford to pay those dividends?" You could try, but it's not going to go well.

So BP kept putting off the bad decision year after year after year hoping that something good will happen. Which for an oil company is that oil prices will go up and perhaps, perhaps the dividend problem will go away. That didn't happen. And, in fact, in 1991 BP was forced to cut dividends. You know why? Because when they went to the bank to borrow the billion dollars to pay the dividends as they had the previous 10 years, the bank said, "You're already the most highly levered oil company in the world, we're not funding you dividends." They cut dividends.

Now, we all agree based on a corporate finance stand point that this is the right thing to do. The company cannot afford those dividends. But to show you that the world doesn't always behave the way corporate finance textbooks would want them to behave. When this dividend cut was announced, the stock price dropped. In fact, to complete the story, it fully recovered a few weeks later. But you're saying, "What happened during those weeks?" So when the dividend cut was announced, these dividend addicts sold their shares, drove the price down. And it took a few weeks for other shareholders to step in and fill the breach. And during that period, half of BP stock management lost their jobs. And if you ask me, they deserved to. They deserved to for putting off this decision for as long as they did.

My advice to companies with dysfunctional dividend polices, policies like BP, is do something about it quickly while you still control your destiny. Don't deal with it only after you're forced to cut dividends. In fact, changing dividend policy is always one of the most difficult things for a company to pull off. And it goes back to that dividend clientele effect we talked about, you get the stockholders we deserve. So if you are a company that's paying too much in dividends, a company like BP, and you're saying, "How do I cut dividends without having a stock price effect?" The bad news is you cannot. Because when you cut dividends, your stock price will drop. The question is, can you bundle your cutting dividends with something that might make the drop in stock prices less severe?

And there are things you can try, in fact, there are studies that look at dividend cuts accompanied by other new stories. If the dividend cut is accompanied by just a drop in earnings. That's the worst possible scenario because investors say they cut dividends because times are bad, and they had no choice. So you look at the stock price effect, not only is it large at the day of the announcement, it continues for weeks after. If you cut dividends and you announce with it simultaneously that you're taking projects with that cash, maybe people don't believe you right away, the stock price does drop on the announcement day but to the extent that you can deliver news. So deliver information that backs up your argument that there are good investments, you might see the stock price come back. Now, don't take any of this stuff in these studies literally. It doesn't mean that if you do exactly this, you're going to get the same impact. But it's worth thinking about if you have to announce a change in dividend policy. You might want to make it a more comprehensive announcement about why you're doing it, and what you hope to do with that cash. Maybe markets will treat you more fairly if you do that.

## The Limited: The Token Dividend Trap

Let's move on to a fourth example. This is again, a company I haven't talked about so far, it's The Limited. And in 1992, The Limited was a high-growth retail company. The reason I picked The Limited is in the 10 years leading into 1992, The Limited's free cash flow to equity was minus $34 million a year on average. Now, think about it. If you have negative free cash flow to equity, and I ask you how much can you afford to pay in dividends, the answer is nothing, right? The Limited managed to pay $41 million in dividends even though it was a high-growth company taking good projects. You're saying, "How come?" Why would a high-growth company that requires the money to take reinvestment start paying in dividend?

So let me lay out the argument that probably got The Limited started on the policy of paying dividends. Early in the 1980s, the free cash flows to equity were negative. Let's assume they were visited by a consultant. The consultant says, "Look, by not paying in dividends, you're making yourself a stock that many shareholders can't buy." And that's actually true. There are some pension funds that cannot invest in shares that don't pay in dividends. So here's what the consultant suggests, "Why don't you pay a token dividend?" The Limited's dividend payouts started as a token dividend with $1 million. But here's the problem, when you pay a token dividend they attract those investors into your stock who like dividends, guess what they want the next year? More dividends, and still more dividends. And that's the pathway that led The Limited to where it is today.

So if you're a company that cannot afford to pay dividends, you have negative free cash flows to equity, don't go looking for trouble. Don't pay a token dividend or a small dividend hoping that, that will be sufficient. Because you're attracting investors who are, in fact, investors you don't want buying your shares if you're a young growth company with negative cash flows.

## Tata Motors: The Family-Group Subsidy

Let's do one final example. And this is Tata Motors. If you look at the free cash flows to equity for Tata Motors pre-debt, it is minus ₹106 billion. Even if you count that up with the target debt ratio, the free cash flow to equity is about ₹48 billion. The reason I emphasized that is no matter what your measure of free cash flow to equity, it turns out that what was returned by Tata Motors during this period ₹75.184 billion exceeds those free cash flow to equity. In fact, the only way you can explain the higher dividends is during this period Tata Motors went in and increased its debt ratio significantly by borrowing huge amounts of money. Remember in our assessment of capital structure we said that Tata Motors was over levered. One reason they have too much debt is because the acquisitions they did during the period. The other reason is because they're paying out dividends that they can't afford to pay out.

Here again, there might be a reason that explains why Tata Motors does what it does. It is part of a family group. And when you have a family group of companies, when one company pays out more than it can afford to, maybe another company in the group is making up the difference. As with capital structure, I think, Tata Consulting Services, which is the Tata group's biggest and most profitable company is, in a sense, subsidizing the dividend policies of all of the older Tata companies, something to think about when you look at a family group company.

## Bringing It Together: Making the Judgment

So let's think about bringing this process to fruition for your company. You've assessed the potential dividend, the free cash flow to equity, you've looked at the actual dividend, you've looked at what kinds of projects your company has, you've also looked at what kind of Jensen's have or returns your stock has been delivering over time. You're in a position to make a judgement about the company's dividend policy. If your company is holding back cash and has a history of taking bad projects and delivering poor returns, you're well within your rights to ask for your cash back. If it's burning through cash by paying out more than it can afford to, and it has good projects, I think you have to start thinking about how do you bring the company back to worth and get it to pay less in dividends because it needs that cash to take the projects. It is a subjective judgment. But it's a judgment you have to make nevertheless.
