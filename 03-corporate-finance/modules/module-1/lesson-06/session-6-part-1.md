# Session 6 Part 1 — Estimating the Equity Risk Premium

## What Drives the Equity Risk Premium

So now that we've talked about how to estimate risk-free rates, let's turn our attention to the price of risk, the equity risk premium. To get a hurdled rate for a company, you need this number, so let's talk about how best to estimate it.

The equity risk premium is the price of risk in the equity market. Put simply, it is the return you will demand over and above the risk-free rate to invest your money in stocks collectively. So let's think about it, generally speaking, what would drive that price of risk?

The first is, it should be greater than zero. Investors, they're investing in a risky investment, should demand a premium over and above the risk-free rate. That's stating the obvious, but might as well state it.

The second is, it should depend on how risk-averse investors are in a market. As risk aversion goes up, risk premiums will go up, and as risk aversion comes down, risk premium should come down. You can already see that given that fact, risk premium should vary across time because investor risk aversion does vary across time.

It should also depend on what you define as the average risk equity. If your definition of the average risk equity is GE, the risk premium you might demand would be very different than if your average risk equity is Facebook. So it depends on your risk aversion, it depends on what the average risk equity is, it will be greater than zero, and bottom line, it will change over time.

## A Thought Experiment: What Premium Would You Demand?

So let's talk about the ways in which you can estimate the equity risk premium. But before we do that, let's do a little test.

Let's assume that you have some money saved, and that money is invested in a government bond only 3% guaranteed. So that's where your money's invested right now. All of it is invested in a risk-free investment making 3%. I come to you with a stock fund. Let's make it specific, the Vanguard 500 Index Fund. That includes a 500 largest Market Cap stocks in the U.S. I offer you an investment in the fund, while in return for investing the fund, I ask you what kind of expected return you would demand from the fund.

Remember that you're making 3% guaranteed. The question I'm asking you is, how much would I need to offer you as an expected return? Remember I can't guarantee a return, these are stocks for you to make the switch from where you are now. Only a 3% return guaranteed to investing in stocks. There is no right answer to this question, but I'd like you to think about what the answer you would give it be, okay? I'll give you a second.

So you've thought about it? Here are your choices. The first and only non-viable choice here is less than 3%. Think of why. If you're already making 3% guaranteed, why would you settle for less than 3% on a risky investment? Every other choice is viable. If you said between 3% and 5%, that effectively means you're demanding a risk premium between 0% and 2%. You're not that risk-averse, and that's okay. Some people are less risk-averse than others. You might have said 5% to 7%, in which case your equity risk premium is between 2% and 4%. Seventy-nine percent would give you four to six, and as you climb that table, you're effectively telling me that you're becoming more risk-averse. The more risk-averse you are, the higher the risk premium you demand.

Now, if I could do this for every investor in the market...do what? Ask this question, essentially back out what your risk premium would be as an investor. The equity risk premium I would use for the entire market would be a weighted average of the risk premiums I get from each and every investor. Weighted by what? Not by how enthusiastic you were when you told me what your risk premium was, but by how much money you have. This is not a democracy, it's a dollar way to democracy. The more money you have, the greater the weight I will attach your equity risk premium.

So in a world in which you can estimate the equity risk premiums by asking people, you could basically take a weighted average of the risk premium that people offer you. You say, "Why don't we do this?" First, there are millions of people in the market. You'd have to ask this of 50, 55, 60 million people all invested in stocks. Let's say you could pull that off. You'd have a second problem. That risk premium is not a constant, and to see why, let me ask you a follow-up question.

Remember a second ago I asked you how much of a risk premium you'd demand for investing in the S&P 500 relative to making 3% guarantee, and you picked a number, two, four, six, or eight? Now, let's assume that in the second sense the market's collapsed. The S&P 500 is down 25%. I'm going to ask you the same question I asked you before, what kind of risk premium would you demand for investing in stocks? I wager that your risk premium has changed. For some of you, it's probably gone up. Why? You had a harsh reminder of what risk in stocks is all about, right? The 25% drop. For others who are contrarians, this might mean that stocks are actually less risky now than they were a second ago. After all, a 25% drop in stocks makes them more of a bargain. Your risk premium could actually go down and for some of you, it might not be changed. The bottom line is, risk premiums change over time for a variety of reasons, and if you've surveyed the premium, you constantly have to update those surveys.

## Three Ways to Estimate an Equity Risk Premium

So here, in broad forms, are the three ways you can estimate an equity risk premium. The first is you could do a survey which tries to be dynamic, where you keep asking people what kind of premium they would demand for investing in stocks. These are called survey premiums. The second, is you can look backwards. What am I talking about? You can look at the historical data. You can look at what you'd have made in stocks relative to something risk-free over the last 20 years, the last 50 years, even the last 100 years. It's called a historical premium. The third is, you can try to back out of stock prices today, what people are demanding and the expected premium going forward. It's called an implied premium. Survey premiums, historical premiums, implied premiums.

## Survey Premiums

Let's start with the first of those. We talked about survey premiums. You ask investors what they would demand for investing in stocks as opposed to investing in something riskless, what they think stocks would do in the future. Clearly you can't survey all investors, there are too many of them, but there have been attempts to survey subsets of investors.

On this table, for instance, I have listings of different groups of investors who've been surveyed. Individual investors used to be surveyed by the Securities Industry's Association, a practice that they ceased in 2004 because they discovered it was close to useless. What you were getting were not expectations but hopes.

Merrill Lynch, every month, surveys institutional investors and ask them a very simple question, what do you think stocks will do over the next year? They're back into an equity risk premium based on the answer and here's how. If equity institutional investors claim that they can make a 7% return over the next year investing in stocks and the T-bond rate is 2.5%, 7% minus 2.5% is 4.5%, that's a survey premium. In the 2013 survey, that number was 4.8%, the equity risk premium.

There's a survey of CFOs that Campbell Harvey and John Graham do every year. It's an extraordinarily useful survey because it asks CFOs who, after all, use hurdle rates in capital budgeting every day, what they use as risk premiums, again a survey estimate. And finally, there are surveys of analysts and academics that also offers a number.

Having said all of this, I don't know of a single bank, a single consulting firm, a single analyst who uses survey premiums to come up with a hurdled rate and a cost of equity. Survey premiums are just too volatile and effectively reflect more of the past than the future, but at least you have a sense of what's involved.

## Historical Risk Premiums

Here's a second way you can estimate a risk premium. You can look at the past. At the start of every year, for the last 25 years, on my website, I've estimated the historical risk premium for the U.S. going back to 1928. If you ask me what the historical risk premium of the U.S. is right now, as of 2017, I could give you 12 different numbers as answers. You think, "12 really?" And here's why.

The risk premium for stocks will depend on what slice of history I look at. The premium I get going all the way back to 1928, 1928 through 2016, is very different than the premium I get if I go back 50 years or 10 years or 25 years. It depends on the slice of history I look at. It depends on what I define as my risk-free investment. Is it T-bills, which are short-term governments, or T-bonds? I get different risk premiums. The premiums over T-bonds tend to be smaller than the premiums over T-bills. It also depends on how I compute an average. I could compute an arithmetic average, where I take the numbers each year for 50 years, add them all up and divide by 50, or I can compute a geometric average, which is a compounded average. My premiums range anywhere from 2.3% to almost 8% depending on the choice I make. That's my first problem with historical risk premiums, you can take your pick.

If you put me on the spot and said you have to pick a historical risk premium, here's what my choices would be. The first is, I would go back as far as I can. In my case, I would go all the way back to 1928. Strange, right? That's before the Great Depression, but here's why I have to do it. Every single one of these risk premiums is an estimate. Remember Statistics 101? When you have an estimate, you have a standard error on that estimate. The standard error you get by looking at the data going all the way back to 1928 is 2.28%. So my risk premium, if I go all the way back to 1928, might be 4.62% if I use a geometric premium, but my standard error is close to 2.3%. That's a big range on my true risk premium. If you add and subtract two standard errors, it could be anywhere from 0% to 9%. That's going back to 1928. Take a look if I go back only 10 years. My risk premium is 2.30%, but before you get too excited, notice the standard error in that number is 8.66%. That 10-year risk premium is pure noise, so I have to go back as far as I can because I need the data.

Second, I need to be consistent about what I call risk-free. I've already told you that I use the T-bond rate, that 10-year bond rate as my risk-free rate. In corporate finance, my risk premiums have to be over the 10-year bond rate. And third, because this number is going to go into a hurdled rate, which I use to discount cash flows in year 1, year 2, all the way through year 10 I'm compounding that discount rate, I'm going to go with a compounded average. If you force me to pick a risk premium, a historic risk premium, as of 2017, the number I'd pick on this graph is 4.62%. That's the geometric average premium for stocks of a T-bonds going all the way back to 1928. But use with caution, it is still an estimate.

## Historical Premiums Outside the U.S.

Now, if you live in the U.S. or you're working in the U.S., or you're looking at a U.S. company, you have historical data going back not just to the 1920s, but going all the way back to the 1850s, maybe the 1870s. That's a lot of historical data. In many markets outside the U.S., you don't have the luxury of going all the way back to the 1920s or even the 1960s. If you asked me what the equity risk premium is for China, I'd be lucky to get 25 years of data. As this table indicates, when you have risk premiums over such short time periods, it's more noise than estimate, and we have to talk about other ways of estimating risk premiums for most other markets.
