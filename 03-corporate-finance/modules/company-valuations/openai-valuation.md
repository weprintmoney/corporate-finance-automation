The OpenAI Foundation board also serves on the **OpenAI Group PBC** for-profit board. The Foundation retains the exclusive right to appoint and replace all for-profit board directors.

A **Safety and Security Committee (SSC)** remains a committee of the Foundation, providing governance over safety and security practices across the entire organization.

### Governance Assessment

OpenAI's governance structure is unique and consequential:

1. **Nonprofit control with for-profit economics:** The OpenAI Foundation holds ~26% of the economic interest in OpenAI Group PBC but exercises **100% control** through exclusive board appointment rights. This is an inversion of the normal correlation between economic ownership and control.

2. **Separation of ownership and control:** Microsoft (27% economic stake) and all other investors have no board seats on the Foundation board and no power to remove directors. Investors have no governance rights commensurate with their economic stake.

3. **Mission-first mandate:** The PBC structure legally requires the board to balance the company's stated public benefit purpose (safe, beneficial AI) against investor returns. The board is not purely a stockholder-serving body.

4. **Altman's anomalous position:** Sam Altman has no equity stake (his grant remains "TBD/pending" as of the 2026 cap table). He serves on the board as CEO but owns none of the company he runs — an unusual situation that reduces one typical agency-cost vector (founder self-dealing) while creating another (misaligned incentives for CEO retention and compensation).

5. **Independent board majority:** 8 of 10 Foundation board members are independent directors, strengthening the case that the board is more insulated from investor pressure than a typical VC-backed startup.

### Power Structure

Power at OpenAI sits with:
- **The Foundation board**, which controls director appointments and the public benefit mandate
- **Sam Altman** as CEO, who sets operational direction and commands enormous reputational/relational capital
- **Microsoft**, as the infrastructure partner and largest investor (~27%), which exercises soft influence through commercial dependency (Azure compute) and board observation rights (not voting seats)

Conventional shareholder power (the ability to vote out the board, demand dividends, or force a sale) is substantially weaker here than at a typical private company. The November 2023 board-vs-Altman crisis illustrated how the Foundation board can unilaterally fire the CEO — but also how quickly external market forces (employees, investors, Microsoft) can reverse a board decision.

**Bottom line:** The governance structure concentrates formal authority in an independent nonprofit board operating under a public benefit mandate — unusual for a company at this scale and valuation. It is more analogous to a foundation-controlled European family firm than a traditional US venture-backed startup.

## Step 3 — Stated Objectives

### What OpenAI Officially Targets

OpenAI's stated mission is "to ensure that artificial general intelligence benefits all of humanity." As a PBC, it must explicitly balance that public benefit purpose against financial returns. In founder/investor communications, Sam Altman has emphasized:
- Building safe AGI as the north star, not short-term profit maximization
- Revenue growth as instrumental to funding frontier research
- IPO as a future milestone, not an immediate driver of decisions

### What Behavior Suggests

The tension between stated and revealed objectives is significant:

1. **Revenue growth is the clear operational focus.** OpenAI went from ~$1B ARR (2023) to ~$40B ARR (August 2026) in roughly three years — the fastest revenue ramp in software history. This pace requires intense product focus, pricing optimization, and enterprise sales motion that look indistinguishable from maximizing revenue.

2. **Losses are treated as strategic investment, not a problem.** The company burned ~$20.9B in 2025 and expects to burn further billions in 2026, with positive free cash flow not projected until ~2029–2030. Management explicitly accepts this as the cost of staying at the frontier.

3. **Valuation-maximization behavior is visible.** The $122B raise at $852B valuation (March 2026), the confidential S-1 filing (June 2026), and the tender offer at $687.69/share (implying $852B post-money) are consistent with a company optimizing for eventual public market value.

4. **Safety investment is real but subordinate.** The SSC and public benefit mandate create real governance constraints, but the compute spending trajectory ($74B projected losses by 2028) suggests capability acceleration is the dominant operational priority.

**Assessment:** OpenAI is pursuing **value maximization** — but on a longer time horizon than typical VC-backed companies, with a genuine (if instrumentalized) safety constraint. For valuation purposes, the appropriate objective function is long-run firm value maximization with a higher-than-normal discount for mission/governance risk.

### Who Is the Marginal Investor?

For a private company of this scale, the marginal investor is not a retail stockholder but falls into two categories:

1. **Secondary market participants:** A liquid secondary market has developed around OpenAI shares, with the June 2026 tender offer setting a reference price of $687.69/share. Institutional buyers in this market (hedge funds, crossover funds, specialized private market funds) are effectively the marginal setters of OpenAI's current implied valuation.

2. **Late-stage / crossover institutional investors:** SoftBank Vision Fund, Tiger Global, and the sovereign wealth funds (GIC, Abu Dhabi's MGX) that participated in the $122B round are representative marginal investors for new primary capital. These are large, sophisticated, globally diversified institutions.

### Is the Marginal Investor Diversified?

Yes — the marginal investors (large sovereign wealth funds, crossover hedge funds, major VC partnerships) are broadly diversified across asset classes, geographies, and sectors. This is an important input for beta: the appropriate risk measure is **market beta**, not total beta — **unless** we are computing the discount rate for an acquirer or internal capital allocation where the investor is OpenAI itself (undiversified in its own business). Given OpenAI's private status and the significant concentration risk for early employees with illiquid shares, we will also compute **total beta** as the relevant rate for private-market pricing (per lesson 11).

## Step 6 — Equity Risk Premium (Mature Market)

### Methodology

Following Damodaran's preferred approach, we use the **implied (forward-looking) equity risk premium** rather than historical averages. The implied ERP is derived by solving for the discount rate that equates the current S&P 500 price with the present value of expected future cash flows (dividends + buybacks), using consensus earnings growth estimates.

### Current Implied ERP

| Source | ERP Estimate | Date |
|--------|-------------|------|
| Damodaran implied ERP (start of 2026) | 4.23% | January 1, 2026 |
| Damodaran implied ERP (most recent) | 4.45% | July 2026 |
| Historical geometric average (1928–2024, stocks vs. T-bonds) | ~4.2% | Long-run |

The July 2026 update (4.45%) is the most current estimate from Damodaran's published data, reflecting the S&P 500 at that time and expected cash flows.

**Mature market ERP used in this analysis: 4.45%**

*Source: Damodaran, "Data Update 2 for 2026: A Testing Year (2025) for US Equities"; updated ERP post as of July 2026.*

Note: The US is treated as a mature market (effectively zero country risk premium for the US itself), so the total ERP for the US = mature market ERP = 4.45%.

## Step 8 — Regression Beta (Why It Doesn't Apply)

### The Private Company Problem

OpenAI has **no publicly traded stock** and therefore no historical stock return data against which to run a regression. A regression beta (the slope of Rj = a + b·Rm) simply cannot be estimated.

### What a Regression Beta Would Look Like for Comparable Public Companies

For context, here are regression betas for the closest public comparables:

| Company | Approx. 5-Year Regression Beta | Notes |
|---------|-------------------------------|-------|
| Microsoft (MSFT) | ~0.90 | Mature, diversified; Azure/AI exposure partially muted by Office/gaming |
| Alphabet (GOOGL) | ~1.05 | Search + Cloud + AI; moderate cyclicality |
| Palantir (PLTR) | ~1.35 | AI/data analytics, high-growth, smaller cap |
| Snowflake (SNOW) | ~1.50 | High-growth cloud data; volatile |
| C3.ai (AI) | ~1.70 | Pure-play AI software; high beta, smaller cap |
| Hypothetical OpenAI (if public) | ~1.60–2.00 | Estimate; high-growth, AI-infrastructure, negative earnings |

The regression beta for OpenAI — if it were public and had traded for 5 years — would likely fall in the 1.60–2.00 range based on its business profile: pure-play AI, high revenue growth, massive operating losses, no earnings anchor, and a product category with high cyclical sensitivity to enterprise tech spending.

However, regression betas are backward-looking, noisy (especially for short trading histories), and particularly unreliable for high-growth companies that are changing rapidly. This is why we proceed with the **bottom-up beta approach** in Steps 10–11.

## Step 9 — Beta Fundamentals

### Determinant 1: Product/Service Type (Business Risk)

OpenAI's revenue mix as of mid-2026:
- ~65% ChatGPT subscriptions (consumer Plus, Team, Pro tiers; enterprise Seats)
- ~25% API access (developers, B2B integrations)
- ~10% enterprise partnerships and strategic deals

**Beta implication — HIGH:**
- Consumer AI subscriptions are discretionary spending. When budgets tighten, subscription services get cut before necessities.
- API revenue is tied to developer/startup activity, which is cyclical with venture capital availability and tech sector health.
- Enterprise AI seats are somewhat stickier (multi-year contracts) but still subject to budget reviews.
- Comparable: Software-as-a-service at high growth phases exhibits betas in the 1.3–1.8 range. OpenAI's pure-play AI positioning and lack of earnings diversification push toward the high end.

### Determinant 2: Operating Leverage

Operating leverage = sensitivity of EBIT to revenue changes = Fixed Costs / Total Costs.

OpenAI's cost structure:
- **Compute costs (primarily fixed commitments):** OpenAI has $600B+ in multi-year cloud purchase commitments and Stargate capital commitments. The committed compute costs are effectively fixed in the short run regardless of revenue.
- **Headcount:** ~15,000 employees (estimated mid-2026). Salaries are largely fixed.
- **R&D spending:** High and growing — treating as semi-fixed given the competitive necessity.

**Operating leverage is very high.** With a negative operating margin of -122% (Q1 2026: for every $1 of revenue, $2.22 of cost), small changes in revenue translate to outsized swings in EBIT. This is structurally equivalent to high operating leverage — the fixed cost base means a 10% revenue increase would improve operating income by far more than 10%.

High operating leverage → **pushes beta higher**.

### Determinant 3: Financial Leverage

As of Q1 2026:
- **Financial debt:** $0 (reported zero debt)
- **Lease liabilities:** <$750M (modest relative to $73B cash + $852B valuation)
- **Debt/Equity ratio:** ~0% (essentially unlevered)

OpenAI is **financially unlevered** — it funds itself entirely through equity raises and has no significant debt service obligations. This is unusual for a company at this scale, but reflects the combination of (1) massive equity fundraising success and (2) no assets to borrow against in a traditional sense (AI model weights are not bankable collateral).

Financial leverage → **minimal upward push on beta** (essentially zero adjustment from unlevering/relevering).

**Summary:**
- Business risk: HIGH → beta > 1.5
- Operating leverage: VERY HIGH → additional upward pressure
- Financial leverage: NEGLIGIBLE → no meaningful adjustment
- **Expected levered beta range: 1.55–1.75** (before leverage adjustment, which is minimal)

## Step 10 — Bottom-Up Unlevered Beta

### Methodology

OpenAI's business spans:
1. **AI model APIs and platform** (analogous to Software – Internet; cloud-native AI services)
2. **Consumer subscription software** (ChatGPT Plus/Pro/Team — analogous to Software – System & Application)
3. **Enterprise AI** (enterprise Seats, custom deployments — analogous to Computer Services + Information Services)

We weight these using approximate revenue share:

| Segment | Revenue Weight | Damodaran Category | Unlevered Beta (cash-corrected, Jan 2026) |
|---------|---------------|-------------------|-----------------------------------------|
| AI API / Platform | 25% | Software (Internet) | 1.59 |
| Consumer AI Subscriptions | 50% | Software (System & Application) | 1.25 |
| Enterprise AI / Custom | 25% | Computer Services | 0.96 |

*Source: Damodaran, Betas by Sector (US), January 2026*

### Weighted Unlevered Beta Calculation

```
Unlevered Beta (operations) =
  (0.25 × 1.59) + (0.50 × 1.25) + (0.25 × 0.96)
= 0.3975 + 0.625 + 0.24
= 1.2625
```

**Bottom-up unlevered beta ≈ 1.26**

### Cash Adjustment

OpenAI holds ~$73B in cash against an $852B valuation:
- Cash/Firm Value ≈ 73/852 ≈ 8.6%

The unlevered beta corrected for cash (pure-play operating beta):
```
Unlevered Beta (pure play) = Unlevered Beta (operations) / (1 - Cash/Firm Value)
= 1.26 / (1 - 0.086)
= 1.26 / 0.914
= 1.38
```

However, given the cash will be consumed by operating losses within 2–3 years and is not a true permanent cash buffer, we use the **operations-level unlevered beta of 1.26** as our base (not adjusting for the transient cash balance).

**Bottom-up unlevered beta used: 1.26**

Note on composition: This blend is somewhat conservative. OpenAI is arguably more "Software (Internet)" than this weighting suggests — its entire business is AI-native and cloud-delivered. Using 100% Software (Internet) unlevered beta would give 1.59. The blended 1.26 represents a reasonable central estimate.

## Step 11 — Levered Beta and Total Beta

### Levered Beta

Using the Hamada equation:
```
Levered Beta = Unlevered Beta × (1 + (1 - Tax Rate) × D/E)
```

Inputs:
- Unlevered Beta: 1.26
- Debt: $0 (financial debt)
- Equity: ~$852B (post-money valuation)
- D/E ratio: 0 / 852B = 0%
- Tax rate: 21% (US federal corporate; PBC is taxed as a C-corp; state taxes add ~5%, so effective marginal ~26%, but use 21% federal as benchmark)

```
Levered Beta = 1.26 × (1 + (1 - 0.21) × 0.00)
= 1.26 × 1.00
= 1.26
```

Since OpenAI has no debt, **levered beta = unlevered beta = 1.26**.

### Cost of Equity (Levered Beta, for diversified institutional investors)

```
Cost of Equity = Risk-Free Rate + Levered Beta × Weighted ERP
= 4.68% + 1.26 × 4.97%
= 4.68% + 6.26%
= 10.94%
```

### Total Beta (for undiversified investors — private company context)

As discussed in Lesson 11, for a private company whose owners/early employees are not fully diversified, the relevant risk measure is **total beta**, which accounts for both systematic and idiosyncratic risk.

```
Total Beta = Market Beta / Correlation with Market
```

Using the Software (Internet) sector correlation from Damodaran's total beta table (January 2026):
- Correlation with market: 30.40% (Software – Internet sector average)
- Software (System & Application) correlation: 26.89%
- Computer Services correlation: 25.57%

Weighted average correlation (using same revenue weights as Step 10):
```
Weighted Correlation = (0.25 × 30.40%) + (0.50 × 26.89%) + (0.25 × 25.57%)
= 7.60% + 13.45% + 6.39%
= 27.44%
```

```
Total Unlevered Beta = Unlevered Beta / Correlation
= 1.26 / 0.2744
= 4.59
```

Since financial leverage is zero:
```
Total Levered Beta = Total Unlevered Beta = 4.59
```

### Cost of Equity Using Total Beta (Private Market Pricing)

```
Cost of Equity (total beta) = 4.68% + 4.59 × 4.97%
= 4.68% + 22.81%
= 27.49%
```

### Which Beta to Use?

| Investor Type | Beta | Cost of Equity |
|--------------|------|---------------|
| Diversified institutional investor (VC/SWF) | 1.26 (levered) | 10.94% |
| Undiversified private owner / early employee | 4.59 (total) | 27.49% |
| **Recommended for valuation (institutional market)** | **1.26** | **10.94%** |

Given that OpenAI's marginal investors are diversified institutional funds (SoftBank, Amazon, Microsoft, large VC firms, crossover funds), the **levered market beta of 1.26** is the appropriate input for WACC. The total beta is reported for completeness and is especially relevant for early employees evaluating concentrated exposure.

## Step 12 — Cost of Debt and WACC

### Estimating the Cost of Debt

OpenAI has **no public bond rating** and **no financial debt** as of Q1 2026. We proceed with synthetic rating estimation for completeness and to establish what OpenAI's cost of debt would be if it chose to borrow.

**Interest Coverage Ratio:**
- EBIT (2025): -$20.9B (negative — deeply loss-making)
- Interest expense: near $0 (no debt)
- Interest coverage ratio: Not applicable / negative

A deeply negative EBIT makes the standard synthetic rating approach non-mechanically applicable. However, we can assess creditworthiness through an alternative lens:

| Factor | Assessment |
|--------|-----------|
| Cash balance | $73B — extraordinary coverage for any near-term debt obligation |
| Revenue trajectory | $40B ARR and growing — strong revenue support |
| Operating losses | -$20.9B (2025), structurally burning cash fast |
| Equity cushion | $852B implied equity value — massive residual value |
| Business model durability | High dependency on frontier AI maintenance; competition risk from Google, Anthropic, Meta |

**Synthetic rating estimate: BB+ to BBB-**

Rationale: OpenAI's massive cash balance and high revenue growth would push toward investment grade, but the deeply negative operating income and enormous projected future losses create significant uncertainty. A BB+/BBB- rating reflects "speculative grade with very strong near-term liquidity but uncertain long-term profitability."

Using Damodaran's default spread table (January 2017 reference values, adjusted upward for 2026 rate environment):
- BB+/BBB- spread over 10-year Treasury: approximately 1.75–2.00% in 2026 rate environment
- Pre-tax cost of debt: 4.68% + 1.875% = **6.56%**
- Marginal tax rate: 26% (21% federal + ~5% state, blended)
- After-tax cost of debt: 6.56% × (1 - 0.26) = **4.85%**

### Market Value of Debt

| Debt Component | Amount | Notes |
|---------------|--------|-------|
| Financial debt (bonds/loans) | $0 | Confirmed as of Q1 2026 |
| Operating leases (capitalized) | <$750M | Q1 2026 disclosure |
| Off-balance-sheet compute commitments | $600B+ | NOT conventional debt; purchase commitments with suppliers |
| **Total debt (for WACC)** | **~$750M** | Lease obligations only |

The compute commitments are contractual obligations but are not financial debt in the Damodaran sense — they are operational contracts that would appear as operating expenses when incurred, not as borrowed capital. They affect free cash flow projections but not the capital structure for WACC purposes.

### Capital Structure (Market Values)

| Component | Market Value | Weight |
|-----------|-------------|--------|
| Equity | $852,000M ($852B) | 99.9% |
| Debt (leases) | $750M | 0.1% |
| **Total Capital** | **$852,750M** | **100%** |

### Marginal Tax Rate

OpenAI is a PBC (C-corp equivalent for tax purposes):
- Federal corporate tax rate: 21%
- State (California): ~8.84%
- Blended marginal rate (with some deductions): **~26%**

Note: OpenAI's massive NOL carryforwards (from years of operating losses) mean its **effective** tax rate is likely near 0% for many years to come. For cost-of-capital purposes, however, we use the **marginal** rate (what they would pay on an incremental dollar of income once profitable).
