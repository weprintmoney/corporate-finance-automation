# Session 16 Part 1 — Currency Consistency and Uncertainty in the Rio Disney Analysis

The last two sessions, we've looked at the Rio Disney Theme Park. We looked at the cash flows, and we looked at the returns, and we've concluded that it was a good investment. But in making this assessment, we cheated. We cheated in the sense, we use U.S. dollars for all our cash flows even though this Theme Park was going to be in Brazil. In this session, I'd like to look at what would have happened if I'd done the entire analysis in Reais, and then look at the uncertainty associated with almost every number in this analysis.

## Recap of the U.S. Dollar Analysis

So, let's turn back to the Disney Theme Park. Let me summarize what I found on a U.S. dollar basis. I projected the cash flows for the next 10 years. I came up with a terminal value that captured what I thought would happen after year 10, and I discounted all those cash flows, all dollar cash flows at a dollar cost of capital of 8.46%. The net present value that I came up with was about 3.3 billion dollars.

But then again, this Theme Park is in Rio. The people coming into the Theme Park are going to pay in Reais and your costs are all going to be in Reais. So, the reality is this entire project, the cash flows are going to be in Reais.

## Should the Currency You Choose Change the Answer?

So, I have a question and this is a very general question. If I had done this entire analysis in nominal Reais instead of dollars, do you think I'd have gotten a different answer? Think about it. If your answer is yes, then we're in trouble because it basically means that whether you think a project is a good project or a bad project, whether you should accept or reject it, will depend on what currency you project the cash flows in, what currency you do the returns in.

The answer you would like to get to this question is, your answer shouldn't depend on what currency you do the project analysis in. A good project in U.S. dollars should remain a good project in nominal Reais. And I've some good news. If you do things right, your analysis should not be affected by the currency you pick. You're saying, "How can that be?"

## Redoing the Analysis in Nominal Reais

Let's go back to the Rio Disney Theme Park and think about what would have been different if we'd done everything in nominal Reais. The answer is everything.

Let's start with the discount rate. The 8.46% cost of capital, that was a U.S. dollar cost of capital, right? If I'd done the analysis in nominal Reais, that discount rate, that cost of capital would have been a much higher number. In fact, we've used this mechanism before and we'll use it again. To convert a U.S. dollar cost of capital in nominal Reais cost of capital, you take the differential inflation, 9% in Brazil, 2% in the U.S., and if you factor that in the cost of capital you end up with the nominal Reais is 15.91%. That's bad news, right? Higher discount rate. Higher discount rate should lead to a lower net present value.

But wait, I'm not done yet. My cash flows are in U.S. dollars. If my discount rate has become a nominal Reais discount rate, my cash flows have to be nominal Reais. So, I've got to convert my dollar cash flows into Reais cash flows. And here's where people make a mistake. To convert the cash flows from dollars to Reais, they often use today's exchange rate all the way through the analysis. If you do that, all is lost.

Because here's what you need to do. Because there is a difference in inflation, 9% percent in Brazil, 2% in the U.S., you have to assume that over time, your exchange rates will change, and they will change in a very predictable way. I'm using what's called purchasing power parody in foreign exchange to estimate what my exchange rates will be a year from now, two years from now, three years from now, and so on. Roughly speaking, here's what's going to happen. My higher inflation currency, in this case the Reais, is going to depreciate roughly 7% percent a year. So, my expected exchange rates would start at 2.35 Reais right now. And end up at 4.56 Reais per dollar by the end of year 10. I used those expected exchange rates to convert my dollar cash flows into Reais cash flows.

I'm ready now. My cash flows are now all in nominal Reais. My discount rate is in nominal Reais. When I take the present value, the net present value that I get for this project in nominal Reais terms is 7.745 billion Reais, converted it to today's exchange rate because net present values in today's dollars, I get 3,296 million dollars, exactly the same answer that I got when I discounted dollar cash flows at a dollar cost of capital.

Incidentally, to compute the terminal value when I did everything in Reais, my growth rate was still the inflation rate after year 10. But remember the inflation rate if I'm doing everything in Reais, will be 9%.

## The Intuition: Pick a Currency and Be Consistent

That takes care of the cash flows and the discount rate. And intuitively, here's what's happening. When I switch from a low-inflation currency which is the U.S. dollar to a higher inflation currency, my discount rate went up which makes sense, but so did my cash flows. Because that same inflation helped me on the cash flows. So you can do any project in any currency. Make a pick and do it right. It is better to pick a currency and stay consistent with that currency all the way through than to switch currencies midway through, and end up with a net present value that doesn't make sense.

## Facing Uncertainty in Every Estimate

So, let's step back and think about all of the numbers I've used on this project. I projected out revenues for the next 10 years, right, based on what I've already seen in my existing Theme Parks in Disney Land, Disney World but I could be horribly wrong. Rio Disney might do much better or much worse in what I projected. I estimated my direct expenses at 60% of my Theme Park revenues and 75% of my resort revenues but that again is based on what I have on the ground right now, and that could be wrong as well. I used a tax rate that could change over time. My risk-free rate could change. My risk premium could change. In fact, every single number in this analysis could be off.

That's probably going to make you uncomfortable, and it should, because you're trying to play guard when you do capital budgeting. You're projecting things into a future that you don't control. And when you feel uncomfortable, let's think about what the possible choices you face here are.

One is you can say, "Well, are you going to wait till the uncertainty goes away before you take this project?" Think about it. If you wait until the uncertainty goes away, how long will you wait? Probably forever.

The second choice you could make is you will not take this investment because there's uncertainty. Yeah, you could try that. But remember risk is danger plus opportunity. If you avoid danger as a company, you will not have opportunity either.

The third is you could be one of those strange people, unusual people who can make judgements in the face of uncertainty and not be phased by it. You say, "I don't care. I'm okay. I've got a net present value I can act on it."

The fourth is maybe you can do something. Something to make yourself more comfortable with the fact that your estimates could be wrong. That is going to be the next phase of what I'm going to talk about is things we can do after we're done to make us feel more comfortable about the fact that there is uncertainty in the future and that all you can do is make your best judgements given the numbers you have at the time of the analysis.

There is not much specific I wanted to do right now, but I want you to think about this whole notion of uncertainty. So much of what we do in corporate finance requires us to make estimates for the future based on incomplete information and things we don't control. You have to be okay making those judgements and more important, you have to be okay being wrong when the numbers turn out to be different.
