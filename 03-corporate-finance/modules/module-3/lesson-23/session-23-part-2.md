# Session 23 Part 2 — Designing Debt for Disney, Vale, Tata Motors, and Baidu

So now that we've talked about the process of designing debt, let's try to apply this to the companies in our lab experiments, Disney, Vale, Tata Motors, and Baidu.

## Designing Debt for Disney by Business

So let's think about applying this to Disney. To think about the right kind of debt for Disney, given that it's in different businesses, it's unlikely that one type of debt is going to fit all of these different businesses. So here's what I tried to do. I took each of Disney's different businesses, tried to think of a typical project in that business, and then ask what type of debt would best fit that project.

In the movie business, for instance, Disney's projects typically tend to be short term. Their cash outflows tend to be in dollars, but the cash inflows would reflect where the movie makes money. And one feature of the movie business is you have a few really big hits. They make hundreds of millions of dollars and lots of small losers. If I were designing debt for the movie business, it would be short term. For Disney, it would probably be primarily U.S. dollar. And if I could, I'd like to tie the coupon rate on the bond to how well the movie does.

If you look at the broadcasting business, the projects will tend to be short-term, but there, the revenues, the earnings, and the cash flows will be driven by the ratings that the show gets. If I could design a debt for the broadcasting business, it would be short-term, and if I could, I'd like to tie the coupon rate to how well the show does, perhaps measured by its ratings.

If you look at the theme park business for Disney, this is Disney's longest-term business. Projects probably last 50, 60, 80, 100 years, and depending on where the project is located, the cash flows can be in U.S. dollars if it's a theme park in the U.S. or in Brazilian reais if the theme park is in Brazil. If I were designing debt for the theme park business it would be really long-term and it probably would be in the currency of the wherever the theme park is located.

Then if you look at the consumer product business, the licensing and the interactive gaming business, the debt will reflect the features of those businesses. For Disney, the licensing business is what drives consumer products. They actually don't produce their own merchandise. They primarily license it. That'll allow them to borrow money on a long-term basis, or at least the medium-term basis to reflect the length of the licenses. The interactive gaming business, if it were a stand-alone business, would be the business I was most likely to issue convertible debt on. Each of these businesses, given their different characteristics, would call for different kinds of debt.

## Disney: Recommended vs. Actual Debt

Let's assume I was able to do this analysis overall for Disney and I came back with the following recommendations based on what I see Disney as a company. I said, "Look, I'd expect your debt to be about medium-term four to five years on average." I'd expect a lot of floating rate debt. Why? Because Disney has pricing power and a lot of foreign currency debt because Disney gets about 18% of its revenues outside the U.S. So I'd expect to see about four to five-year debt, a lot of floating rate debt, and a lot of foreign currency debt.

So let's look at what Disney actually has so we can compare it to what you'd expect Disney to have. Disney's actual debt has a maturity of about 7.92 years on a weighted average basis. That's a little high, but that's not that different on a duration basis from the four to five years that I suggested I should see in Disney's overall debt. So on the maturity standing, I'm okay with Disney's existing debt.

If you look at how much of Disney's debt is in foreign currency, it's only 5.49%. You're saying, "What do you mean only?" Remember that Disney gets 18% of its revenues outside the U.S. At least, at first sight, it looks to me like Disney has too little foreign currency debt.

Disney's floating rate debt is about 5.67% of its overall debt. Think about it. How much you borrow on floating rate debt will depend on how much pricing power you have. Do you think Disney has pricing power? Well, I do. In fact, given its pricing power, I would expect it to have a lot more floating rate debt.

So here's my bottom line. I think Disney's debt is okay in terms of maturity. Perhaps, I'd like it to be a little more short-term, but I like a lot more foreign currency debt and a lot more floating rate debt.

## Two Ways to Adjust the Debt Mix

So think about how we can put this recommendation into practice at Disney. There are two ways you can adjust the debt at Disney, assuming you want to take my recommendations to heart. The first is, you can use swaps and derivatives to take the existing debt and tweak it. What would you do? You'd swap a significant portion of its long-term fixed rate U.S. dollar debt perhaps for shorter-term, floating rate foreign currency debt. Overnight, your problem will go away. The second, and this is a less painful solution if you don't want to do swaps and derivatives, is on the next project you take, the theme park you built in Shanghai, borrow disproportionately large amounts in floating rate foreign currency debt and over time, your debt ratios should start to adjust. So there are two ways in which companies can adjust debt that doesn't match. One is by taking their existing debt and using swaps and derivatives. The other is by adjusting the financing they use on their new projects.

## Bookscape, Vale, Tata Motors, and Baidu

Now, let's try this for our other companies. When I look at Bookscape, Bookscape uses leases as its primary way of borrowing money. In fact, it's the only debt that Bookscape has as a company. That actually makes sense. For a retailer, your biggest debt is tied to your store. That actually is matching up your debt to your assets pretty well. So for Bookscape, I'm going to leave well enough alone. I would expect it to have long-term dollar-denominated debt, and it has leases which effectively reflect the life of its store.

With Vale, I'd expect to see a lot of long-term debt. Why? Because iron-ore mines last a long time. I would expect the debt to be dollar-denominated because your cash flows are going to be in dollars. You sell iron ore into a commodity market that's dollar based. But the costs are in reais. Their actual debt turns out to be predominantly U.S. dollar debt, and very long-term. With Vale, I think we're on the right track. The debt reflects what I'd expect to see.

When you look at Tata Motors, an automobile company, I'd expect the debt to be medium to long-term because a typical project for an auto company is another big assembly plant. I would expect the debt to start shifting more into foreign currency debt, especially as Jaguar Land Rover becomes a larger part of Tata Motors and expect the debt to be fixed rate debt. Automobile companies don't have a lot of pricing power. Perhaps Jaguar does, but Tata Motors does not. If you look at the existing debt at Tata Motors, 71% of debt is Indian rupee debt. The rest is euro debt, about 29%. For the moment, that might be okay, but as Jaguar Land Rover grows to become a larger part of Tata Motors, I would start to expect to see that foreign currency portion become larger. Almost all of the debt is fixed rate debt. Perhaps some floating rate debt would make sense for Jaguar Land Rover. And the average maturity is about 5.33 years.

Finally for Baidu. Baidu has relatively little debt. And if you look at its characteristics, its a company that derives almost all of its revenues in China. It's a technology company. Looking at it just on that basis, I'd expect to see a lot of convertible debt and debt in the local currency. It actually borrows money, primarily in U.S. dollars and euros, about 82% of its debt. It has pretty long-term debt, and a very small portion is floating rate debt. That actually does not match up to what I'd expect to see. Hopefully, they can use derivatives, forwards, futures, and options to hedge themselves against the exchange rate risks they're exposed to, but there's clearly exchange rate risk if you borrow money in a foreign currency and operate primarily in China.

## Bottom Line: Find the Right Financing Mix

So the bottom line is, when you look at your company, there is a right financing mix for your company in terms of duration, in terms of currency, in terms of fixed or floating rate, in terms of straight or convertible, and perhaps even in other features. The key is to find what that right financing is for your company. So you got all the ammunition, all the tools you need for designing the right financing for your company. Go for it. Take your company apart and design a customized bond, a customized debt for your company that reflects the company as it exists. That is a key part of the financing principle.
