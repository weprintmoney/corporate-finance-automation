# Session 25 Part 2 — Good and Bad Reasons for Paying Dividends

So let's look at the third school of thought in dividends. Dividends can be neutral, dividends can be bad, but dividends must be good, at least for some investors, otherwise you wouldn't have so many companies paying dividends. In this part of the session, let's focus on bad reasons and good reasons for paying dividends.

## Bad Reasons for Paying Dividends

Let's look at the two bad reasons companies often give for paying dividends. The first bad reason, and it's often used, is what I call the bird in the hand fallacy. And here's how the argument goes. If you're an investor, you should value dividends more than capital gains because you actually get the dividends whereas capital gains are in the future and you don't know what they will be. That is technically true. But if you think about what happens in the ex-dividend day, you can see why this choice is a false one. If you have a company that pays a dividends, here's what happens. The stock price drops right away. So it's really not a choice between dividends today and capital gains in the future, it's a choice between dividends today and capital gains today. And that burden of hand argument really doesn't hold up.

Here's another bad reason given for paying dividends. We had a really good year last year, we made a lot of money, therefore, we're going to pay dividends. And here's why it's a bad reason. You could have a really good year followed by a really bad year. You can have a year where you don't invest much followed by years where you have to invest a lot. If you're a company that pays out dividends based on just what happened last year, here's what's going to end up happening. You're going to pay a lot of dividends, and the next two or three years, when you have bad earnings or have to reinvest a lot, you're going to have to go out and issue shares. So when you pay dividends, you should look look at not just what happened in the most recent year, but what you expect to happen in the future.

So those are the bad reasons. What are the good reasons for paying dividends? There are three good reasons you could potentially have for paying dividends. The first is, here what's called a clientele effect. The way I like to describe this is you get the stockholders you deserve. The dividend policy you have will attract investors who like that dividend policy. That's your dividend clientele. And if you accumulate a clientele of investors who like dividends, then you should pay dividends. The second is a signaling argument. It's often argued that when a company changes its dividends, it's actually signaling something to the market. And I'm going to give you both the positive signal and the negative signal associated with the company increasing dividends. There is a third reason, it's not a noble one. There are two players in this game, right? There are stockholders and bondholders, bankers who've lent money to the firm and equity investors. When you pay a dividend, you might be transferring wealth from the bondholders or lenders to the equity investors, and here's why. Remember, lenders would prefer you don't pay any dividends at all. After all, they feel safest when you just hold on to the cash. Every time you pay a dividend, you're effectively making them worse off. And to the extent that you can pay out more than they expected you to, you're transferring wealth from those lenders.

## The Clientele Effect

So let's start with the clientele rationale for paying dividends. As I said, when you set dividend policy, you attract investors who like that dividend policy. In fact, this manifests itself in a very simple set of statistics. If you look at the types of investors in different companies, you find very quickly that investors congregate to companies that pay out dividends that they like. Investors with low tax rates, for instance, who have lots of cash flow needs, tend to buy high dividend paying stocks. Investors with higher tax rates who might not need the cash flows right away are more likely to buy stocks that don't pay dividends. In fact, studies have looked at the types of investors in different companies, and here's what they tend to find. Poorer investors, investors with lower tax rates, are the investors most likely to be buying shares in high dividend paying stocks.

Now, if you buy into the clientele effect, it has a consequence. And here's the consequence. Let's say over time you've actually accumulated a clientele that likes what you do. You pay a high dividend, and they like your high dividends. But let's assume circumstances change. You're no longer able to pay those high dividends. Why? Because your business has become riskier or requires more reinvestment. One of the most difficult challenges in dividend policy is changing dividends when your dividend clientele requires you to pay a high dividends. We'll come back and talk about the challenge of making those changes in a later session.

## Dividends as a Signal

Here's a second rationale for paying dividends. Dividends send a signal. In fact, the signal they often send is a positive one. When you increase dividends, you're signaling to the market that you feel pretty secure about future earnings. And there's some evidence to back this up. If you look at what happens when companies announce dividend changes, here's what you observe on average. Companies that announce increases in dividends tend to see a jump in the stock price, not by much, by about 1%. Companies that announce cuts in dividends see a drop in the stock price. And it's actually a much larger drop, about 4.5% to 5%. You're saying, "How come?" Companies are far more likely to increase dividends than decrease dividends, so perhaps the market reflects that. So dividends can send a signal, and that signal can be good news on average. In other words, an increase in dividends can be viewed as positive news and your stock price goes up.

But can increasing dividends be a bad signal? Well, I think they can. It depends on the company. Let's suppose you've invested in a young growth company which you think has lots of projects. And unexpectedly, this company announces it's going to start paying dividends. I would assume you're a little worried, right? Because that announcement would suggest to you that perhaps, perhaps the growth that you expect in this company might not be as high as you expect it to be. Perhaps the projects are not as lucrative as you thought they were. Put differently, there are cases, I mean not uncommon, where an increase in dividends or an initiation of dividends can be viewed as bad news. In fact, there's some evidence that when companies initiate dividends, it's the start of slower growth rather than higher growth, as you can see in this graph. The growth in earnings for a company actually peaks in the quarter in which dividends are initiated, and they level off or become lower in future time periods.

There's one final piece of evidence on dividend increases and decreases that I think is worth looking at. Dividends operate as a signal only if investors don't have much better information, much richer information they can use. Put differently, if you're a company with lots of analysts who can track how well you're doing or how badly you're doing, it is entirely possible that an increase in dividends or decrease in dividends might not have the impact it used to have. There's some evidence that's starting to happen with the U.S. stocks. In this graph, for instance, you look at the effect of dividend increases and decreases over different time periods. And as you can see, as we've gone from the 1960s, to the 1970s, to the 1980s, you can see that dividend changes are stating to have a much smaller impact than they used to. One consequence of having much more information available to us is the effective dividend changes might not be as large as they used to be when we did not have this information.

## Wealth Transfer from Bondholders to Stockholders

There's one final rationale for increasing dividends. By increasing dividends, you could make your bondholders worse off and transfer wealth from them to the stockholders. The best way to actually see this is to look at what happens around those dividend increases we talked about earlier. Remember when dividends are increased that stock prices increase on average? Well, when dividends increase, if you look at bond prices in these same companies, you see the exact opposite effect. A dividend increase is good news for stockholders, stock prices go up. But a dividend increase is bad news for bondholders, and we talked about why. When dividends increase, that's more cash leaving the company, cash that could have been used to service the debt, and as a lender or bondholder, you feel more worried. That's why a lot of bank covenants, lot of lenders, restrict how much you can pay in dividends.

## Summary and Manager Perceptions

So let's summarize. There are two bad reasons for paying dividends. One is the notion that somehow dividends are more certain than capital gains and will give you a higher stock price. The second is that you had a good year, and you're going to use the cash flows or earnings from that good year to pay dividends. There are three good reasons for paying dividends. One is because you have accumulated a clientele who like dividends, the second is you're using dividends to signal that you have solid and predictable earnings, and the third is, by paying more in dividends, you're transferring wealth from lenders to stockholders. But those are all potentially reasons why you see companies continue to pay big segments or big chunks of their earnings as dividends.

So when, for instance, managers agree with the statement that dividend payout ratios affect the price of the stock and 61% say they agree, that doesn't mean that it's right. But essentially, it at least gives you a sense of what manager perceptions are about the different forces that affect dividends.

So now that you know what the trade off is on dividends, you can take a deeper look at why your company might have the dividend policy that it does. If your company has been around a long time and has paid a lot of dividends over time, you've accumulated a clientele of investors who like those dividends, not surprisingly, your company will continue to pay dividends. If your company has never paid dividends, odds are it has investors who like that policy and don't be surprised to see it continue not to pay dividends. Dividend changes though, can give you information about a company, but whether that information is good news or bad news will depend on the company.
