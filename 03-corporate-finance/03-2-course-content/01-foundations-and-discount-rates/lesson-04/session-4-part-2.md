# Session 4 Part 2 — The Capital Asset Pricing Model: Risk, Diversification, and Beta

So now that we've laid the foundations for what goes to good risk and return model and at least described the capital asset pricing model in very general terms, let's take a closer look at the capital asset pricing model as well as alternatives to the model. As I mentioned in the last part of the last session, the capital asset pricing model is built on the presumption that the measure of risk is best captured by the variants of actual returns around an expected return.

## Defining Risk as Variance: Four Investment Examples

You say, "What are you talking about?" Let me give you examples of four investments to illustrate exactly what I mean.

Let's assume that you have a one-year time horizon and you invest in a one-year Treasury bill. That's a U.S. government Treasury bill. Let's assume the U.S. government has no default risk. At the end of the year, I come and knock on your door and ask you, "What did you make on that one-year T-bill?" The return you'd have made would have been defined upfront as a guaranteed return. That is a truly riskless investment because the actual return is always equal to the expected return. What you are promised up front is what you get delivered. That's a riskless investment.

Let's move one step up the ladder. Let's say you still have a 1-year time horizon, but you buy a 10-year government bond. Let's still assume the government has no default risk. If at the end of the year, I came and asked you, "What did you make on your 10-year government bond?" A portion of your return is guaranteed, right? The coupon. But a portion is not. The price of the bond could have changed if interest rates had gone up or down. So a 10-year government bond is not risk-free if you're looking at a 1-year time horizon because there's variants in your actual returns around an expected return.

Take a third example. Let's assume you buy stock in Procter & Gamble. Procter & Gamble is a mature consumer product company. Let's say you do it because you expect to make a return of 8%. Not unrealistic, that's a pretty reasonable return. One year from now, I come and knock on your door, I say, "What did you make on that stock?" If you were lucky, you might have made 18%, 20%, or 25%. The stock would have done better than expected. If you are not, you might have made 2% or even -5%, worse than expected. That stock is riskier than the government bond, which was riskier than the T-bill because the actual returns are greater than the expected return in some cases and lower in others. But there's variants.

Let's do one final example. Let's suppose you buy shares in Tesla. Why? Because you expect to make a 15% return. It's a risky investment. A year from now, I come and knock on your door and say, "What did you make?" If you were lucky, you might have doubled your money, as Tesla stockholders often have between 2012 and 2016. If you were not, you could have lost half your money. Again, the returns are different from your expected return. And in this case, they're different by more than they were in Procter & Gamble, which in turn was more risky than the government bond.

In every one of these cases, notice when I talk about variants, I'm talking about variants in future returns, not variants in past returns. There is no risk in the past. I know that's stating the obvious. But the reason I state that is often in finance when we are asked to measure risk, the way we do it is we look at past returns, past returns of Procter & Gamble, past returns of Tesla. We do it because that's where the data is. But we cannot lose sight of the fact that our mission in risk measurement is to look into the future.

## Climbing the Risk Ladder: From Firm-Specific to Market Risk

The first step in the CAPM, at least, we defined risk as variants. Now, comes the second step. And this to me is perhaps the most critical step of the model. When you invest in a stock, let's take Disney, you're exposed to risk from all kinds of actions. Every new movie that Disney releases could do much better than expected or much worse than expected. That's risk you're exposed to as a Disney investor, but it's risk that is specific to Disney. In what sense? If a Disney movie does badly, Disney will be affected, but no other companies.

Disney also owns a piece of ESPN. It actually owns all of ESPN and ABC. That's the entertainment business. To the extent that there's something that affects the entire entertainment business, let's say Amazon and Netflix disrupting it, Disney is going to be affected and so are other entertainment companies. But only they are going to be affected, maybe 8, 10, 15 companies. We've gone from risk that affects 1 company to risk that affects 10 companies.

Let's keep climbing the ladder. To the extent that the U.S. dollar gets stronger or weaker, Disney is going to be affected because it has foreign operations, but so will 1,000 other companies because they all have foreign operations. You've gone from risk that affects 1 company to 10 companies to 1,000 companies. And let's take one final step. If interest rates in the U.S. go up or inflation increases, the economy strengthens, Disney is going to be affected, but so is every other company in the market. We have gone from risk that affects one company, just Disney, to risk that affects all companies.

## Diversification and the Marginal Investor

You think, "So what?" If you put all your money into Disney, you're exposed to all these risks, right? But let's say that you own Disney and 50 other companies. Here's what's going to happen. Risk that affects one or a few companies is going to get averaged out. It's not rocket science. It's the law of large numbers. For every company, when something bad happens, something worse than expected on an individual project, there will be another company where something better than expected happens. So those risks will average out. It's only the risks that cannot get averaged out, the risks that affects most or all companies that will stay in your portfolio.

And here's where we make a big assumption that drives how we measure risk in almost every risk and return model in finance. We bring in the marginal investor. You say, "What's the marginal investor?" The marginal investor in a company is an investor who meets two criteria, he or she owns a lot of shares and trades those shares. Why? Because at the margin, those are the investors who affect prices the most. In corporate finance, and in fact, all of finance, we make the assumption that the marginal investor in a company is well-diversified. If a marginal investor is diversified, here's what's going to happen. The risk that they're going to care about is the risk you cannot diversify away, the risk that affects most or all companies. And only that risk will be captured in your risk measure and brought into your expected returns.

## The CAPM's Additional Assumptions and the Market Portfolio

Now, once you make that leap of assuming that diversification reduces your exposure to risk, why stop? And in the CAPM, we make two additional assumptions. The first is there are no transactions costs, and the second is you have no private information, no way of telling which stocks are cheap and which are expensive. If I have those two assumptions, I've taken away the two reasons why investors stop diversifying, right? Because if you think about it, the reason most of us end up with only 30 or 40, or 50 stocks in our portfolio, even if we're large investors is, A, because there's a transactions cost and, B, because we think we can pick the right stocks. If I take away those limiting assumptions, here's what happens. Each of us will keep diversifying. Until when? Until we have a small piece of every single traded asset in the market in our portfolio.

You're saying, "That's completely unrealistic." You're right. But let's take it to its logical limit. If every one of us ends up with this market portfolio, a small piece of every traded asset in the world, the way we think about risk is going to change. You're saying, "But if we all hold the same portfolio, think of it as a gigantic index fund, what'll set me apart from my neighbor who doesn't like to take any risk at all?" The way in the CAPM we reflect our risk aversion, or how much risk we want to take, is in our determination of how much of our money we put into this market portfolio, a portfolio of every single traded risky asset in the world and the riskless asset.

So let's say your neighbor hates to take any risk. He's going to put his entire wealth into the riskless asset. You like to take some risk, you might put half your money into the riskless asset and half into this market portfolio. Let's say your neighbor to the other side wants to take even more risk. He's going to put all of his money into the market portfolio. But let's say the neighbor next to him is willing to take even more risk. How is she going to take that risk? Here's what she will do in the CAPM. She will go out and borrow money and buy the same market portfolio. In effect, every single investor in the world is going to end up holding just the market portfolio or the riskless asset in different proportions.

## From Market Portfolio to Beta and Expected Return

Once you make that leap, measuring the risk of an individual asset then becomes simple. The risk of an individual asset then becomes the risk that it adds to the market portfolio. You're saying, "Well, how will I capture that?" Simply, you do it statistically. When you have a stock or an asset that moves with the market, it will add more risk to the market portfolio than if you have a stock or asset that doesn't move as much with the market. Statistically, this is captured with a correlation, the correlation between your stock and the market, and that correlation becomes the basis for computing a beta. A beta measures how much risk an asset will add to the market portfolio. Beta is not a measure of total risk. It's a measure of risk added to a portfolio. And that's the beta that we're going to use to come up with an expected return in the capital asset pricing model.

So the expected return becomes the riskless rate plus the beta times the equity risk premium, and those will become the basis for the calculations we will do. So think about it. In the CAPM, we defined risk as variants. We said that only the portion of variants you cannot diversify away gets rewarded. We measured that portion of variants with a beta, and we've converted the beta into an expected return. It's a neat, tightly-constructed model. But we'll come back and ask, "Does it really work?"
