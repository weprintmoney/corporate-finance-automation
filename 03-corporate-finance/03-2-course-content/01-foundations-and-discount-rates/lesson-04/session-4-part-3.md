# Session 4 Part 3 — Alternatives to the CAPM and the Marginal Investor Assumption

Having looked at the steps to derive the capital asset pricing model, now let's ask a follow-up question. What are the alternatives to the model? Should we stay with this model? How well does it work?

To answer that question, let's go back and look at the first two steps for deriving the capital asset pricing model. The first step, we defined risk as variants, variants of actual returns around an expected return. Right? In the second step, we said only that portion of the variants that cannot be diversified away will get rewarded. Why? Because a marginal investor is assumed to be well-diversified. Every single risk and return model in finance shares those first two steps. It's in the third step that you get deviations from the capital asset pricing model.

## From the CAPM to the Arbitrage Pricing Model and Multifactor Models

The capital asset pricing model was derived in 1964, and for about 14 years, was the only game in town. In 1978, a professor at Yale, Steve Ross, said, "Why are we trying to capture all of this market risk, risk we're exposed to, this is macroeconomic risk, in one number, the beta? Why don't we allow for multiple sources of market risk and a beta against each one?" Initially, people's response was, "That's nice, but how will we know?" He said, "That's easy. Give me about 50 years of stock return data and I will tell you." And that's exactly what he did. He took 50 years of stock return data, and based on the data, he came back with a conclusion. He said, "Looking at these 50 years, there are 5 market risk factors and here are the betas." You say, "How do you find that?" Remember we defined market risk as the risk that affects most or all stocks at the same time? He sent the computer on a search mission. He said, "Look through the data, find common patterns." The computer found five common patterns, hence five factors. And once it found those five common patterns, it was able to estimate the betas against each one. Steve Ross had uncovered the arbitrage pricing model, the first serious competitor to the CAPM.

The problem with the arbitrage pricing model, though, is the factors were statistical factors. They had no names attached to them, and there was no way a company would decide to estimate a hurdle rate if its factors were called factor one, factor two, factor three, factor four, and factor five. So in the years following, people tried to put names on the factors, not randomly, but by looking at the data. When you put macroeconomic names...and it has to be macroeconomic because these are market risk factors, names like the level of interest rates, the slope of the yield curve, inflation rates, GDP growth, you've effectively gone from arbitrage pricing models to multifactor models. By the mid-'80s, we had a bunch of multifactor models operating as competitors to the CAPM. None of them caught on either, and here's why. The factors that you identify tend to be unstable. In other words, you look to the 1970s, oil prices were a big factor driving stock prices. By the 1980s, they weren't. So we've gone from the CAPM to the arbitrage pricing model to the multifactor model, but the CAPM stays standing because the alternative models haven't displaced it.

## Proxy Models: Fama and French

In the early 1990s, two professors at the University of Chicago, Gene Fama and Ken French, said, "Why are we even trying to estimate how much risk there is in a stock and come up with a risk and return model? Why don't we let the market tell us what's risky and what's safe?" You're saying, "How will the market tell you?" They looked back at about 30 years of data and they looked to see what kinds of companies, what kinds of stocks earned high returns and what kinds of stocks earned low returns. They found, for instance, that small market cap companies earned higher returns than large market cap companies, and they made a leap of faith. They said, "That must mean small market cap companies are riskier than large market cap companies." They're trusting markets to tell them what's risky by looking at the actual returns. They developed what were called proxy models. And the first two proxies they came up with were market capitalization and a price-to-book ratio. Small market cap companies were riskier than large market cap companies. Why? Because they earned higher returns. Low price-to-book companies were riskier than high price-to-book companies for the same reason. In the years following, there have been other proxies that have been added to the list, but proxy models are your fourth choice, the CAPM, arbitrage pricing model, multifactor models, or proxy models.

## Why the CAPM Still Stands

Now, guess which model still stays standing? In most consulting firms and most banks, if you look at which model is used to come up with hurdle rates, it remains the CAPM. And that is surprising given the limitation of the CAPM and their legion.

Let's start with the first one. The model makes unrealistic assumptions. Does it? Absolutely. No transactions cost, no private information. Clearly, it makes unrealistic assumptions. That by itself, though, wouldn't stop me. Because after all, given a choice between making unrealistic assumptions and ending up with a model that you can use or making realistic assumptions and ending up with a model you cannot use, I would take the former over the latter every single time.

The second critique of the CAPM is that the estimates could be wrong. Your beta could be wrong, your risk-free rate could be wrong, your risk premium could be wrong, to which my response is, "So what?" Any risk and return model, you're going to be subject to that problem.

It's the third critique that cuts to the bone. Remember the Fama-French proxy model I talked about? They did look at the CAPM and they asked a very simple question, "What proportion of the variation in returns across stocks is explained by differences in betas?" You're saying, "Why does that matter?" If the CAPM is the right model for risk, 100% of the differences in returns across stocks across very long time periods should be explained by betas. The actual number is well below 10%. Betas don't explain more than 90% of the variation in returns across stocks, and this is the model we continue to use to come up with expected returns. You're shocked, right? You're saying, "How come?"

Well, here's the reason the CAPM persists. It is true that the CAPM makes lots of mistakes. It's not a very good model. But it's also true that the alternative models we've come up with, the arbitrage pricing model, the multifactor model, even though they do better at explaining past returns, don't do that much better a job at predicting future returns. And in corporate finance, it's all about predicting future returns. So at least for the moment, I'm going to stick with the CAPM, with its simplicity. I'm going to actually argue that there are ways of estimating parameters for the CAPM that will make it a better-performing model. But I'm going to stick with it not because I believe in betas passionately, but because it does at least as good a job as the next best model, it's a lot simpler to work with, and a lot more intuitive.

## Is the Marginal Investor Diversified?

Now, if you look at the marginal investor assumption, it's a key assumption in all of these risk and return models, right? So while we're arguing about the CAPM versus the arbitrage pricing model or the multifactor models, remember they all share a common assumption. They assume that the marginal investor is well-diversified. And if that assumption fails, then every single one of these models will also fail. So one of the final questions I want to ask in this session is I want to ask, "Is that a good assumption to make?"

And let me turn to Disney. Here's what I did. I looked at the same top 17 stockholders I had in previous parts of this class, but here I asked a different question, "Am I on safe ground assuming that the marginal investor in Disney is a diversified investor?" Remember, there are two criteria that you have to meet for an investor to be diversified. The investor needs to own a lot of shares and trade those shares. If you look at the top 17 investors, the marginal investor in Disney is likely to be a large institutional investor, a State Street, a Fidelity, a Barclays. And I think we're on pretty safe ground assuming that that investor is diversified.

Now, just to give you a sense of when you might run into trouble, what if I gave you a company like Microsoft or Oracle, where the largest investor is an individual? In the case of Microsoft, it might be Bill Gates. In the case of Oracle, it might be Larry Ellison. I'm going to argue that even in those companies, the marginal investor is a diversified institutional investor, and here's why. Bill Gates and Larry Ellison might own a lot of shares in their companies, but they don't trade their shares. To be a marginal investor, you need both criteria to be met.

In fact, in all of my publicly-traded companies, I think I'm on pretty safe ground, though not perhaps as safe as I was in Disney. In the case of Deutsche, Vale, Tata Motors, and Baidu, the marginal investors are all diversified institutional investors, partly because these are large market cap companies. And because the marginal investor is diversified, I'm on safe ground assuming that the CAPM or arbitrage pricing model or multifactor model is a reasonable model because I'm looking at only the risk you cannot diversify away.

So think about who the marginal investor in your firm is. Take a look at the top 17 investors and once you've taken a look, here's what I'd like you to do. Make a judgement. Make a judgement on whether the marginal investor in your company is a diversified investor. If the answer is yes, you're on solid ground. You can move on. If the answer is no, you might have to do some adaptation, adjusting of your expected return from any of the risk and return models in finance before you can come up with a hurdle rate. But more on that later.
