# Session 8 Part 2 — Interpreting the Regression Beta

## The Intercept: Jensen's Alpha

So now that you've run a regression of returns in the stock against returns in a market index, you think, "What do I do with this? What does it tell me about a company?" To answer that question, let me use the Disney regression to illustrate the after-effects.

When I ran the regression on Disney, I got an intercept. And if you remember, the intercept was 0.0071. Since I'm working in percentage terms, that becomes 0.71%. You think, "Is that a good number, is that a bad number?" Remember, to make a judgment on whether Disney was a good or a bad investment, I need to compare that number to the risk-free rate times one minus beta.

A little mechanical detail here. Because I ran my regression using monthly returns, my intercept is a monthly intercept. You think, "So what?" The risk-free rate I should be computing should be a monthly risk-free rate. And to compute the monthly risk-free rate, here's what I did. I took the average T-bill rate by month for the 59 months. You think, "Why the T-bill rate?" Because I'm looking at one-month holding periods. I took that average T-bill rate, which is an annualized rate, because that's how rates get reported and divided by 12. Basically, I'm using a simple way of coming up with a monthly number. That number is 0.04%. On a monthly basis, my monthly risk-free rate is 0.04%.

I multiply that 0.04% by one minus beta. You think, "How would I know what the beta is?" Remember, the slope of the regression was 1.25. I used that as my beta. I end up with a risk-free rate times one minus beta of minus 0.01%. You think, "What does that tell me?" If Disney had done exactly as predicted during the period of this regression, that's what their intercept should have been, minus 0.01%. What its actual intercept was, was 0.71%. You take the difference between those two numbers. You get 0.72%. Disney did 0.72% better than expected on a monthly basis between 2008 and 2013.

Now, if that doesn't impress you, you think, "0.72%? That doesn't sound like much." Remember, it's a monthly number. If I wanted to tell you on an annual basis how well Disney did, the shortcut I could take is multiply the 0.72% by 12. That gives me about 8.52%. But this is a compounded return. Money earns money. So if I took the compounded value, which is 1 plus that 0.72% raised to the power of 12, because you're compounding, the return I get for Disney has an excess return, a Jensen's alpha, is 9.02%. Between 2008 and 2013, Disney delivered whatever you'd have expected it to deliver given what the market did and given what its beta was, plus 9.02% a year.

I'll give you a simple analogy to bring this home. Let's assume Disney had a beta of 1.25, and the market was up 20%. Let's ignore the risk-free rate for the moment. If your stock has a beta of 1.25 and the market is up 20%, your stock needs to be up 25% to break even. Here's what Disney did. They delivered that 25% plus 9.02% each year. This is a risk-adjusted, market-adjusted measure of performance, and at least over this period, Disney did well as an investment.

Now, does this mean we should be rewarding Disney's management with bonuses and options because they did well? Well, maybe and maybe not, and here's why. When Disney has a positive Jensen's alpha, it did well. It could have been because of things that Disney did as a company. It could also have been because they got lucky. They were in a sector that did well over that period. So to make that judgment for Disney, here's what I did. I computed the Jensen's alpha for all entertainment companies. And the average across all entertainment companies was about 1%. Disney earned 9% more than expected. I would argue that Disney's performance has to be more than just the sector they are in, that at least some of it has to be due to choices and decisions that managers at Disney made.

## The Slope: Beta and Its Standard Error

So that's the intercept. Let's move on to the slope. If you remember, the slope of the line was 1.25, and the standard error in that estimate was 0.10. Let's see how we'll use those two numbers. If you asked me for a point estimate, a single number for Disney's beta, the answer is simple, it's 1.25. If you asked me to give you a range with about 67% confidence, which is not a whole lot of confidence because you're going to be wrong one-third of the time, I'm going to add and subtract one standard error. If I add 0.10 to 1.25, I get 1.35. If I subtract 0.10, I get 1.15. With 67% confidence, Disney's true beta could be anywhere from 1.15 to 1.35. If I increase that confidence interval to 95%, I need to be more confident, I'm going to add and subtract two standard errors. The true beta for Disney could be anywhere from 1.05 to 1.45. The next time somebody tells you the beta for a company, remember, it's not a fact, it's an estimate. And that estimate could come with a pretty wide range on it.

So we've talked about the slope, we've talked about the intercept, but remember, that standard error that you saw on Disney is not unique to Disney alone. In fact, there are lots of companies with standard errors much wider than 0.10. I'll give you one example before we move on. I estimated beta for GoPro, the young camera company, the digital camera company. The beta that I got was two. The standard error on that estimate was close to one. Think about the range I can give you on that beta. So betas are estimates which come with standard errors. Standard errors are features, not bugs. They're part of the process, we've got to learn to deal with them.

## The R-Squared: Market vs. Firm-Specific Risk

The third and final output for the regression, of course, is the R-squared. The R-squared for Disney was 73%. What does that tell me? To the extent that this regression was run against the S&P 500, 73% of the risk in Disney comes from the market, 27% is firm-specific. If you're not a diversified investor, of course, you don't care. You're exposed to all of that risk. If you're a diversified investor, here's what's going to happen. The 27% that's firm-specific will get diversified away. It's only the remaining 73% that's going to become part of your expected return and your hurdle rate.

## Beta Services: Bloomberg

Finally, in recent years, you haven't had to run regressions to get betas. You can use a service. This is actually one of my favorite services, it's Bloomberg. To do this, you need a Bloomberg terminal somewhere close by. You have to find your company, you have to type in the word "beta," and this is the page that will pop up. You think, "How do I use this page?" This page actually has a regression that we just ran to the left. You see the regression, the scatterplot? And to the right, you see the output. The raw beta is actually the regression beta. This is using the exact same time period over which I ran the regression, so let's compare numbers. I came up with a regression beta of 1.25 running my own regression. Bloomberg seems to agree with me, 1.247, rounded up, 1.25. The intercept that Bloomberg reports from this regression is 0.60%. If you remember, my intercept was 0.71%. One reason for the difference is, Bloomberg runs its regression using only price returns. It does not incorporate dividends, so that explains the small difference in the intercept and the Jensen's alpha. The R-squared is 73%. That matches my number. Put differently, I feel okay about my regression and I also feel okay about the service that I'm using because the numbers match up pretty closely, because I'm looking at the same time period and the same market index.

One final point that I want to make about these Bloomberg regressions. Notice that adjusted beta. You think, "What is that?" That's actually just a raw beta moved towards one. What Bloomberg does is it takes two-thirds times your raw beta plus one-third times one. You think, "Why two-thirds and one-third?" No good reason. It just moves the regression beta towards one and it's trying to be helpful. So beta services report betas but they do exactly what I suggested, run an aggression, take the slope of the line, and report a beta for the company.

## From Beta to Expected Return and Cost of Equity

Let's bring it all together. November of 2013, let's suppose I wanted to bring these numbers together into an expected return. Here's what my numbers look like. The beta for my regression is 1.25. The risk-free rate was the U.S. 10-year T-bond rate as of that time, 2.75%. The equity risk premium is 5.76% based on where Disney does its business, not just the U.S. equity risk premium. The risk-free rate, 2.75% plus beta 1.25, multiplied by the risk premium, gives me an expected return for Disney of 9.95%.

You're probably saying, "What does that even mean?" If you are an investor in Disney, here is the way you can read the 9.95%. Is the return you can expect to make in the long-term on Disney, assuming the stock is correctly priced. So if you are a passive investor, you believe markets are efficient, that becomes your expected return. If you're a stock picker, this becomes the return you need to make to break even on Disney. So when you go out and do research on Disney, collect your data on Disney, do your own research. If you conclude that you can make 12.5% return on Disney investing in the stock, that makes it a good stock. You're making more than your required rate of return. Put differently, this becomes the basis for both passive and active investing, and it's going to become the basis for how we think about the hurdle rate in capital budget because of investors. Equity investors collectively need to make 9.95% to break even investing in Disney.

Think about what this makes it to managers at Disney. If you're the CFO of Disney, the 9.95% becomes the return you need to make, not even to keep investors happy, but just to break even. That's your cost of equity. One question you might ask, "What if I don't deliver it?" Your stock price will drop, and if it drops enough, perhaps you'll lose your job. You can see very quickly why corporate governance and this cost of equity view of the world are connected. If you're not afraid of your stockholders, you won't view this as your cost of equity. But if you are, you now have your cost of equity.

## Assignment: Run It On Your Company

So here is what I'd like you to do if you run a regression beta on your company or you have access to a Bloomberg terminal and can print off that page for your company. Take you through the process, was your stock a good or a bad investment during the period of your regression? Look at the intercept. What's the risk of your stock? Measure the beta. What's the range on that beta? Look at the standard error. And finally, look at the R-squared to get a sense of where the risk in your company comes from.
