# Session 10 Part 1 — Bottom-Up Betas

In the last session, we talked about the determinants of betas. Specifically, we said that the beta for a company is determined by three actions that the company takes. The first is what kind of product or service it provides. The second is fixed cost. And the third is how much it borrows. But at this stage in the process, you're probably wondering, "Well, how will that give me an actual number for a beta?" Well, in this session, we're going to fill in the blanks.

## Betas as Weighted Averages

So, let's talk about an alternative way of estimating betas. A way that does not depend on a single regression, one slice of history, to give you the beta of a company. I called the beta that I'm going to come up with a bottom-up beta. But give it whatever name you want because here's what we're going to try to do. We're going to draw on a property that betas have, that make them very easy to work with. Betas are always weighted averages.

You're saying, "What does it even mean?" If you run a mutual fund and you have 50 different stocks in your mutual fund, the beta of your mutual fund will be a weighted average of the betas of 50 stocks in the fund, weighted by how much money you have in each stock. If you think about a beta for a company, the beta for a company that's in many businesses will actually be a weighted average of the betas of the individual businesses that the company is in. So, we take a company like GE, in 26 different businesses. If you can estimate the betas for each of the 26 businesses, the beta for GE will be a weighted average of the betas for those 26 businesses. The weights will reflect the values of each business. This is the approach we're going to use to break open the tyranny of a regression beta.

## The Bottom-Up Beta Sequence

So, let's go through the sequence that you have to go through to estimate the bottom-up beta for a company. And to illustrate this process, let's assume you have a hypothetical company. In your hypothetical company, you tell me what businesses you're in. So, let's play a game. Let's assume your company's in two businesses, steel and chemicals.

Here's what I'm going to do. I'm going to find as many publicly traded steel companies as I can, and as many publicly traded chemical companies as I can. Let's say I find 100 steel companies and 100 chemical companies. You're saying, "Why publicly traded?" Because I can look up the regression betas for each of these companies. I now have 100 regression betas for steel companies and a 100 regression betas for chemical companies.

Here's what I'm going to do next. I'm going to take the average beta across steel companies and the average beta across chemical companies. No game playing, just a simple average. I'm also going to take an average debt to equity ratio across steel companies and an average debt to equity ratio across chemical companies. You're saying, "Why?" Because steel businesses and chemical businesses might have very different policies on how much to borrow than your company does. I'm going to unlever the beta. If you're wondering what that is. Remember in the last session how we took Disney's regression beta and took out the effective debt? That's what we're doing. We're taking out the effective debt and we're going to end up with an unlevered beta for being in the steel business and an unlevered beta for being in the chemical business.

We're almost done. I'm going to come back to you and ask you how much value you get from each business? Your first reaction might be, "I don't know how much value I get, but I can tell you how much revenues I get." So good. That's a good starting point. Give me those numbers. You tell me you get 60% of your revenues from steel, 40% from chemicals. I take a weighted average of the unlevered betas. I now have an unlevered beta for your company. What am I going to do with this unlevered beta? I'm going to ask you a final question. I'm going to ask you, "Do you have any debt?" If you have no debt, I'm done. This is now the beta for your equity. If you do have debt, I'm going to compute a debt to equity ratio, lever the beta, and remember that's going to push up the beta and that's going to become the beta of equity for your company.

## Why Bottom-Up Beats Regression

Now, think of why I'm doing this. I'm doing this because I don't like regression betas, right? But think about where I got the 100 steel company betas and the 100 chemical company betas. I got them from regressions. All I've done is replaced a single regression beta with an average of 100. You're saying, "What's the big deal? How does this help me?"

First, remember the law of large numbers. While each regression beta is noisy, has a big standard error, the average of 100 betas will be much more precise. In fact, it'll be roughly 10 times more precise. Why 10 times? The square root of 100. That's the number of companies you had in your sample. I get a more precise beta when I do a bottom-up beta.

Here's a second advantage. If you entered the chemical business yesterday, there is no chance that running a regression beta on your company is going to give me a true beta for your company because you entered the chemical business too recently. But when I do a bottom-up beta, I set the weights. I can reflect the company you are today. In fact, I can even be proactive. If you plan to be in the technology business tomorrow, I can bring that into your beta calculation. A bottom-up beta is more precise, more forward-looking, and more dynamic than a regression beta.

## Applying It to Disney

So, what we're going to do is use this approach to estimate a beta for Disney. So, let me at least set up the table. Disney is a company in five different businesses, and I've listed them out here. Now, I didn't have to do any research to find these five businesses. This is how they broke themselves down in their most recent annual report.

The first and biggest business for them is Media Networks. That includes ABC. It also includes ESPN. The second is the Theme Parks and Resorts: Disneyland, Disney World, Euro Disney, Tokyo Disney. And in November of 2013, this is when I did this, Hong Kong Disney had just opened but wasn't contributing much. Then there's Studio Entertainment. This is Disney's movie business, not just for animated movies, but Pixar, Marvel, and at that time, Lucasfilms had just been bought but hadn't really become part of the company, in terms of providing any revenues. Then your consumer products. These are the toys, the merchandise that Disney produces. It's mostly a licensing business now. And finally, Disney has a fifth business that it calls Interactive. This is a gaming business. This is a business that is supposed to create the apps for the future, the digital products that Disney hopes to make money on.

Five different businesses, and Disney has been helpful in providing me details on each of these five businesses. They break these businesses down not just by revenues, but also with operating income, depreciation and amortization, EBITDA, and even assets. This is the raw data that I'm going to use to come up with a bottom-up beta for Disney. But it's helpful that Disney has provided the breakdown of the businesses on multiple dimensions, and they will all come in useful.

## Starting Point for Your Company

So, if you have a multi-business company, here's the first thing I'd like you to do. I'd like you to find out what businesses your company is in, and start breaking the businesses down. Look on the annual report. See what they break the businesses down by. If you're lucky, they might do what Disney does and break their businesses down on multiple dimensions. If you're not, all they might give you is revenues by business. Take what you can.
