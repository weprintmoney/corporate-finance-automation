# Session 8 Part 1 — Estimating Regression Betas

So now that we've talked about risk-free rates and equity risk premiums, let's turn to the final parameter that you will need to convert these numbers into an expected return, a beta, a measure of relative risk.

## The Regression Approach and What Its Outputs Mean

Now, if you've taken a finance class, you were probably taught how to estimate betas the same way that I was. I'll tell you how I was taught betas. I was asked to run a regression, a regression of returns on the stock against returns in a market index. The slope of the line, I was told, was the beta. A good place to start, but remember, it's a regression. There are two other pieces of output from that regression, I'm going to argue, that provide you useful information about the company.

Notice that when you run a simple regression, you get an intercept? I'm going to argue that the intercept in this particular regression is going to tell you whether your stock was a good investment during the period of your regression. Key word is "was." It's not going to tell you whether your stock will be, but it was. So the intercept tells you something about the performance of your stock during the period of your regression. Was it a good investment or a bad investment?

The final piece of output from the regression, outside of the intercept and the slope, is the R squared. Now, in statistics, remember what the R squared measures. It measures the proportion of your dependent variable that is explained by your independent variable, right? You're saying, "So what?" In this particular regression, think of what your dependent variable is. It's a stock. Your independent variable is the market. When I tell you the R squared is 25%, I'm telling you 25% of the risk of your stock is explained by the market. Remember in the risk and return models we talked about, risk that you can diversify away and risk you cannot? The 25% is the portion of your risk you cannot diversify away, so by definition, the remaining 75% is diversifiable.

## Jensen's Alpha: The Intercept as a Performance Measure

Enough of abstractions. Let's talk a little bit about how the intercept tells you something about performance. Now, this may seem complicated, but hang in there with me. When you run a regression, you get the expected return on the stock is equal to the intercept, plus the slope times the return on the market, right? When you look at the CAPM, though, the return on a stock is the risk-free rate, plus beta times the return on the market minus the risk-free rate. Trust me on this one, but if you do the algebra, you can rewrite the CAPM as follows. The expected return on a stock can be written as the risk-free rate times one minus beta, plus the beta times Rm.

Compare that equation to the equation we just got from the regression. If your regression was over the same period, the second term cancels out, beta times return on the market, and if your stock did exactly as you predicted it would do during the period of the regression, here's what you should expect to see. The intercept you should get from your regression should be roughly equal to the risk-free rate, times one minus beta. Sounds like an abstraction, but it's actually a good measure of performance.

Incidentally, if your stock did much better than expected, you should expect to see an intercept that's much more positive, much larger than the risk-free rate, times one minus beta. And if your stock did much worse than expected, your intercept should be much less than the risk-free rate, times one minus beta. In fact, if you simplified this and took the difference between the intercept and the risk-free rate, times one minus beta, that's called Jensen's Alpha. It's named after Michael Jensen, who used this approach in the late 1960s to answer the question, "Do mutual funds beat the market?" And the answers he found then, has been reaffirmed since, is they don't. Risk-free rate, times one minus beta is a measure of performance. It's a Jensen's Alpha for your stock.

## The Four Choices Before Running the Regression

So, you ready? Let's try to run this on Disney. I'm going to try to run a regression, but before I do that, I have to make some choices. Here are the four choices I need to make.

The first is, I have to decide how far back in time I want to go to run this regression. Disney has been a publicly traded stock since the 1950s. I could go back all the way to the 1950s and that's good, right? I have more data. If this were a statistics class, you'd probably affirm that. But here's why you might not want to do it. My job is to estimate a beta for Disney going forward. And in the 1950s, Disney was a very different company than the company you see today. So here's the trade-off in terms of how far back you want to go. If you go back too far, you end up with a company that doesn't look like your company today. If you don't go back far enough, you don't have enough data. Most beta estimation services use between two and five years, and I'm going to use five years as my regression period for Disney because I think that that five-year period gives you a much better estimate of beta.

So the first choice you have to make when you run a regression to get a beta is how far back to go. The second choice I have to make is, once I've decide to go back 2 years, or 5 years, or 10 years, I have to decide how I'm going to slice up the data. Let me explain. Let's suppose I decide to go back five years. I could look at five years of daily returns. That's going to give me about 1,250 observations. I can look at 5 years of weekly returns, that's 260 observations. Five years of monthly returns, 60 observations. And 5 years of quarterly returns, which will give you 20 observations. Again, you might think, "More is better than less, and therefore go with the daily data." But be careful. Here's the trade-off. When you run regressions with daily data, you have a lot more noise in your returns, partly because stocks don't always trade towards the end of each trading day. So most services go between weekly and monthly returns. So if you go with five years of data, you could go with monthly returns, but if you have only two years of data, you might have no choice but to go with weekly returns.

How far back do you go? How do you slice up the data? Third choice you have to make is you have to estimate returns for your stock. Now, there are two ways you make money when you invest in stocks. One is from price appreciation, the other is from dividends. There are some services that only use price appreciation, just the price appreciation portion for returns, but in reality, you should be including dividends in your returns, as well. But here's the catch. You don't get dividends every day or every week, or even every month. You get them probably once every three months for most companies, perhaps once every six, and in some European companies, once every year. Don't try to be fair and spread the dividends out across the months, put it only in the month in which the stock goes ex-dividend. That is the month by which you have to buy the stock to get the dividend, so you have to get returns on your stock.

And here's your final choice. You have to pick an index to run a regression against. Just in the U.S., there are 120 different market indices. You might have heard of a few, the Dow 30. People talk about the market, they still talk about the Dow 30. You got the S&P 500, the NYSE Composite, the NASDAQ. You have a wide array of choices. You're saying, "How do I decide which one to pick?" because you can get very different betas against each index. Here's the tiebreaker. Remember, this is in the context of the CAPM, right? And in the CAPM, remember what we all held in our portfolios? We held the market portfolio, which included every single traded asset in the market. The closer you get to their ideal, the better your beta, right? That should already give you a way of deciding whether you should use the Dow 30 or the S&P 500. The Dow 30 might be widely talked about, has been around a long time, but it's only 30 companies. The S&P 500 is only 500 companies, but they're in market cap terms. That's about 70% of all equities. There's a reason why services latch on to the S&P 500 for U.S. companies.

## Running the Regression on Disney

So here are my choices for Disney. I decide to go back five years. I decide to use monthly returns. I decided to use the S&P 500, albeit reluctantly, and here's why. Disney is a U.S. company, but there's no reason why I need to be U.S.-focused. I could have run this regression against a global index, but in this case, I'm going to stay with the S&P 500, and then I computed returns for Disney and the S&P 500 every month for 60 months.

What does that mean? I'll give you an example. In November of 2009, Disney started the month at $30.22. It ended the month at $32.25, price appreciation of $2.03. During the month, the stock also paid a dividend of 35 cents. If you held a stock during that month, your total return would have been $2.03 from price appreciation plus 35 cents from the dividend. Divided by the price at the start of the month, I get a return of 7.88% for the month. I do this for every one of the 60 months. Actually, with 60 months of data, you're going to end up with 59 months of returns because you will lose the first month. You do the same thing with the index. Index at the start of the month, index at the end of the month, dividends in the index each month, compute the returns in the index. When you're done, you're going to have 59 months of returns on Disney and 59 months of returns on the S&P 500.

You're almost ready to run the regression, right? But before you do it, remember the caution that you probably got from your statistics professor. Always visualize the data before you run a regression. You're saying, "What does that mean?" I think of it as a scatter plot. On a scatter plot, you have, in this case, Disney on the y-axis and S&P 500 on the x-axis. Each point on this graph is a month of data. There should be 59 months of data on this graph. You're saying, "What's that line?" That's my best fit line, my regression line. We live in an age where regression packages do this for us, but it's still good to visualize the data. That line is the best fit line because it minimizes the square distances of those points from the line.

And at the bottom of the page, you actually see the beta for Disney, the regression output. The intercept was 0.0071, plus a slope of 1.25 times the return on the market. The R squared of the regression was about 74%. You're saying, "What's that number in brackets below the 1.2587?" That's called a standard error. That's the standard error of my beta estimate, and we will look at how that standard error plays into the estimate of beta that we get for Disney.

## Why You Should Try This Yourself

So the process of estimating regression betas starts by setting up the regression. Now, do you think you can do this for your stock? It's not rocket science, right? You need to collect data on prices and dividends for your stock going back in time. You have to make some choices. How far back in time? Daily or weekly, monthly? Make those choices. Come up with those returns for your stock. See if you can get returns on an index. If you can't, run the regression yourself. You're saying, "Why would I want to do that when I can look up a regression beta?" It's good to see how the sausage gets made, and the only way to do that is to actually run a regression beta.
