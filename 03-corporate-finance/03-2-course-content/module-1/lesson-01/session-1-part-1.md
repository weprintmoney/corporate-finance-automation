# Session 1 Part 1 — The Big Picture of Corporate Finance

In this session, we're going to look at corporate finance big picture. We're going to look at the principles that govern corporate finance, and we're going to use those principles to draw five broad themes that are going to run through this class over the rest of the sessions. So let's get started.

## From the Accounting Balance Sheet to the Financial Balance Sheet

If you think about corporate finance, you tend to think about numbers. And if you think about numbers, you tend to think about accounting. So the first thing I'd like to do is take you out of your accounting mindset. Let me explain what I mean by that.

You've all seen accounting balance sheets, and in a sense, they encapsulate how accountants think about the world. Accountants break assets down into current assets. These are assets like inventory and accounts receivable and even cash. Fixed assets, which tend to be long term assets, land, building, equipment, machinery. Financial assets, which might be investments in other companies. And what accountants euphemistically like to call intangible assets. But the most widely and most commonly used of these intangible assets is an asset called goodwill, which is really not an asset at all. It's a plug variable.

Accountants also like to break down their liabilities into current liabilities—these are short term, current accounts payable, supplier credit, short term debt—long term debt, and what, again, accountants like to measure a shareholders equity. That's an accounting balance sheet, and here's what characterizes an accounting balance sheet. It's backward looking. Whether accountants like it or not, accounting balance sheets reflect the history of the company. The longer a company has been around, the more substance there is going to be on an accounting balance sheet. Accounting balance sheets are also rule driven. Take a look at any of the accounting rule books. They run to hundreds of rules. Accountants love to tell you what you should be doing and what you should not be doing.

Now, here's what's different about the way we're going to think about the world in finance. We're going to take that balance sheet, and in one way, we're going to simplify. There are going to be only two items on each side of the balance sheet. At another level, though, this is a far more complex view of the world, and here's why. If you look at the assets out of the balance sheet, and I look at any company, here's how I break down your assets. The first grouping of assets, I call assets in place. These are investments you've already made as a company. Last year, two years ago, five years ago, maybe even 100 years ago. The second is what I call growth assets. These are investments I expect you to make in the future and the value that I attach to these investments. You might say how far into the future. Next year, two years out, as far as the eye can see. Assets in place and growth assets. I'm giving you the potential for value. Even if you're a young company, if I think you have lots of potential.

On the other side of the balance sheet, notice there are only two items. There are only two ways you can fund a business. You can borrow the money or use your own money. Borrowed money, we call debt. Your own money, we call equity. So a financial balance sheet simplifies the world. It brings into perspective the fact that in a growth company, the bulk of your value will come from growth assets, and it will lay the foundations to how we think about corporate financial decisions for companies. So keep this financial balance sheet in mind as we go through the process of understanding corporate finance.

## The Investment Principle

Now, I did tell you that corporate finance is a big picture class. So let me be explicit about what the big picture in corporate finance looks like. And this is a page you're going to see repeatedly through the class because it's going to become the anchor for the class. If you look at corporate finance, there are three broad principles that drive corporate finance. And let me state those principles upfront.

The first principle I call the investment principle. The investment principle says that when you as a business go out and make investments, make sure those investments earn a return that is greater than some minimum acceptable hurdle rate. You're saying, that's a buzzword, a hurdle rate. We'll come back and be more explicit about it. But let me lay out the sub principle that's going to govern how we think about hurdle rates.

The hurdle rate for an investment should be higher for riskier investments and lower for safer investments. If you're taking a risky investment, you might demand a 15% return. If you're taking a safe investment, you might settle for eight. The hurdle rate should also reflect where you get your money, whether you borrow money or use your own money. It should reflect the mix of debt and equity. So the hurdle rate should reflect the risk of what you're taking and where you get the money.

The returns on investment should be based on cash flows, as opposed to what, as opposed to accounting earnings. They should reflect when you get those cash flows, because it matters whether you get them right up front or later in the process. It should reflect the fact that if you have side benefits, a side case, those should also be built into the returns. So returns on a project should be based on cash flows, should reflect when you get those cash flows, and have all side effects built into them. That's the investment principle. Make sure you invest in assets that earn a return greater than that hurdle rate.

## The Financing Principle

Let's turn to the second principle. It's called the financing principle. I mentioned that there are only two ways you could raise money. You could use debt, borrowed money, or your own money, equity. The financing principle says, find a mix of debt and equity that maximizes your value as a business. So in effect, you're looking for that mix, 10% debt, 90% equity or 50% debt, 50% equity, that maximizes your value as a business. That's the first part of the financing principle.

Here's the second one. Match your debt up to your assets. If you have long term assets, you're taking projects that last 30 years, make sure your debt is long term. If your assets generate cash flows in euros, borrow in euros. So the financing principle says, find a mix of debt and equity that maximizes your value as a business and match up the debt to your assets.

## The Dividend Principle

Which brings us to the third and final principle that drives corporate finance. It's a dividend principle. Remember, we said that if you look at projects, you want to make sure they earn a return greater than your hurdle rate. Well, let's suppose you've reached a stage in your business where you look around and you cannot find projects that make your hurdle rate. Your hurdle rate is 10%, and everything you look at makes less than 10%. Don't force the issue and try to take the best possible project now. There's a better alternative.

If you cannot find investments that earn your hurdle rate, give the cash back to the owners of the business. And if you're wondering how to give the cash back, if it's a public company, there are two ways you can do it. You can either pay dividends or you can buy back stock. Which one you should use will depend on what your stockholders or owners would like. So the dividend principle says if you cannot find investments around the hurdle rate, give the cash back.

## The Singular Objective: Maximize Firm Value

The investment principle, the financing principle, the dividend principle. Everything we do in corporate finance will fall into one of these three buckets. And doing all of this, you have a singular objective in corporate finance. You want to make your business the most valuable business you can. You want to maximize the value of your business.

As I said, everything we're going to do in this class is somewhere on this page, and in fact, we're going to use this page to extract almost every tool and technique that's now part of corporate finance. So get the big picture. You can lose the details, but the big picture is perhaps the most critical part of what I'd like you to take out of this class.
