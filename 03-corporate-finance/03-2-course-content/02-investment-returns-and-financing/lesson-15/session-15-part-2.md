# Session 15 Part 2 — NPV, IRR, and Terminal Value

## Two Time-Weighted Return Measures: NPV and IRR

So let's take those present value mechanics and convert them into a time-weighted return. Broadly speaking, there are two ways you can compute a time-weighted incremental cash flow return for a project.

Here's the first. It's called the net present value. Take the present value of each and every cash flow you have in a project, and add them all up, including the initial investment. The net present value, that aggregated value, if it's greater than zero, tells you the project is a good project. It's as simple as that.

You're saying, "But what if I have a billion-dollar project, and my net present value is only $1? Why would I still accept it?" Because this is $1 over and above what you needed to make on this project. Remember, the net present value is a measure of excess value. You've earned your cost of capital plus $1, so the net present value is a dollar value of incremental cash flow returns.

If you prefer a percentage of return, here's how you compute it. You take those incremental cash flows, and you look for that discount rate that makes a net present value zero. That discount rate is called an internal rate of return, and here's how you use it. If your internal rate of return, or IRR, is greater than your hurdle rate, you have a good project. Take it.

So the net present value and IRR are both time-weighted incremental cash flow measures of return.

## Terminal Value for the Disney Theme Parks

Now, before I do this, use either of these approaches on the Disney theme parks. Let me put some closure on the cash flows. Remember when we talked about accounting returns? When I got to year 10, I waved my hands and talked about how these projects were really long-term projects, and putting a 10-year life was unfair. Well, I'm going to do something about it now.

I'm going to assume that in the case of the Disney theme parks in Rio, that the projects will not end after year 10, but will continue forever. I know that's scary, forever, but hang in there for a moment. I'm going to assume that beyond year 10, the number of tourists coming into the theme park will level off, but that the cash flows will grow at the inflation rate. In other words, Disney will raise ticket prices at the inflation rate. I have a growing perpetuity, right?

So here's what I do. At the end of year 10, I use the growing perpetuity equation we just talked about to compute the value of the cash flows beyond year 10. So I take the cash flows in year 11, which is the cash flow in year 10 grown out 2%, and I divide by the difference between my discount rate, the cost of capital of 8.46% and my growth rate forever of 2%. I come up with $11,275 million. I call that my terminal value.

Now, to illustrate how this number stands out, I took the incremental cash flows in the project for the next 10 years, and you can already see the terminal value is the biggest number on this table. You're saying, "What does that measure?" It measures what I will get for the Disney theme parks if I sell them lock, stock, barrel, and Mickey included at the end of year 10. It is my expected value for the theme park at the end of the 10th year. It's my way of bringing in cash flows after year 10.

## Computing NPV and IRR for the Project

I'm almost home. Here's what I do next. I discount those incremental cash flows and the terminal value back to today using my 8.46% cost of capital. That's a cost of capital for this theme park. The present value that I come up with, or the net present value, is $3.3 billion.

You say, "What do I do with that?" If you believe that net present value, that tells me that this is a good project. It tells me more. If Disney accepts this project, I would argue that its value as a company will increase by $3.3 billion. That's one of the nice things about NPV. It's additive. If you take a project with an NPV of $3.3 billion, your value will increase by $3.3 billion. If you take a project with a net present value of zero, your value will not change, and if you take a project with a net present value of minus $2 billion, your value just dropped by $2 billion.

Now, if you asked me to convert the net present value into an internal rate of return, here's how I'd do it. I'd compute what's called a net present value profile. Sounds fancy, but all I do is I take my incremental cash flows, and I discount them at different discount rates starting at 2%, 4%, 6%, and I plot the net present values. Remember, the IRR is that discount rate that makes my net present value zero. In this particular case, that happens at about 12.6%. That is the internal rate for the return for this project, and compared to my cost of capital, 8.46%, I come to exactly the same conclusions that I do with NPV. This is a good project.

## When NPV and IRR Diverge

I know in many corporate finance classes, people spend hours, perhaps even days talking about the differences between NPV and IRR and which one's a better approach. In my view, 90% of the time, NPV and IRR give you the same decision.

It is true that sometimes, NPV and IRR can lead you in different ways, especially when you're ranking projects, and you can see why. NPV is a dollar value. IRR is a percentage value. So when you're comparing projects with different scale, IRR is going to bias you towards smaller projects. It's easier to make a higher percentage return on a small project than a big project. So if you are a small company that faces capital constraints, you might think about using IRR over net present value.

The second reason for the difference, and this is a technical reason, there can be only one net present value for a project, but you can have more than one IRR, especially if your cash flows change signs. You're saying, "What should I do then?" Well, go back to net present value.

There's a third and more subtle reason why NPV and IRR can sometimes give you different rankings. When you have a multi-year project, you're making implicit assumptions about the return you can make on your intermediate cash flows. What am I talking about? You get cash flows in year one, year two, and year three. You have to reinvest those cash flows, right? The NPV approach assumes you can reinvest those cash flows at your hurdle rate, your cost of equity or cost of capital. Your IRR assumes you can reinvest those cash flows at the internal rate of return.

It is true that the net present value approach makes better assumptions about reinvestment than the IRR does, and here's why. If you have a project with an IRR of 50%, not only are you assuming that this project will generate 50%. You're also assuming that you can find other projects in years one, two, and three where you can invest your cash flows at that 50%. There are ways you can get around this. There's an approach called the modified internal rate of return. My suggestion, though, is far simpler. When you have significant reinvestment rate problems, just go back to the net present value.

## Wrap-Up

So let's wrap it all up. If you have to look at a project, and you can estimate its earnings, get from the earnings to the cash flows, get from the cash flows the incremental cash flows, and then close the loop by computing the present value of those incremental cash flows. Look at the NPV for the project, and also compute the IRR. See if this project passes muster based on a time-weighted incremental cash flow basis.
