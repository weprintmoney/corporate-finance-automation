# Session 19 Part 1 — From Optimal Debt Ratio to Value Impact

So now that we've used the cost of capital approach to come up with the optimal makes of debt and equity for the company, let's complete this here. Let's talk about what to do next. To illustrate what this process involves, let me go back to my Disney Cost of Capital Schedule. Remember how I came up with the optimal debt ratio for Disney is 40%, that makes it which its cost of capital is minimized. Well, to put some flesh on that recommendation, let me explain to you what it's going to mean.

## The Disney Recommendation and Three Pushback Questions

Disney, currently, as of November of 2013, has $15.96 billion in debt. If you trust my analysis, its optimal debt ratio is 55.1 billion. In effect, I'm asking Disney to borrow 39.1 billion. In fact, I'm telling it to do more. I am asking it to borrow 39.1 billion and buy back stock who pay a special dividend. That's a pretty large action, right? And if you were Disney, you probably should be pushing back. And here are three questions you probably will ask me if I make this recommendation.

Here's the first one, why should we do it? You're going to see very quickly that the answer that your cost of capital will go down is not good enough. Second, if you have any sense, you should ask me, "What if something goes wrong? What if we borrow 55.1 billion, and there is a recession or there is a market crisis? Are we going to go bankrupt?" And the third question you should ask me is, "What if we don't want to buy back stock? What if we have plans to build new theme parks in Shanghai, or expand the ESPN globally? Wouldn't it make more sense to borrow the money and take those investments instead?"

So why should we do it? What if something goes wrong? And will the optimal that you've computed still hold if instead of buying back stock, you took projects instead? Let me take each of those questions and address them.

## Why Should We Do It? Converting Cost of Capital Change into Value

Let me start with why should we do it? At first sight, the answer seems pretty simple, right? The cost of capital which is 7.81% today will become 7.16%. But think about it. I want you to borrow almost $40 billion in buyback stock to lower your cost of capital by 0.65%. That doesn't sound like very much, right, in terms of benefits? So, I'm going to convert that change in cost of capital into real money.

And here's the first way to do it. Remember I described the cost of capital as that discount rate you would use to value an entire business? Well, let's assume that I tried to value Disney, and I'm going to keep this simple. We're going to come back and talk about full evaluations of Disney. But in this evaluation, let's assume I computed the free cash flow to the firm. Free cash flow exactly the way I computed the cash flows for a project by starting with the after tax operating income, adding depreciation, subtracting capex, and subtracting change in working capital, and I came up with 3.657 billion for Disney. That's Disney's current cash flow.

Let's assume that I took the existing market value of the firm which is 133.9 billion, and that is just a market value of the operating assets or the enterprise value, and I use that as my base value. Here's the question I'm going to ask, "If the current market value is right and the current cost of capital is right, what growth rate would I need to get that market value?" I solve for the growth rate. Hang in there for a moment because you're going to see exactly why I need this. The growth rate that I get by using the existing value of the firm, the existing cash flows, and the existing cost of capital is 4.94%.

Now, here's how I'm going to use it. I know my cashflow. I know my growth rate. I put in the new cost of capital of 7.16%. The value that I get for Disney with that new cost of capital is 172.9 billion. So, my numbers are right. Disney's value will increase by almost $39 billion. That's too good to be true, right? And, in fact, it is. And here's why. That growth rate of 4.94% is way too high to last forever.

## A Simpler Computation: Annual Financing Savings

So, I'm going to do a much simpler computation of why you should move to the optimal. And here's what I'm going to do. One way to think about your cost of capital is this is what it costs you today to finance your entire business. So, at the existing cost of capital of 7.81%, each year, Disney is paying out 10,458 million as a cost to finance a business. That's 7.81% of the existing enterprise value. If they go to their optimal, 7.16% cost of capital, and that cost on an annual basis will drop to 9,592 million. On an annual basis, Disney will save $866 million. That's in a year.

If you take the present value of those savings using a growth rate that's equal to the risk-free rate, a growth rate that is sustainable, and the 7.16% cost of capital, the increase in value you get for Disney is $19.6 billion. So ask me again, "Why should Disney do this?" The answer is not 65 basis points whether the cost of capital will go down. It's because if they do this, their firm value will increase by 19.6 billion.

## Where Does the Value Come From? Value Transfer, Not Creation

Let's stop and ask question, though. "Where is that increasing value coming from?" After all, I'm not changing the investments Disney is making. It's still invested in the same theme parks, the same broadcasting businesses, the same merchandising businesses it is before. So, where's the additional value coming from?

The answer, of course, is a very cynical one. Remember the biggest benefit of debt is a tax benefit. That 19.6 billion is not value creation in the traditional sense, it's a value transfer. And the reason I described it as a value transfer is when Disney borrows money, it's transferring value from the taxpayers to stockholders in a company. Now, don't get me wrong. I'm not saying Disney is being immoral or unethical from doing this. The tax code does allow you to borrow money. But let's be honest about where the value created from debt comes from. It comes from the fact that there's a tax benefit associated with debt.

## Translating to Per-Share Value: The Buyback Premium

Now, let's do one final piece to this puzzle. Remember I said you have to go out and buy back shares. Now, if you think about buying back shares, you can't buy back shares at today's price because nobody will offer their shares back. So, the essence of a share buyback is you have to offer a premium. Now, the stock price for Disney before any of this process unfold was $67.71. Let's assume Disney decides to borrow money and buy back shares. One interesting question to ask is, "When you buy back shares, how much of a premium should you offer on those shares? Or how much will your value per share go up?"

Here's what I did. I took the total change in value that I computed by looking at the savings each year and computing into a present value, that's 19.623 billion, and I divide it by the total number of shares outstanding, I get a value of $10.90. You're saying, "Wait. But I thought we were buying back shares?" You're right. But when I divide it by the total number of shares outstanding, here's what I'm assuming. I'm assuming that everybody in Disney will get an equal share of the spoils. Everybody in what sense? The people who sell their shares back and the people who hold onto their shares all get the same piece of that part. So, the $10.90 will be how much you pay as a premium on the shares you bought back, and the $10.90 will be the increasing value for your remaining shares. This is the fairest, fairest way to spread the spoils across the stockholders. But as we will see, it's not often what happens.

But in this process, at least, you've converted the change in cost of capital into a change in value, into a change in value per share. So, if you've tried the cost of capital spreadsheet in your company, take a look at the numbers because you should be able to see how much your value as a company increases by going to the optimal, and how much of an increase in the stock price you're going to get from the optimal. If the change is small on a percentage basis, 2%, 3%, 4%, probably isn't worth your while, and your company's worth even change its debt ratio. If it's substantial, 20%, 25%, 30%, then, time to take a closer look.
