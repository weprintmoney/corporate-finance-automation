# Session 18 Part 2 — Disney Optimal Debt Ratio via Cost of Capital

## Starting Point: Unlevered Beta and Operating Income

So now that I've laid out the steps involved in using the cost of capital approach to come up with the optimal debt ratio, let's get specific and use Disney as our example. So the first step, as I said, is to get the beta every debt ratio. And to get the beta, I need an unlevered beta. If you remember, the way I got the unlevered beta for Disney is I used a bottom-up beta. I'm going to return to that bottom-up beta. Based on the five businesses that Disney is in, the bottom-up beta that I came up with for Disney's operations was about 0.92. This is actually the exact same computation you saw few sessions ago when I talked about bottom-up betas. I'm going to start with that unlevered beta of 0.92, and I'm going to use it come up with my levered betas.

I need an interest coverage ratio. To get the interest coverage ratio, I need to know what the operating income is. So here again I'm going to do my homework. Based on Disney's current financials, it looks like they have about $10.03 billion in operating income. And I have adjusted for leases, because leases are treated as debt, and that's going to be the basis for my computing interest coverage ratios. So the unlevered beta and the current financials are the starting point for my analysis.

## Cost of Equity at Every Debt Ratio

So let's start with the beta and the cost of equity for Disney at every debt ratio. This, as I said, is the easier to have because it's purely mechanical. Let's start with the easiest debt ratio. If Disney has no debt, its levered beta will be equal to its unlevered beta, which is 0.9239, and its cost of equity of 8.07% will also be its cost of capital. So at 0% debt, I'm done.

I had a 10% debt ratio. I have 10% debt and 90% equity. Ten divided by 90 is 11.11%. That's a debt to equity ratio. I come up with a levered beta of 0.99 and I use that to come up with a cost of equity. You see where I'm going next? I take every debt ratio, I compute the debt to equity ratio at that level, I come up with a new levered beta and a new cost of equity. And here's what happens to my cost of equity. It starts at 8.07%, but by the time I get to 90% debt, which is where I would be if I did a leveraged buyout of Disney, my cost of equity is 38.69%. Half of my table is done, but this beta is coming from the fact that I can lever or unlever beta and tell you what the cost of equity will be at every debt ratio.

## Cost of Debt: The Iterative Process

Let's turn to the cost of debt because it's a more messy process. To show you how the cost of risk is computed, I'm going to take you through one debt ratio by hand. And let's try another one together. I'm going to try to compute what the rating and cost of debt for Disney would be at a 10% debt ratio. And here's what I'm going to do. I'm going to start with the total value of Disney as a company today. I take the market value of equity, add the market value of debt, I get the total value for the company of $137.8 billion. You say, "What do I need that?" Well, the first step in computing the cost of debt to 10% debt ratio is computing how much dollar debt I will have at a 10% debt ratio. Ten percent of 137.84 billion is 13.784 billion. That becomes my dollar debt, and it starts the process rolling.

Then I look at the EBITDA, the depreciation of the EBIT, and I'm going to make an assumption. I'm going to make an assumption that when I borrow money, that money's not invested in projects, it gets used to buy back stock or pay a dividend. It's called recapitalization. And the reason I do it is I want to isolate the effect of changing my capital structure. So I leave the EBITDA, the depreciation of the EBIT, unchanged at what it was at no debt. Then comes a tricky number. I need the interest expense. I multiply my dollar debt, 13,784, by the interest rate to come up with the interest expense. Hang in there with me, because there's a problem there, we'll come back to it. I divide the operating income by that interest expense, 10,032 divided by the 434 million interest expenses, I come up with an interest coverage ratio of 23.1. I turn to the next page and have a look-up table. Disney's a big company, and based on this look-up table, 23.1 gives me a triple-A rating. That's the rating that I attach to Disney at a 10% debt ratio. I'm almost done. With that rating, I come up with an interest rate, and that interest rate becomes my cost of debt pretax for Disney at a 10% debt ratio.

So let me list the steps and you tell me where the problem is. I start with the dollar debt, I multiply by the interest rate to come up with an interest expense, I divide the operating income by the interest expense to come up with the coverage ratio, I use the coverage ratio to get a rating, I use the rating to get an interest rate. See where the problem is? There is a chicken and the egg problem. To get the interest expense in step two, I need the interest rate in step five. To get the interest rate in step five, I need the interest expense in step two. You say, "What I'm going to do about this?" I'm going to give you a simple solution to the problem, but for the moment, accept my word that at the 10% debt ratio, the problem doesn't affect your answer.

## Closing the Loop at 30% Debt

To see what I'm going to do, let me take you to a 30% debt ratio. Let's see how the cost of debt plays out at the 30% debt ratio. Again, I start with the dollar debt. At a 30% debt ratio, the dollar debt is 41,352 million. That's 30% of 137.84 billion. The EBITDA, the depreciation of the EBIT remain the same as they were at a 20% debt ratio. Why? Because I'm assuming recapitalization, no new projects. Then I get to the interest expense. When I first try to do this, there is no interest rate that I can use, right? So here's what I'm going to do. I'm going to use the interest rate I've already computed at a 20% debt ratio, and that interest rate is 3.15% and use that as my interest rate at a 30% debt ratio. You think, "That's completely unrealistic." I agree but hang in there, because that interest expense that I get of 1,302 million gives me an interest coverage ratio of 7.7. I turn back to the previous page where I have the look-up table. And when I look up that interest coverage ratio, the rating I get is AA, and the interest rate that goes with that rating is 3.45%.

We have a problem and I know we have a problem, here's the problem. The interest expense was computed using the 3.15% rate. The interest rate I'm claiming that Disney will have is 3.45%. Both numbers can't be right. So here's how I close the loop. I take the 3.45% as my new interest rate and recompute the interest expense. Nothing else really changes. The interest expense goes up to 1,427 million. The interest coverage ratio decreases, not surprisingly, but my rating still stays AA. I'm done. I have a rating and a cost of debt for Disney. It's this iterative process that I use to get the debt ratio and the cost of debt at every single debt ratio.

## Ratings, Tax Rate Adjustments, and After-Tax Cost of Debt

And when I'm done, here's what you're going to see. As my debt ratio goes up, my dollar debt goes up. No surprise there. As my dollar debt goes up, my interest expense goes up more than proportionately. Why? Because my rates are rising. As my interest expense goes up, my coverage ratio drops. As my coverage ratio drops, my rating, which starts at AAA, ends at C. My pretax cost of debt rises as my rating drops.

One final adjustment. Notice that the tax rate that I'm using once I get past 60% is lower than 36.1% I've been using so far. I'll come back and talk about it, but it reflects the fact that at very high debt ratios, my interest expense gets so large that I'm losing tax benefits. In the last column, you see my after-tax cost of debt. It starts at 2.01% when I have no debt or very little debt and ends up at 10.33%.

Let me talk a little bit about the tax rate issue. At a 60% debt ratio, I have a lot of debt. But at that 60% debt ratio, my interest expense is 9,511 million less than my operating income. I'm able to get the entire tax rate benefit of 36.1%. At a 70% debt ratio, the interest expense actually balloons to 11.1 billion higher than my operating income, and I can't claim that entire interest expense. I can claim only up to 10,032 million. So here's how I compute the effective tax rate. I compute the tax saving I will have on my maximum deduction, which is 10,032 million, and I divide that tax saving by the entire interest expense. And my tax rate is 32.6%. And as I get to 80%, that number gets even lower.

## Optimal Debt Ratio and the Kink in Cost of Capital

So here's the net effect. Using the approach that I just described for Disney, I get a cost of equity and an after-tax cost of debt at every debt ratio. My cost of capital is minimized at about a 40% debt ratio. Initially, debt helps me, but at some point in time, it starts to hurt me. In fact, if you prefer this is a graph, there you go. It's not quite U-shaped because the initial slope is gradual. But there is an inflection point, where if you hit that, your cost of capital starts to rise very quickly. My advice to companies is if you're going to make a mistake, be under-levered rather than over-levered. It's a much pleasanter mistake to make.

Incidentally, I've been looking at Disney for a long time now. This is my fourth iteration of the book and my fourth cost of capital chart. I'm going to take you back to 1997 and show you my Disney cost of capital chart then, because there is an interesting feature that reveals a weakness, perhaps, something that could be fixed in the way we approach the cost of capital. Notice there's a kink in the cost of capital? Somewhere between 50% and 70%? Here's why it happens. The way the cost of capital is computed is I compute the cost of equity at every debt ratio. And as my debt ratio changes, my cost of equity will always change. But my cost of debt comes from that rating spreadsheet, right? Where I look up based on your interest coverage ratio what your rating should be. If your interest to coverage ratio doesn't move enough, I might leave your rating unchanged as I go from 50% to 60% debt, and that's what's happening here. It's because my cost of debt is discontinuous that you get the kink in the cost of capital graph.
