# Session 31 Part 2 — Fundamental Growth: Reinvestment, Returns, and Revenue-Based Models

## Estimating Growth for Tata Motors

So let's continue to build on that proposition of growth coming from fundamentals by looking at Tata Motors first and then looking at Disney, and talking about what to do when those fundamentals don't hold.

So let's move to Tata Motors. Just like Deutsche, I'm going to estimate how much of the net income Tata Motors puts back into the business, and how well it reinvests. But rather than distrusting the retention ratio, here's what I'm going to do instead. I'm going to look at how much of the net income Tata Motors actually puts into projects, net CapEx and change in working capital, allowing for the fact that some of that reinvestment might be covered by debt.

And over the 5 years prior leading into 2013, Tata Motors' equity reinvestment rate, the percentage of the net income that they reinvested back, was about 87.7%. During those five years, the return on equity the company made on average again was about 43%. Notice that the 2013 numbers, the most recent year's numbers, are not as good. Tata Motors reinvested less in 2013, and had a lower return on equity in 2013 than over the previous 5 years.

If you ask me what the expected growth for Tata Motors is going to be, I can use one of two estimates. I can assume that the 2013 numbers are now indicative of the future, and the product of those two numbers, the reinvestment rate of 80.5% and the return on equity of close to 30%, gives me a growth rate of about 24%. That's using 2013 numbers. If, like Deutsche, I fall back and normalize our average numbers, I actually get a higher growth rate because I have a higher equity reinvestment rate, close to 88%, and a return on equity of 43%. The product of those two numbers gives me a growth rate of 38%. If you're saying, "Which one is the right one?" Remember, this is valuation. We don't know what the right number is; all you can do is make your best judgment.

## Return on Equity: Great Projects vs. Leverage

Now if you look at the process by which we estimated growth in equity earnings, the driver for that growth and the value of that growth is the return on equity. Just as a tangent, I want to talk a little bit about how a company can end up with a higher return on equity.

One is to take really great projects; you could end up with a higher return on equity as a consequence. But here's the other way you could push up your return on equity. You could take average projects and end up with a higher return on equity by using debt. You're saying, "How does debt help me?" When you borrow money, you have a cost of debt, right? In fact, you have an after-tax cost of debt. If your after-tax cost of debt is 4%, and you take projects that are in 10%, 12%, or 15%, equity investors, in a sense, keep the difference.

As you [inaudible], you can actually decompose the return on equity for a company into two components. The first is the return on capital; think of that as what your return on equity would have been if you'd used no debt. Add to that the effect of debt. And the effect of debt is a function of how much debt you use, which I measure with your debt-to-equity ratio, and how much that debt helps you as an equity investor, and that's captured by the difference between the return on capital and your after-tax cost of debt.

Put differently, you can end up with a higher return on equity because you use a lot of debt, and that higher return on equity will have a lower value than a higher return on equity coming from great projects.

To illustrate that process, let me give you an example. Let's assume you have a company with a 15% return on capital, an after-tax cost of debt of 5%, and a debt-to-equity ratio of 100%. Here again, I'm going to use book values because a return on equity is a book value concept. This company's return on equity will be 25%, and here is why. Fifteen percent is my return on capital, but because I'm borrowing a dollar of debt for every dollar of equity and earning a 10% spread, my overall return on equity is going to be 25%.

Notice though, that that 25% return on equity comes with a cost. The cost is that you borrowed more money. And remember, that when you value equity, you're going to use a cost of equity that reflects the beta of your company. And if you remember, betas are a function of how much you borrow. So if you have two companies with a 25% return on equity, one of which got there with no debt and one with a lot of debt like this one, the company that got to the 25% return on equity without using debt will be worth more than the company that uses 100% debt-to-equity ratio. So it is true, you can pump up the growth in equity earnings by using more debt, but it's not true that that's always going to lead to a higher value for your equity.

## Growth for Disney: Firm-Level Reinvestment

Now, let's get back to measuring reinvestment rates and growth rates, and use Disney. But remember, with Disney, I'm valuing the entire company, not just the equity in the company. So here, I'm going to focus not on the equity reinvestment rate and return on equity, but the overall reinvestment rate and the return on capital.

If you remember, for the most recent year for Disney, when we looked at the cash flows, we estimated the reinvestment rate that year to be about 54%; that's a net CapEx plus change in working capital, divided by the after-tax operating income. In that year, Disney's return on capital was 12.61%. If you take the product of those two numbers, the reinvestment rate times return on capital, the growth rate I get for Disney is 6.8%.

## Revenue-Based Growth: The Baidu Example

So this approach to estimating growth based on fundamentals works if you have a stable return on equity and a stable return on capital, a predictable value. But what if you have a company whose operating margins are changing, and because they're changing, your return on equity and your return on capital are also changing? You cannot use the equation that I used for Tata Motors and Disney to come up with a growth rate for the company.

Here's what you have to do. You have to start with your top line, which is revenues, and focus those out first. Then you've got to project out the expected operating margins over time, and that might reflect either increasing margins or decreasing margins depending on what you think will happen to your company. And then you have to estimate how much you would reinvest based on your revenues, not your operating income. And I use a ratio called the sales-to-capital ratio to estimate that reinvestment. What that allows me to do is look at the change in revenues and tell you how much you need to reinvest to get that change.

To illustrate this, let me use Baidu. For Baidu, here's what I'm going to project. I'm going to project pretty high revenue growth, revenue growth of about 25% a year for the next 5 years, scaling down over time to a growth rate of 3.5%. Remember, I'm doing everything in Chinese [inaudible]. So that gives me projected revenues.

For the operating margin, which is 49% now, I'm going to assume that over time competition is going to push that margin down to 35%, self-changing margins. Using those changing margins, I get an operating income. Using that operating income, I get after-tax operating income. So far so good, right?

To get my reinvestment, I'll start with a change in revenue. The change in revenue comes from my growth in revenues. But to get to my reinvestment, I use the sales-to-capital ratio. The sales-to-capital ratio for Baidu that I estimate is 2.64. You're saying, "What does that tell me?" Based on that number, here's what I assume will happen: for every dollar Baidu invests, I'm expecting it to create $2.64 in additional revenues. So to get the $7.169 billion in additional revenue in year 1, I will need to reinvest about $2.7 billion in year 1; that's a 7,169 million divided by the 2.64, and I do that for each year. That reinvestment is a composite number; it includes net CapEx and change in working capital. And that reinvestment is what I'm going to subtract out from after-tax operating income to get to free cash flow to the firm.

## Choosing the Right Growth Approach

So let's review. If you have a company with stable returns on capital, you can estimate the growth rate by using the fundamental growth rate equation. If you have a company with unstable margins, either increasing or decreasing margins, you've got to go the long way, estimate revenues and work down to cash flows. The endgame in either case is a free cash flow to the firm.

So let's now talk about estimating growth for your company. Assuming your last year's free cash flow as a firm, start with the fundamentals. What was your investment rate? What was your return on capital? You'll be doing a firm valuation. What was your equity investment rate? What was the return on equity if you're doing an equity valuation? Estimate a fundamental growth rate. But before you use it, stop and ask yourself a question: "Is my company in steady state? Are its margins stable?" If the answer is no, you might have to shift to the revenue-based model where you start with revenues and estimate free cash flows to the firm from those revenues.
