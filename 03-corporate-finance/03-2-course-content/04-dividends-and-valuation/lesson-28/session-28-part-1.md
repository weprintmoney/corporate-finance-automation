# Session 28 Part 1 — Me-Too Dividend Policy and Peer Group Assessment

## Me-Too Corporate Finance

If you remember in the context of capital structure, I talked about how most companies decide how much to borrow. I said they look at everybody else in the sector and look at what they do and try to match up. Unfortunately, with dividend policy, the same pattern applies. If you ask me why do companies pay the dividends that they do, the answer in most cases is because everybody else in the sector does exactly the same thing.

So in this session, let's talk about assessing dividends from the perspective of your peer group. So how do companies decide whether to pay a lot in dividends, very little in dividends, and no dividends at all? The answer is, much of corporate finance is what I call "me-too corporate finance." What does that mean? The way companies decide how much to pay in dividends, they look at everybody else in the sector, look at what they do, and they say, "Me too." So if you're in a sector where nobody pays dividends, even if you can afford to pay dividends, you choose not to pay dividends because you want to be me too.

## Comparing Companies to Their Sectors

So let's talk about fleshing in the details of me-too dividend policy. To make a judgment on whether the company pays out too much in dividends, here's what you're going to do. You're going to compare what your company does to what the rest of the sector does. And that's what I've tried to do in this table.

Let me start with Disney. I've looked at the dividend yield and the dividend payout ratio for Disney. Its dividend yield is about 1.17%, its payout ratio is about 17%. If you look at the dividend yield for the entertainment sector, it's surprisingly close. The dividend yield is about 1% for the sector, its payout ratio is about 23%. Now, if I didn't known better, if you ask me, why does Disney do what it does, my simplest answer might be "Because that's what everybody else in the entertainment sector does."

Let's move on to Vale. Vale has a very high dividend yield and a very high payout ratio, and we talked about why, because iron ore prices have collapsed and Vale wants to continue to do what it did. Well, guess what? Everybody else in the sector is doing exactly what Vale does. They too have high dividend yields and high payout ratios, a payout ratio that exceeds 100%.

If you look at Tata Motors and you look at its dividend yield and dividend payout ratio, it's lower than what the rest of the auto sector is doing, but not by much. Again, if you look at the policy, it looks like it's closely patterned to what other companies are doing.

With Baidu, which pays no dividends… before you pick on the company, notice that the payout ratio and the dividend yield for all tech companies is low as well.

With Deutsche, which has a very high dividend yield and a dividend payout ratio, it's much higher than the sector but part of the reason for that is Deutsche's net income collapsed in the year that I looked at it, where I computed the payout ratio. And that might explain the discordant number more than the bank trying to pay out more than the sector.

So in effect, when you look at these five companies, you can already see how much the sector drives what that they do.

## Regression Analysis Across the Market

But let's take a closer look at Disney's peer group. Here's what I did. I took all of the entertainment companies in my sample and I looked at dividend yields and dividend payout ratios by company. And if you look across a sector, notice there are wide divergences across companies, some companies pay no dividends, others pay very high dividends. By looking at the average of the median, you're throwing away a significant amount of information, right? Because all you're using is one number as your basis for the comparison. Could we use more of these numbers?

In fact, I'm going to draw on a technique I use with the debt ratio analysis. Remember when I looked at a sector, I ran a regression? In the regression, here's what I'm going to do. I'm going to take the dividend yield and the dividend payout ratio and look at the variables that should drive it. In fact, rather than just stay at the sector, I'm going to go to the entire market.

So let's take a closer look at these companies. Now, one way in which you can use more of the information, rather than just stay focused on median average, is run a regression of dividend yields and payout ratios against variables that you think should drive those numbers in the sector. I did that, but rather than show you those numbers, I'm going to show you the numbers I got when I ran a much more expanded regression. In this regression, I looked at dividend yield and dividend payout ratios for the entire market, something similar to what I did with debt ratios. So I was trying to explain dividend payout ratio and yield differences across companies using differences in betas and growth and debt ratios.

The R-squareds are still moderate, 19.6% in the case of payout and 25.8% in the case of dividend yield. But it is what it is. It is, in fact, a number that I can still use. If I can plug in Disney's numbers into those regressions, and that's exactly what I did, Disney's beta, Disney's growth, and Disney's market debt to cap ratio, I can come up with a predicted payout ratio and a predicted dividend yield for Disney.

When I do that, here's what I get. My predicted payout ratio for Disney is 27%, my predicted dividend yield is 1.4%. You're saying, "What does that tell me?" Given how the rest of the companies in the U.S. market are setting dividends, that's what I'd expect Disney's payout ratio and yield to be. Its actual yield and payout ratio is slightly lower. So to the extent that I trust this regression, my conclusion would be Disney pays too little in dividends relative to the typical company in the market. You know what I'd be missing when I say that? Remember that Disney returns a bulk of its cash in the form of buybacks? Perhaps, this regression should be run not just with dividends but with dividends plus buybacks. I haven't done that, but it's something to think about.

## Changing Dividend Policy and the Clientele Effect

So let's close our assessment of dividend policy by looking at how companies change dividends in either direction, either from no dividends to high dividends or high dividends to low dividends. You're saying, "What's the problem with doing that?" It goes back to that clientele effect I've talked about off and on through these dividend sessions. In the clientele effect, I argued that you get the stockholders who like your dividend policy. So when you change dividends, you're going to upset those stockholders. So if you've never paid a dividend and you start paying a dividend, you're paying dividends to people who don't like to get dividends. If you've always paid a dividend and you try to cut dividends, you're trying to take cash away from investors who like dividends. That's part of the reason we talked about how to bundle dividend changes with other changes perhaps to get a better reaction from the market.

So to illustrate how difficult it is to change dividend policy, let me set up an example for you. Let's assume you're the CEO of a phone company, a phone company which historically has paid a large dividend because it's a safe business and does not have much in reinvestment needs but let's say, circumstances have changed. This phone company is now a technology company and requires large reinvestment. You have a problem, right? Your existing dividend policy reflects a company that no longer exists.

So think about actions you could take as a CEO. You could try to be courageous and go in front of your shareholders and say, "We're a different company. We can't afford to pay the dividends we used to," and cut dividends. That's not going to go over well. You could continue to pay the dividends and perhaps put off investing, in which case, you will never be able to survive as a growth company, or you could try to have your cake and eat it too. Here's what you could do. You could actually divide the company into two parts. One could be the stable, mature part of the company which continues to pay the dividends, and you could spin off the other part, the risky part that requires reinvestment, and have different dividend policies for each one. That way, investors can self-select which stock they want to hold. That's what many phone companies have tried to do over the last two decades.

## Time for a Reset in Dividends

So it's time for a reset in dividends. Dividends are a carryover from a different era. The notion that you can keep paying a fixed dividend when your net income is unreliable, unpredictable, and unstable strikes me as dangerous. That is perhaps the reason why we're seeing an increasingly large number of companies shift away from dividends to stock buybacks. I think it's time we went back to a policy of residual dividends, pay what's left over rather than pay the same amount year after year. A stock is not a bond, and we should start treating it as a residual claim.

So you're ready? You have everything you need to assess the dividend policy of your company. So here's what I'd like you to do to complete your analysis. Now that you've done your dividends and buybacks for your company, compare what your company does to other companies in the sector. You might not be surprised to find the following, that your company's dividend policy is largely explained by what the rest of the sector does. It's me-too corporate finance at play. It's unfortunate, but it is the truth.
