# Session 19 Part 2 — Buyback Pricing, What-If Analysis, and Debt Capacity Constraints

So now that we've talked about the effect of moving to the new debt ratio in terms of change in value, let's complete the process by looking at the other two questions. What if something goes wrong, or what if we don't want to buy back shares or not allowed to buy back shares? Before we do that, though, notice that as we ended the last session, I talked about the fair price, a price at which everybody in the company gets an equal share of the spoils. That fair price happened to be $78.71. What does that mean? If you pay $78.71, everybody gets $10.90 as a premium.

## Setting a Buyback Price Different from the Fair Price

But more generally, what if you set a buyback price that was different from the fair price? Here's how you'd compute who gains and who loses from that decision. If you're given a buyback price, here's what you should do. You should first estimate how many shares you will buy back at that buyback price. So take the dollar debt you take on. Divide by the buyback price. That will tell you how many shares you can buy back because you're using the borrowed money to buy back the shares. Second, look at how much of the increase in value you gave to the shareholders who sold their shares back by taking the premium you paid on the buyback price. Whatever's left over is what's going to be due to your remaining shareholders.

So let's take an extreme scenario. Let's assume you were able to buy shares back at the old price, $67.71. If you are able to buy the shares back at the old price, here's what will happen. Out of the 1.8 million shares, you're going to buy back shares using the $39,175 million new debt at a $67.71 price. That's going to give you 1,221.43 million shares leftover after the buyback. You say, "What do I do with that?" Hold on. Remember, at the new cost of capital, your enterprise value is going to be $153.53 billion. You add cash. You subtract out the new debt from it. The new value for your equity is going to be $102.326 billion. You divide that by the remaining shares outstanding. The value per share you're going to get, if you're one of those who does not sell your shares back, will be $83.78.

Think of what's happened here. You still have the same increase in value of $19 billion plus, but now, instead of having to share that increase in value with all the shareholders in the company, you have to share it only among the shareholders leftover after the buyback. If you're able to buy the shares back at less than the breakeven price... remember, the breakeven price was $78.61... the shareholders who sell their shares back will get a smaller share of the spoils, but the shareholders left behind will get a bigger share. So if we can buy back shares at the low price, do so, but here's what tends to happen more frequently. Let's say instead of paying $78.61, you'd paid $84 or $86. Then, you're going to end up with the reverse. The people who sell their shares back will end up with a larger portion of the premium, and those who hold on will be punished.

In fact, here's the proof that if you paid $78.61, the breakeven price, that everybody's going to end up with the same share. At a $78.61 price, you're going to end up with 1,301.65 million shares outstanding. If you look and take the value of the equity, $102.326 million, and divide by the remaining shares, 1,301.65 million, you end up at $78.61. The people who sell their shares back get $78.61. The people leftover get $78.61. To me, that's a magic number in buybacks, that breakeven number. So that, so far, answering the first question. Why should we do it? About $10.90 per share.

## What If Something Goes Wrong? Operating Income Sensitivity

Let's turn to the second question. What if something goes wrong? You're saying, "What are you worried about?" Well, remember, I'm borrowing money based on operating income, right? And that operating income can go up or down. Now, I could approach this purely statistically by looking at how much the operating income has varied in the past, but I think it's much better to approach it intuitively. Look at what's hurt your company in the past. Maybe, if it's a cyclical company, it could be a recession. If it's a defense contractor, it'd be loss of a contract. And then, ask yourself, "What'll happen to your optimal debt ratio if something bad happens?" So one way to ask, "What if something goes wrong?" is to change your operating income and see how much it changes your optimal debt ratio. The other is to put a constraint on your rating. Remember when I said that your optimal debt ratio is the one at which your cost of capital is minimized? I never checked your rating. For all you know, your cost of capital could be minimized when your rating is BB or even B, and many companies will find that unacceptable, having their rating drop below an investment grade, so let's try both on Disney.

To answer the first question, "What if something goes wrong?" I looked at Disney's operating income. Why? Because I wanted to see what a bad year for Disney looked like. Looking from 1987 through 2013, the worst year that Disney has had was 2000, where its operating income dropped almost 30%. That gave me a sense of what a bad year for Disney looks like. In fact, I looked at the last few recessions to see how much Disney was affected, and it looks like it's operating income is hurt between 16% to 30%, depending on the depth of the recession. So, at least, I have a sense of what a bad year will look like for Disney.

Here's what I did next. I kept everything in my spreadsheet fixed for Disney and changed the operating income. I dropped it by 10%, 20%, 30%. You know what I was checking to see? Would my optimal, which was 40% with my existing operating income, drop off quickly? Because if it dropped to 20%... the optimal from 40% to 20%... if my operating income drops by 10%, that's a danger sign. In the case of Disney, there seems to be a tremendous buffer built in. I can drop the operating income by 30%, and nothing happens to my optimal debt ratio. What does that tell me? Disney has enough safety built into its operating income that it can afford to go to 40% and not be worried that if it has a bad year, terrible things are going to happen to it as a company.

## Rating Constraints and Their Cost

Here's the other way I can bring in what-if analyses into my optimal debt ratio. Most companies that I talked to have a rating below which they don't want to drop, a bond rating. That rating could be investment grade. For many companies, BBB, or higher for others. That rating constraint is driven by sensible considerations. One is that if your rating drops below investment grade, those indirect bankruptcy costs we talk about could kick in, and therefore, companies want to avoid it. But when you have a rating constraint, you're going to give up something, and one of the things you should always do when you impose a constraint is cost out that constraint. In other words, when a company says, "I want to be AA at any cost," rise to the challenge. Give them the cost, and here's a simple way to do it.

The way I computed my optimal is I did not look at ratings. At the optimal debt ratio of 40%, it turns out that my synthetic rating for Disney is A. Let's assume that managers at Disney insist on a AA rating. Don't ask me why, but let's say they did. The optimal debt ratio for Disney if I impose that constraint has to be 30%. They can't go to 40% because that puts their rating at A, and at a AA rating, their value as a company is $147.8 billion. That's about $5.7 billion lower than their unconstrained value. The cost of having a AA rating is $5.7 billion. If they insist on a AAA rating, I'd have to stop at 20% debt, and if I do that, I lose about $12.1 billion in value. The cost of having a AAA rating is $12.1 billion. So when a company says, "You cannot be too conservative," the answer is, "Yes, you can." If you borrow too little, you might be leaving $10 billion, $15 billion, or $20 billion of value on the table, tax benefits that you could have claimed for your stockholders that you chose not to. So what if something goes wrong? Either change the operating income or put in a rating constraint, which brings me to my third and final question.

## Taking Projects Instead of Buying Back Stock

What if instead of buying back stock, I wanted to take projects instead? Would my optimal be different if I took projects? The short answer is no, if you stay in the same business mix. For Disney, if the plan is they're going to build more theme parks, expand broadcasting, stay in the movie business, their 40% debt ratio will hold because all you're doing is scaling the company up. But if you're going to go into different businesses... Let's say Disney wants to borrow money and buy Twitter. Well, my optimal will be different. Why? Because I'm entering a risky business with negative cash flows, my optimal debt ratio will decrease. What does this mean? Don't give managers a blank check. Just because of excess debt capacity doesn't mean they can use it to do whatever they want. You need to tell me what your plans are on investing before I can tell you what will happen to your optimal.

So now that we've completed all the loose ends, do so for your company as well. You got the optimal for your company. Try asking some what-if questions. "What if my operating income drops? What if I put in a rating constraint?" Hey? Also, look at whether your company has plans to expand and grow. See what that effect will be because those are questions you need to get answered before you decide to jump, especially to a higher debt ratio.
