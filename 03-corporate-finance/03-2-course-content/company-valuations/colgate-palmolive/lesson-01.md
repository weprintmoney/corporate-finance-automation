# Colgate-Palmolive (CL) — Lesson 01: Valuation: The Big Picture / What is Corporate Finance?

### Lesson 01 — Valuation: The Big Picture / What is Corporate Finance?

- **Lesson 01, Session 1 · Part 1 — "The Financial Balance Sheet and the Singular Objective of Maximizing Firm Value"** ([transcript](../../01-foundations-and-discount-rates/lesson-01/session-1-part-1.md))
    - Quote: "You want to make your business the most valuable business you can."
    - Question for Colgate-Palmolive: Split Colgate-Palmolive's balance sheet into assets in place and growth assets. In the base year, how did capital allocation (R&D, capital spending, acquisitions) divide between defending the existing business and funding growth, and does that split match what management says the strategy is?
    - Sources needed: FY2025 advertising, R&D, capital spending and acquisition cash; dividends and buybacks; growth by segment; the stated strategy.
    - Where to find: `sources/filings/cl-10k-fy2025.txt` (cash flow statement, notes 3, 14 and 17, MD&A); ledger `company.rd_expense`, `company.capex`, `company.history`, `governance.objectives`, `facts.uses-of-cash-2025`, `facts.sales-drivers-2025`; model-output.md §5 and §8.
    - Answer: No filing splits Colgate into existing business and growth, so the split is a judgment made from where the cash went. Almost all of the value is assets in place: brands that already exist and already earn 34.56% on invested capital against an 8.34% cost of capital (model-output.md §8). The 2025 spending says the same. Defending the existing business: advertising of $2,703M, 13.3% of net sales, and capital spending of $564M, which is below depreciation and amortization of $630M (`facts.uses-of-cash-2025`, `company.capex`, `company.da`). Funding growth: research and development of $366M, 1.8% of net sales (`company.rd_expense`), and one acquisition, Prime100, a fresh pet food business in Australia, for $293M net of cash. Research, capital spending and the acquisition together were $1,223M. Dividends and buybacks were $3,033M, 2.5 times that (`facts.uses-of-cash-2025`; model-output.md §5). Does it match the strategy? Only partly. The 10-K opens by calling Colgate a "caring, innovative growth company" and the stated aim is "driving organic sales growth" (`governance.objectives`). Net sales grew 1.4% in 2025, and all of it was price: selling prices rose 2.1% while volume fell 0.4% (`facts.sales-drivers-2025`). The money says mature cash generator; the words say growth company. The cash is the better evidence.

- **Lesson 01, Session 1 · Part 2 — "Companies Age Like Human Beings — Placing the Company on the Corporate Life Cycle"** ([transcript](../../01-foundations-and-discount-rates/lesson-01/session-1-part-2.md))
    - Quote: "The third theme is that companies, just like human beings, age."
    - Question for Colgate-Palmolive: Where does Colgate-Palmolive sit on the corporate life cycle, judged by revenue growth, margins, reinvestment, and how it uses cash? Does management's behaviour (acquisitions, payouts, any sale of the business) agree with that placement, or is the company refusing to act its age?
    - Sources needed: Ten years of sales and operating income; reinvestment; payouts against free cash flow to equity; the acquisition record.
    - Where to find: ledger `facts.growth-and-margin-history`, `facts.acquisitions-2018-2025`, `facts.writedowns-2021-2025`, `facts.payout-policy-2025`, `company.history`; model-output.md §5 and §8; `module-1-cross-checks-output.md` §6.
    - Answer: Mature, and mostly acting like it. Growth: net sales grew 3.3% a year from 2016 to 2025, and operating income before write-downs grew 0.7% a year (`facts.growth-and-margin-history`). Margins: high but slipping, from 24.0% in 2017 to 20.7% in 2025 before write-downs. Reinvestment: negative in each of the last three years, a reinvestment rate of −12.94% in 2025, because capital spending runs below depreciation and working capital releases cash (model-output.md §8). Cash use: $9,438M returned over 2023 to 2025 against free cash flow to equity of $8,204M (model-output.md §5), with the dividend raised again in 2025 and a new $5 billion buyback authorized (`facts.payout-policy-2025`). Those are the habits of a mature company and they fit. The one place Colgate has not acted its age is acquisitions. It spent $3,894M on deals from 2018 to 2025 and wrote down $2,211M of goodwill and intangibles over the same years, 57% of what it spent (`facts.acquisitions-2018-2025`). The 2025 charge of $919M was on the skin health business, mainly Filorga, where growth in China fell short (`facts.skin-health-writedown-2025`). That is a mature company paying for growth it could not produce itself, and losing. It is why the owner's decision on operating income matters: the $919M is added back to get base operating income of $4,225M, because it is a non-cash charge, but the $2,211M is put back into invested capital so the failed deals still count against returns (`company.ebit` and `company.invested_capital` notes). The alternative was to ignore the write-downs on both sides, which would flatter returns.

## Module 1 Step — Questions for This Lesson

- **Pick a company**
  - Which company, and why is it a good subject (data available, decisions to judge)? Colgate-Palmolive Company (NYSE: CL), type `public`, base year FY2025, valued as of 2026-11-20 (`meta`). Data: two annual reports, the latest quarterly report, the 2026 proxy, twelve years of filed figures, twenty Capital IQ pages, and a clean five-year price history (`sources/README.md`). Decisions to judge: buybacks and dividends that have run book equity down to $54M (`company.equity_book`); $3,894M of acquisitions with $2,211M written down; debt at 9.34% of value against a 30% optimum (model-output.md §1 and §3); two businesses with different risk; and about two-thirds of sales outside the United States (`country.exposures`).
  - Where does it sit on the corporate life cycle, judged by growth, margins, reinvestment and cash use? Mature: 3.3% sales growth a year over nine years, a 20.7% operating margin before write-downs, negative reinvestment, and more cash returned than free cash flow to equity. See Part 2.

## Notes

This lesson fixes the frame: Colgate is a mature company with very high returns on a small capital base, slow growth that now comes from price, and a payout that takes all the free cash flow. The owner's decision on operating income is recorded here because every later return figure rests on it: base operating income is $4,225M (reported $3,306M plus the $919M write-down), restructuring and litigation charges stay in because Colgate books them most years (`facts.operating-profit-reconciliation-2025`, `facts.restructuring-program-2025`), and $2,211M of write-downs goes back into invested capital. The alternative, the company's own figure of $4,347M that also strips litigation and restructuring, was rejected (`facts.operating-profit-excluding-items-2025`). Lesson 3 tests whether the growth target is being met in ways that add value. Lessons 13 and 14 use the 34.56% return. Lessons 22 to 26 start from "returns everything it earns."

One wording point for a later correction pass: the `company.ebit` note in the ledger calls the litigation charge "the 65 ERISA litigation charge." Inside operating profit the charge is $99M; $65M is the figure net of a $34M benefit booked below operating profit (`facts.operating-profit-reconciliation-2025`). The value 4,225 is unaffected.
