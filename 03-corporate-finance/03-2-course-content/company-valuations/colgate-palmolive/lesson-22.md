# Colgate-Palmolive (CL) — Lesson 22: Moving to the Optimal

### Lesson 22 — Moving to the Optimal

- **Lesson 22, Session 22 · Part 1 — "Under-Levered vs. Over-Levered: Takeover Risk, Bankruptcy Threat, and the Good Projects Test"** ([transcript](../../03-financing-mix-and-dividends/lesson-22/session-22-part-1.md))
    - Quote: "Having too little debt might make you the target of a hostile acquisition."
    - Question for Colgate-Palmolive: Is Colgate-Palmolive under- or over-levered? If under, is it a takeover target (past returns, size, ownership)? If over, how close is distress? How good are its available projects, and what is the best path and speed to the optimum - recapitalize, or change financing of future projects?
    - Sources needed: Actual and optimal debt ratios; size, ownership, takeover defenses, stock performance; returns on capital; uses of cash.
    - Where to find: model-output.md §1, §3, §5 and §8; ledger `facts.cost-of-capital-grid`, `governance.controlling_holders`, `facts.holders-capital-iq`, `facts.change-in-control-terms`, `facts.board-structure-2026`, `facts.shareholder-return-2020-2025`, `facts.jensens-alpha-estimate`, `facts.dividends-xlsx-results`, `facts.acquisitions-2018-2025`, `facts.payout-policy-2025`.
    - Answer: The lecture's decision tree has three questions. Colgate's answers:

        | Question | Answer | Evidence |
        |---|---|---|
        | Under- or over-levered? | Under | 9.34% against 30% unconstrained and 20% recommended (lessons 18, 19, 21) |
        | Under threat of a takeover? | No, but exposed to an activist | Below |
        | Good projects available? | Good returns on what it has; few new places to put money | Below |

      The tree's conclusion for a company that is under-levered, not under immediate threat, and short of good new projects: move gradually, and use the borrowed money to return cash to shareholders, not to invest.
      Takeover threat. A buyer would need about $78.5 billion for the equity before any premium. Few can pay that, and none could do it with borrowed money: lesson 18 shows the business cannot carry even 40% debt. So a buyout is not a realistic threat. Colgate also has no structural shield to make one harder: no poison pill, no classified board, one vote per share, and shareholders holding 15% can call a meeting (`facts.change-in-control-terms`, `facts.board-structure-2026`).
      An activist is the realistic version of the threat. The case would be simple to state: over five years to December 2025 the shares returned 4.0% in total against 96.2% for the S&P 500, and trailed the company's own chosen peer group (`facts.shareholder-return-2020-2025`); the balance sheet has room; and $2,211M has been written off acquisitions. Against that, 87% of the shares sit with institutions, the three largest of which are index managers who rarely lead campaigns, and the largest holder Capital IQ tags as an activist owns 0.05% (`facts.holders-capital-iq`). Our reading: no pressure today, and a real possibility if the shares keep lagging.
      The stock evidence is mixed, and we say so. The regression in lesson 8 gives a small positive Jensen's alpha, about 2.1% a year, because it measures Colgate against a beta of 0.315 (`facts.jensens-alpha-estimate`). The professor's dividend workbook, which uses the bottom-up beta of 0.7321, shows a negative alpha in four of the five years from 2021 to 2025 (`facts.dividends-xlsx-results`). Same returns, different yardstick. Shareholders comparing Colgate with the market or its peers see underperformance.

## Cumulative Project — Questions for This Lesson

- **Is your firm under, over or correctly levered?**
  Under-levered. Debt is 9.34% of market value. The cost of capital approach and the APV approach both put the unconstrained optimum at 30%, the industry is at about 19%, and our constrained recommendation is 20% (lessons 18 to 21). On any of those, Colgate has roughly half the debt it should or less. Confidence in the direction is high; in the size, moderate.

- **If under levered, is it under threat of a hostile acquisition? If over levered, how imminent is the bankruptcy threat?**
  No threat of acquisition, mainly because of size: $78.5 billion of equity. It has few formal defenses, and it does not need them. The threat it is exposed to is a shareholder campaign over weak returns and an under-used balance sheet. None is visible in the ownership data as of September 2026. Bankruptcy is not a question: coverage is 15.82 times (model-output.md §2).

- **How good are the potential projects that the company can invest in?**
  This needs the distinction from lesson 14. The existing business is excellent: a 34.56% return on capital against an 8.34% cost (model-output.md §8). But that is the return on brands built over decades, and the question is about new money.
    - Organic projects earn well and are small. Capital spending is $564M a year, less than depreciation, and reinvestment has been negative in each of the last three years (model-output.md §8). The business does not have a queue of projects waiting for funding; it already generates more cash than it can use.
    - Acquisitions are where large sums can go, and the record is poor: $3,894M spent from 2018 to 2025 and $2,211M written down over the same period (`facts.acquisitions-2018-2025`).
  So the answer is: good projects, but not enough of them to absorb $9 billion of new borrowing. Borrowed money pushed into acquisitions would likely earn less than it costs.

- **What is the best path that you see for your firm to get from its actual to its optimal debt ratio?**
  Borrow gradually and return the cash through special dividends, over about five years, for as long as the share price is above its estimated value. This is a recommendation, with alternatives.
    - Target: 20%, which needs $9,226M of new debt, taking the total from $8,084M to $17,310M (`facts.cost-of-capital-grid`).
    - Pace: about $1,845M a year for five years. That is 61% of the $3,033M Colgate already returns to shareholders each year.
    - Use of the money: special dividends, not buybacks. The valuation puts a share at $61.83 against a price of $98.71 (`facts.professor-spreadsheet-results`; lessons 29 to 34). Buying shares back above their value moves wealth from the holders who stay to the ones who sell, so borrowing to buy at this price would undo part of the gain from the new debt. A special dividend pays every holder the same. The regular dividend stays where it is, because a regular dividend raised with borrowed money is hard to bring back down (lesson 25). Colgate has a buyback program of up to $5 billion authorized in March 2025 (`facts.payout-policy-2025`); buybacks become the better route only if the price falls to or below the estimated value. This choice is a judgment that rests on the valuation being right.
    - Why gradual and not a one-time recapitalization: nothing forces speed. There is no bidder. A single $9 billion payout would test the rating agencies' reaction all at once. Spreading it lets Colgate stop if S&P's response is harsher than the synthetic table suggests, which lesson 19 gives reason to expect.
    - Why not invest the money instead: the acquisition record above.
  The alternatives. Going to 30% would add $17,881M of debt; rejected for the reasons in lesson 19. Doing nothing is the path Colgate is on, and it costs between $2.26 a share (our 20% target) and $3.86 a share (the full optimum) in value left with the tax authorities (`facts.cost-of-capital-grid`; model-output.md §3). That is 2% to 4% of the share price: worth having, not urgent.
  One consequence to state plainly. For the five years of the move, Colgate would return more cash than its free cash flow to equity, on purpose. Lesson 26 finds that it has returned about what it generated; this path changes that, and the difference is deliberate borrowing, not overpayment.

## Notes

The conclusion of the capital structure module for Colgate: under-levered, not under threat, with strong returns on existing assets and few good uses for new money. The recommended path is slow and uses special dividends while the price is above the estimated value. The whole exercise is worth a few percent of the share price, so it belongs in the report as a secondary finding; the valuation turns on the cost of capital and on how long the excess returns last, not on this.

What would change the recommendation: an acquisition large enough to use the debt capacity (then the question becomes the price paid); a sustained fall in operating income (the optimum drops to 21% with income 20% lower, model-output.md §4, and the target should drop with it); or evidence that S&P would rate Colgate BBB at 20%, in which case the target should be lower.
