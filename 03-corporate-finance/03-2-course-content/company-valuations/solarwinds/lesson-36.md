# SolarWinds (SWI) — Lesson 36: Course Synthesis

### Lesson 36 — Course Synthesis

- **Lesson 36, Session 36 · Part 1 — "Ten Lessons Revisited: Governance, Risk, and the Marginal Investor"** ([transcript](../../04-dividends-and-valuation/lesson-36/session-36-part-1.md))
    - Quote: "Not everyone will agree on every decision being the best decision from their interests."
    - Question for SWI: Applying the four-stakeholder-conflict lens (stockholders / bondholders / managers / society) to the SUNBURST crisis and the subsequent Turn/River sale — whose interests actually drove the sale decision (Silver Lake's exit-value maximization vs. public minority stockholders vs. customers/society still exposed by the original breach) — and does REPO-SWI-VAL's existing governance write-up already answer this, or leave it open?
    - Sources needed: the merger proxy's "background of the merger" narrative, board voting record, and any dissenting-shareholder or appraisal-rights litigation over the Turn/River deal.
    - Where to find: SEC-EDGAR — SWI DEFM14A merger proxy / Schedule 13E-3 ("Background of the Merger" section); REPO-SWI-VAL (Steps 2–3 already cover governance and stated objectives — check before redoing); NEWS-WEB (any shareholder-litigation coverage of the deal).
    - Answer: The controlling block drove it, structurally and provably — but the process was cleaner than Step 2 of `valuation-report.md` implies, and that write-up leaves the question open on two counts. First, the correct source is `swi-defm14c-2025-merger-information-statement.md` (a Schedule 14C information statement; no DEFM14A or SC 13E-3 exists for this deal), and it shows the Principal Stockholders — nine Thoma Bravo funds and four Silver Lake vehicles — holding 111,564,519 shares, ~**65%** of voting power, not the ~75% Silver Lake alone the repo asserts; they executed a DGCL §228 written consent on 2025-02-07 within hours of signing, so the ~35% float never voted and there was no majority-of-the-minority condition, only DGCL §262 appraisal rights. Second, the record shows a genuine double market check that Step 2 doesn't cover: 13 parties contacted in the 2023 Initial Market Check, nine NDAs, eight management presentations, and bids of ~$14.00 cut to $13.00 (Party A), $12.00 (Party B) and a third, all withdrawn in November 2023 citing subscription-transition risk; then 20 financial and 14 strategic parties canvassed February 2024–January 2025; then Turn/River at $17.00–18.00 negotiated up to $18.50 with a 30-day window-shop, an independent three-director Transaction Committee, and two fairness opinions. Customers and society — the ~18,000 SUNBURST-exposed organizations — had no seat at all, and SWI's indemnity for every Cyber Incident liability simply travelled with it through the N-able separation agreement. Verdict: the sale served the 65% block, and the float got a fair price by process rather than by power.

- **Lesson 36, Session 36 · Part 2 — "The Trade-Off on Debt and the Three First Principles of Corporate Finance"** ([transcript](../../04-dividends-and-valuation/lesson-36/session-36-part-2.md))
    - Quote: "If you cannot find investments that make your hurdle rate, find a way to get the cash back to your stockholders."
    - Question for SWI: Tying together all three first principles for SWI's final public year (FY2024) — did its investment returns (ROIC) clear its hurdle rate (WACC, per REPO-SWI-VAL Step 12), was its capital structure near an optimal debt/equity mix (or over/under-levered given its LBO legacy debt), and did its dividend/buyback policy correctly reflect the answers to those first two questions — and does that three-part synthesis explain why Silver Lake chose to sell rather than keep running SWI as a public company?
    - Sources needed: SWI's ROIC vs. WACC (investment principle), actual vs. optimal debt ratio (financing principle), and dividend/buyback history vs. FCFE (dividend principle) — a full synthesis across all three, most of which is not yet built.
    - Where to find: REPO-SWI-VAL (WACC and capital structure at Step 12 is the one piece already done; ROIC, optimal-debt-ratio, and dividend/FCFE analysis are not yet in the file and would need fresh work); SEC-EDGAR — SWI 10-K FY2024 full financials; DEAL-DOCS (Turn/River deal rationale as the real-world "answer key").
    - Answer: Fail, pass, pass — and that combination is precisely why the right answer was to sell. **Investment:** ROIC of 6.7% in FY2024 ($156.3M after-tax EBIT ÷ $2,348.0M invested capital), 4.8% in FY2023 and negative in FY2021–22, against the 10.33% WACC from Step 12 of `valuation-report.md` — four consecutive years of value destruction, driven mostly by the $2,363.2M of goodwill Silver Lake and Thoma Bravo themselves created in the 2016 take-private, which no operating execution could earn a return on. **Financing:** broadly right — market debt at 26–32% of capital against a WACC-minimizing ratio of roughly 30% (levering Damodaran's 1.2482 unlevered beta past ~40% drops interest coverage below 1.5x and pushes WACC above 11.5%), and the $664.4M of 2022 repayments moved SWI toward that point, not away from it. **Dividends:** also right — with ROIC below WACC the prescription is to return cash, and SWI returned $405.4M in two special dividends ($237.2M in 2021, $168.2M in 2024) without borrowing to fund either, with the 2024 payout matching FY2024 FCFE of $167.8M almost to the dollar. A firm that cannot clear its hurdle rate, is already optimally levered, and is already distributing its free cash flow has no internal path to compounding value; the only lever left is a change of owner. Turn/River's answer — re-lever to ~$2.75B of committed debt (7.2x adjusted EBITDA) and reset the equity basis — raises IRR without raising ROIC, which is exactly the trade Damodaran warns is pricing rather than value creation.

## Cumulative Project — Questions for This Lesson

- **What is the narrative that you would use to describe your company, as its run today?**
  SolarWinds, as of its final public year, is a mature, asset-light IT-monitoring software company finishing a slow, largely defensive transition from perpetual licenses to subscription SaaS — high-margin (48.3% adjusted EBITDA), high-retention (99% net retention, 97% maintenance renewal), and almost entirely unreinvested (a 1.6% reinvestment rate against 93.5% recurring revenue), because its controlling PE sponsors correctly judged that internal reinvestment opportunities didn't clear the firm's own cost of capital. Every first-principles test this project has run agrees: investment returns never cleared WACC in 2021–2024 (Lesson 27/36 Part 2's "fail"), financing was close enough to its own optimum that no large value was left on the table by staying there (Lesson 19), and dividend policy correctly reflected both — two special dividends totaling $405.4M returned cash the business couldn't productively use, funded from cash balances rather than new debt (Lessons 24–27). With growth capped near 5% (not the 8.1% management's own long-range plan later assumed), no internal lever left to compound value, and a governance structure that left minority shareholders with appraisal rights rather than a vote (Lessons 2, 33), the only remaining move for a controlling sponsor sitting on a sub-hurdle-return asset was a change of owner — which is exactly what happened. Turn/River's answer since April 2025 is a different story: re-lever to roughly 58%–60% of capital (raising, not lowering, the cost of capital per Lesson 34 Part 2), keep management's more aggressive 8.1% growth plan, and bet the return on resetting the equity basis and IRR rather than on raising ROIC — the same "pricing, not value creation" trade this course's first principles warn against, now running under new ownership rather than old.

## Notes

The final lesson pulls together everything the project built: the four-stakeholder-conflict lens applied to SUNBURST and the sale (Part 1), and the three first-principles tests — investment, financing, dividend — applied to SWI's last public year (Part 2). Part 1 delivers the project's last correction to the ownership-concentration error (Silver Lake and Thoma Bravo together at ~65% of voting power, not Silver Lake alone at ~75% — see [`README.md`](README.md) for the full detail) and documents a genuine two-round market check that undercuts any simple "controller expropriated the float" story. Part 2's fail/pass/pass verdict — ROIC never cleared WACC, financing was near-optimal, dividends correctly returned excess cash — is the cleanest single summary of why Silver Lake sold: with no internal lever left to compound value, a change of owner was the only remaining option, and Turn/River's re-leveraging plan raises IRR without raising ROIC, exactly the pricing-versus-value-creation trap this course spends its first principles warning against. Every earlier lesson in this project feeds one of these three tests — Lessons 14/27 for ROIC, Lessons 17–21 for financing, Lessons 25–26 for dividends — so this file is best read as the project's conclusion rather than a standalone answer.
