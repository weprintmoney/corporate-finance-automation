# SolarWinds (SWI) — Lesson 15: Time Weighting Cash Flows

### Lesson 15 — Time Weighting Cash Flows

- **Lesson 15, Session 15 · Part 1 — "Sunk Costs, Non-Incremental G&A, and Time-Weighting Cash Flows"** ([transcript](../../02-investment-returns-and-financing/lesson-15/session-15-part-1.md))
    - Quote: "The first rule in capital budgeting is you have sunk cost, money were already spent, don't consider them."
    - Question for SolarWinds: For SolarWinds' typical project (an acquisition, a product line, a plant), which costs already spent are sunk, and which allocated overheads are truly incremental? What does a typical project look like in life, investment needs and cash-flow pattern?
    - Sources needed: Capex, R&D and goodwill; the non-GAAP "acquisition and other costs" line; the segment note (to see whether overhead is allocated); revenue mix and contract terms.
    - Where to find: `company.capex`, `company.rd_expense`, `company.goodwill`, `company.history`, `company.deferred_revenue`; `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md` (MD&A non-GAAP reconciliation, Note 16, Item 7A).
    - Answer: SolarWinds' typical project is not a plant. It is either a subscription customer (a contract the company sells and then renews) or a bought product that is folded into the observability platform. The expensive part of both has already been spent.
        - **Sunk.** The $2,363.2M of goodwill (`company.goodwill`) is what the 2016 buyout paid above the book value of the assets. Of it, $891.1M of the 2022 goodwill impairment is already written off (`company.history`, 2022 note). None of it changes what a new project costs or earns, so it drops out of any project decision. So does the cost of the 2020 SUNBURST cyber incident (lesson 16). The "acquisition and other costs" line, which holds legal and integration fees on deals, was $1.145M in FY2024, $2.612M in FY2023 and $0.540M in FY2022 (facts.acquisition-and-other-costs-2022-2024). It is small enough that sunk or not makes no difference.
        - **Overhead.** SolarWinds reports one operating segment, because its chief executive manages the business as a whole (10-K Note 16). General and administrative expense of $125.8M (facts.ga-expense-2024) is therefore never allocated to products. Most of it would exist whether or not any one project went ahead: public-company costs, finance, legal, and the SEC lawsuit over the cyber disclosures. Test each item with "what happens if I take it / what happens if I don't". Only the sales, support and hosting spend that rises with a project is incremental. Public-company costs ended when the company was taken private on 2025-04-16.
        - **Life, investment and cash-flow pattern.** A subscription contract runs one year or more ("annual and multi-year maintenance contracts and subscriptions", 10-K Item 7A) and renews at a high rate (lesson 20 gives the rates). Customers pay in advance, which is why deferred revenue is $389.3M (`company.deferred_revenue`). Revenue is booked evenly over the term, so cash arrives before accounting revenue. The investment is spread thin: R&D of $108.6M a year (`company.rd_expense`), expensed as incurred, and capex of $20.5M (`company.capex`) on revenue of $796.9M (`company.revenue`). There is no large up-front outlay followed by a payback period. The project is cash-positive from the first invoice, and the real question is how long the customer stays.

- **Lesson 15, Session 15 · Part 2 — "NPV, IRR, and Terminal Value via the Growing Perpetuity"** ([transcript](../../02-investment-returns-and-financing/lesson-15/session-15-part-2.md))
    - Quote: "If you take a project with an NPV of $3.3 billion, your value will increase by $3.3 billion."
    - Question for SolarWinds: What terminal growth rate is defensible for SolarWinds, and does any disclosed valuation of it (a fairness opinion, an analyst model, management's plan) use a growth rate above the risk-free rate or the economy's growth?
    - Sources needed: The terminal growth in the ledger; the risk-free rate; the banks' rates in the merger document; management's forecast growth.
    - Where to find: `market.terminal_growth`, `market.risk_free_rate`, `forecast.management_growth`, `forecast.analyst_growth` (omitted); `sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md` (Opinion of Goldman Sachs, Opinion of Jefferies); model-output.md §6.
    - Answer: 2.5% is defensible (`market.terminal_growth`), against a risk-free rate of 4.43% (`market.risk_free_rate`). The rule is that a company cannot grow forever faster than the economy, and the risk-free rate is the proxy for the economy's nominal growth. Neither bank broke it.
        - Goldman Sachs used perpetuity growth of 2% to 3% with discount rates of 9% to 11% (facts.gs-perpetuity-growth-range, facts.goldman-dcf-discount-rate-range). Jefferies used 2.5% to 3.5% with 10.70% to 11.70% (facts.jefferies-perpetuity-growth-range, facts.jefferies-dcf-discount-rate-range). The ledger's 2.5% is the midpoint of Goldman's range and the low end of Jefferies'. No analyst consensus loaded (`meta.omitted`, `forecast.analyst_growth`).
        - The growth that is above the risk-free rate is in the explicit period. Management's plan takes revenue from $796.9M in 2024 to $1,273.0M in 2030, 8.12% a year (`forecast.management_growth`). That is fine for six years and not fine for ever.
        - The tail has a different problem. Jefferies applied the growth rate to the terminal-year cash flow. That is the "FCFF-growth" method in model-output.md §6, which gives a terminal value of $5,552.5M against $3,808.9M under consistent reinvestment. Management's 2030 free cash flow to the firm is $419.0M (`forecast.years`, 2030). Its 2030 after-tax operating income is $511.0M × (1 − 25%) = $383.3M. A company that grows forever must reinvest to do it. Growing a cash flow that is larger than after-tax earnings assumes no reinvestment at all. That is the largest judgment in the valuation, and lesson 32 returns to it.

## Cumulative Project — Questions for This Lesson

- **If you can identify a typical project for your firm, what pattern of cash flows do you foresee for the project?**
  A subscription customer. Cash comes in before the revenue is recognized, because customers pay annually or multi-annually in advance (deferred revenue $389.3M, `company.deferred_revenue`). Costs are small and spread out: hosting and support to serve the customer, and the sales cost to win or renew the contract. The platform investment sits in R&D of $108.6M (`company.rd_expense`) and capex of $20.5M (`company.capex`) a year, both low against revenue of $796.9M (`company.revenue`). The 2024 history row shows the same pattern at company level: capex of $20.5M against D&A of $74.4M (`company.history`, 2024). The pattern has three phases: the first invoice (cash in), ratable recognition over the term, and renewal. Value depends on the renewal rate and the price at renewal, not on a payback period. The acquisition version of a typical project is the opposite: a large outlay up front (the $2,363.2M of goodwill came from one) followed by cash flows that must earn the cost of capital of 10.23% (model-output.md §1) to avoid an impairment, as the 2022 write-down shows.

## Notes

This lesson set the frame for the later cash-flow work. Sunk costs (goodwill, the 2022 impairment) are ignored, and overhead is not allocated because the company has one segment. The typical project is a recurring contract with a low capital need. The terminal growth rate of 2.5% is below the risk-free rate of 4.43% and inside both banks' ranges, so the rate is not the problem. The problem is the terminal cash flow that gets grown, and that is quantified in model-output.md §6 and decided in lesson 32. No acquisition-specific sunk-cost question is asked: the Squadcast purchase (March 2025, per Capital IQ) falls after the analysis date and is in no filing.
