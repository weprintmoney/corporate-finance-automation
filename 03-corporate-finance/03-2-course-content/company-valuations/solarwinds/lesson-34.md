# SolarWinds (SWI) — Lesson 34: Value of Control

### Lesson 34 — Value of Control

- **Lesson 34, Session 34 · Part 1 — "Stories Into Numbers: Building the Full DCF Valuation"** ([transcript](../../04-dividends-and-valuation/lesson-34/session-34-part-1.md))
    - Quote: "A good valuation is a bridge between stories and numbers."
    - Question for SolarWinds: What is the status-quo story for SolarWinds (how it will grow, what margins it will earn, how much it must reinvest, how risky it is) and what intrinsic value per share does the full DCF produce from that story? How does it compare with the market price or the last transaction?
    - Sources needed: Management's 2025–2030 forecast, cost of capital, terminal assumptions, debt, cash, share count, unaffected and deal prices.
    - Where to find: `forecast.years`, `forecast.growth_period_years`, `forecast.stable_beta`, `forecast.stable_debt_ratio`, `forecast.stable_roic`, `market.terminal_growth`, `company.*`; model-output.md §1 and §6; `sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md`.
    - Answer: $15.66 a share (model-output.md §6, course method). The story:
        - Growth. A mature IT-monitoring business finishing its move from perpetual licences to subscriptions. Management's plan takes revenue from $832.0M in 2025 to $1,273.0M in 2030 (8.12% a year from 2024's $796.9M; `forecast.years`, `forecast.management_growth`). History is 5.0% a year, so the plan is the optimistic part of the story.
        - Margins. EBIT margin rises from 32.69% to 40.14% (`forecast.years`). The plan defines EBIT as adjusted EBITDA less stock compensation less D&A; on that basis 2024's margin was about 29.1% (29.11%, `forecast.stress_cases`, §6 stress cases), and the GAAP 26.2% ($208.4M ÷ $796.9M, my arithmetic) is not comparable with the plan's figures.
        - Reinvestment. Free cash flow after stock compensation goes from $212.0M to $419.0M. Inside the plan reinvestment is thin; after 2030 we force it to 30.38% of after-tax EBIT (g ÷ stable ROIC = 2.5% ÷ 8.23%, `forecast.stable_roic`, lesson 32).
        - Risk. Unlevered beta 1.22, levered 1.6714 on 33.03% debt; cost of equity 12.65%; cost of capital 10.67% for 2025–2030 (model-output.md §1). From 2031 the firm is stable: beta 1.00, debt 21%, pre-tax cost of debt 5.38% (the A- rate the §3 schedule assigns at 21%, not the 8.85% B- rate used to 2030), cost of capital 8.23% (§6, lesson 32).
        - Arithmetic. Present value of 2025–2030 cash flows $1,303.2M plus present value of terminal value $2,596.7M gives enterprise value $3,899.8M. Less debt $1,285.0M, plus cash $259.3M, is $2,874.1M; less the $187.0M equity claim is $2,687.1M; over 171.6M basic shares is $15.66.
        - Against prices. $15.66 is 1.03x the $15.18 unaffected price and 0.85x the $18.50 deal price (§6). It sits inside both banks' DCF ranges, in the lower part of Goldman Sachs' $14.30 to $23.70 and of Jefferies' $14.70 to $19.70 (`facts.gs-dcf-range`, `facts.jefferies-dcf-range`). The market price was 3% below intrinsic value under this story (15.18 ÷ 15.66 − 1, my arithmetic). It sat between the history-based value of $12.65 (revenue at the 2022–2024 rate of 5.25% with the plan's margins, §6 stress cases) and the plan-based $15.66, nearer the plan (about 84% of the way, my arithmetic), and it got there without having seen the plan: the forecast was published in the information statement dated 2025-03-27 (`sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md`), seven weeks after the 2025-02-05 unaffected date.
        - Why the banks' midpoints are higher. Jefferies applied 2.5% to 3.5% growth to terminal-year cash flow, the method that grows the plan's no-reinvestment cash flow forever. Using that method with our rates gives terminal value $7,490.2M against our $4,769.8M. That difference is about $8.63 a share, which would lift $15.66 to $24.29 (§6, "value per share with the other terminal value"). The banks used higher discount rates (Jefferies 10.70% to 11.70%, `facts.jefferies-dcf-discount-rate-range`; Goldman Sachs 9% to 11%, `facts.goldman-dcf-discount-rate-range`) that work in the other direction.

- **Lesson 34, Session 34 · Part 2 — "The Value of Control: Status Quo vs. Optimal, and the Four Levers to Restructure a Company"** ([transcript](../../04-dividends-and-valuation/lesson-34/session-34-part-2.md))
    - Quote: "The value of control in any company is the difference between two values: the status quo value and the optimal value."
    - Question for SolarWinds: What would change about SolarWinds if it were run optimally (operations, reinvestment, financing mix, growth period), what is it worth with those changes, how large is the value of control, and how likely is it that the change happens given who controls the company?
    - Sources needed: The optimal debt ratio and its value; return on capital against cost of capital; the margin plan; ownership and consent rights.
    - Where to find: model-output.md §3 and §6 (last paragraph); `governance.controlling_holders`, `governance.board`, `governance.marginal_investor`, `debt.covenants`; `sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md`.
    - Answer: Only the financing lever can be priced from the model, and it is worth $0.69 a share. The other three are judgment calls and are not computed.
        - Financing (computed). Optimal debt is 21% against 33.03% today (model-output.md §3). At the optimal cost of capital of 10.03% the enterprise value is $4,017.7M and value per share is $16.35, against $15.66 at the status quo (§6): a gain of $0.69 (4.4%, 0.69 ÷ 15.66, my arithmetic), or $117.9M of enterprise value ($4,017.7M − $3,899.8M). The gain is modest because the stable period already assumes the 21% optimum, so only the 2025–2030 years gain. The §3 table shows a larger $144.4M "value of moving" and $10.9M a year in savings; it capitalizes the savings on the market firm value of $3,889.9M and is a different measure from the DCF difference, and the adjusted present value in §9 gives a third ($220.3M, $1.28 a share); the three are on different bases (§6, §9). To get there SolarWinds would repay $468.1M of debt (§3, "debt above optimum"), more than its $259.3M of cash.
        - Operations (judgment). The plan already assumes a 40.14% EBIT margin by 2030, up from about 29.1% in 2024 on the plan's own definition (26.2% GAAP; `forecast.stress_cases`). The status-quo case is management's plan, so it already contains most of the operating improvement a new owner would claim. No additional credit.
        - Reinvestment and return (judgment). 2024 return on book capital was about 6.7% against a 10.67% cost of capital (lesson 33), and capitalizing R&D does not change that: adjusted ROIC is 6.53% (6.55% on a 5-year life), because the capital base is the 2016 buyout goodwill (model-output.md §8, "R&D capitalized"). The return on operating capital, excluding goodwill, is 87.35% (60.56% on a 5-year life, §8), but that figure is after-tax operating income over $191.0M of operating capital, about three years of R&D (§8): it shows how little capital the business runs on, not what a new dollar earns. The new dollars have been acquisitions, which is where the $2,363.2M of goodwill came from (`company.goodwill`), and on all its capital the company earns 6.66% (§8). So the DCF assumes a stable return on new capital equal to the 8.23% stable cost of capital (`forecast.stable_roic`): growth after 2030 adds no value, and the 87% is the reason reinvestment inside the plan is thin, not a return that new capital can be assumed to earn. A new owner cannot raise the return on the goodwill, and the operating business is already capital-light, so no additional credit; if new capital did earn 12%, the shares would be worth $17.73 (§6 sensitivity), which is the upside case, not the base. The main risk to the status-quo value is the plan, not the return assumption: with revenue at the 2022–2024 rate of 5.25% the value is $12.65 with the plan's margins, $9.57 with the margin held at the plan's first-year 32.69% and $7.79 with it held at 2024's 29.11% on the plan's basis (§6 stress cases). The plan carries the value, the margin expansion more than the growth.
        - Growth period (judgment). Extending the six years would need a stronger moat than retention of 97% to 99% shows (lesson 32).
        - Value of control, financing lever only: $0.69 a share, about $117.9M in total (§6; $0.69 × 171.6M is $118.4M, the difference is rounding).
        - The deal premium is much larger. $18.50 is $3.32 above the $15.18 unaffected price (my arithmetic), a 22% premium (`facts.premium-over-unaffected`), about $569.7M across 171.6M shares (my arithmetic). The financing lever explains about one-fifth of it (3.32 ÷ 0.69 = 4.8, my arithmetic). The rest is not control value from better management as we measure it. Our status-quo value, $15.66, is $0.48 above the unaffected price and $2.84 below the deal price (my arithmetic), so $18.50 is 18% above intrinsic value on management's plan (18.50 ÷ 15.66 − 1, my arithmetic) and 22% above the market: about a seventh of the premium closes the gap from the market price to the plan's value, and the larger part is paid on top of it.
        - Who controls: Silver Lake (36.56%) and Thoma Bravo (29.79%) together held 66.35% at the 2024 proxy (`governance.controlling_holders`); at signing the two held 111,564,519 shares, about 65% of votes (`facts.sponsor-voting-power-2025-02`). The stockholders' agreement gave each a consent right over a change of control (`governance.marginal_investor`). With governance this concentrated, the lecture's rule says the market price carries no premium for a change of management, because public holders cannot force one. The $15.18 price sat below our status-quo value, between the history-based $12.65 and the plan-based $15.66 (§6 stress cases), nearer the plan, and the market had not seen the plan: the gap to intrinsic value is the market pricing SolarWinds between its history and a forecast not yet published, not a bet on control.

## Cumulative Project — Questions for This Lesson

- **What is the status quo value of your firm?**
  $15.66 a share, enterprise value $3,899.8M (model-output.md §6). The story and arithmetic are in Part 1 above.

- **If you were running this firm, what would you change about the way the firm is run (in terms of investing, financing and dividend decisions)?**
  Financing first. Debt is 33.03% of capital and the cost-of-capital approach puts the optimum at 21% (model-output.md §3); repay about $468.1M of term loan rather than carry it. The loan floats at SOFR plus 2.75% (`debt.rate_type`), so repaying it lowers exposure to rate rises as well. Dividends second: stop paying out more than free cash flow to equity while debt is above the optimum. In 2024 the company returned $194.7M (the $168.2M special dividend plus $26.5M of shares withheld for taxes) against FCFE of $127.0M, $67.7M more than it generated (model-output.md §5); applied to the loan, that cash would have covered part of the $468.1M. The credit agreement also limits dividends (`debt.covenants`). Investing third: return on book capital is below the cost of capital (6.7% against 10.67%) because the capital base is buyout goodwill, while the return on operating capital is 87.35% (model-output.md §8), a measure of how little capital the business runs on, not of what new capital earns, since the new dollars have been acquisitions at 6.66% on all capital; the plan's growth spending should have to clear the cost of capital, which the DCF assumes new capital after 2030 only just does (stable return 8.23%, equal to the stable cost of capital, `forecast.stable_roic`). Any spending that earns more is upside the valuation does not count (12% on new capital would give $17.73, §6 sensitivity). The change is in the bar for new spending, not a stated cut.

- **What is the value of your firm with these changes made?**
  $16.35 a share with the financing change alone (model-output.md §6), against $15.66. The investing and operating changes are not priced: the margin plan is already in the status-quo case, and a higher bar for growth spending cannot be valued without a revised forecast. Value of control from the levers we can measure: $0.69 a share.

- **What is the likelihood that this change will happen?**
  Low for the financing change as run by the existing owners. In 2024 the controlling holders chose a special dividend, not debt repayment, with debt already above the optimum (`facts.special-dividend-2024-per-share`). Their control is concentrated: 66.35% of the shares (`governance.controlling_holders`), so no other holder can force a change, and three of the nine directors are Silver Lake designees (`governance.board`). What did happen is a change of control: on 2025-02-07 the two sponsors consented to sell for $18.50. The buyer's financing runs the other way. Debt commitments are a $2,225M first lien term loan, a $525M second lien and a $200M revolver (`facts.new-debt-2025`). The two term loans alone, $2,750M, are more than double today's $1,285.0M and about 65% of the deal's enterprise value of $4,200.3M (my arithmetic, 2,750 ÷ 4,200.3; deal enterprise value is $18.50 × 171.6M + $1,285.0M − $259.3M, on basic shares and before the equity claim). Sponsors borrow to raise the return on their own equity, not to lower the company's cost of capital, so the optimum in §3 is not their target.

## Notes

The status-quo value ($15.66) is 3% above the $15.18 market price (1.03x, model-output.md §6). The market sat between the history-based $12.65 and the plan-based $15.66 (§6 stress cases), nearer the plan, without having seen the plan (published 2025-03-27); it does not read as a bet on a change of control, which governance this concentrated would not let public holders force. The financing lever is real but modest ($0.69). Of the $3.32 deal premium, $0.48 closes the gap from the market price to our status-quo value and $2.84 is paid above it (my arithmetic); it is not explained by control value of the kind this lesson measures. The stable return is set equal to the 8.23% stable cost of capital because the record shows no excess returns on new capital; 12% is the upside case ($17.73, §6). Lesson 36 returns to what does explain the premium.
