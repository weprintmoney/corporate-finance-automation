# SolarWinds (SWI) — Lesson 17: The Financing Principle: Debt vs. Equity Trade-Off

### Lesson 17 — The Financing Principle: Debt vs. Equity Trade-Off

- **Lesson 17, Session 17 · Part 1 — "Debt vs. Equity and the Corporate Life Cycle"** ([transcript](../../02-investment-returns-and-financing/lesson-17/session-17-part-1.md))
    - Quote: "One of the most common problems among corporations is corporations that refuse to act their age."
    - Question for SWI: SWI is a mature, low-growth (~5% YoY), high-margin (48% Adjusted EBITDA margin) software company that nonetheless carries leverage inherited from a 2016 Thoma Bravo/Silver Lake LBO rather than organically chosen debt — per the life-cycle framework, does SWI's current ~23–30% debt weight look mature-appropriate, or is it a legacy LBO artifact rather than a level SWI's own cash-flow stability would independently justify?
    - Sources needed: SWI's debt ratio pre-2016 LBO, post-LBO, and at IPO (2018) vs. current; mature-software peer debt ratios for comparison.
    - Where to find: SEC-EDGAR — SWI historical 10-Ks/S-1 (2018 IPO prospectus) for pre-existing leverage; PEER-FILINGS (Dynatrace, other mature software peers, debt ratios); REPO-SWI-VAL (current debt figures).
    - Answer: Pure legacy artifact — the direction of travel proves it, and the peer set confirms it. SWI's debt has only ever gone one way: total principal was $2,358.1M at FY2017 (first lien $1,678.1M + second lien $680.0M) against negative book equity of −$729.8M, $1,970.1M at FY2018 post-IPO (about 30% of a market capital base at the $15.00 IPO price on 304.9M shares), and $1,235.7M in both FY2023 and FY2024 after a $664.4M repayment in FY2022 (swi-10k-fy2018.md and swi-10k-fy2024.md, Debt footnotes; swi-10k-fy2024.md, Statements of Cash Flows). SWI never raised incremental debt after the 2016 take-private; Amendments 7 and 8 in 2024 only refinanced, repriced and extended maturity — the behavior of a company managing an inherited stack, not choosing a target ratio. The settling comparison is Dynatrace, same Thoma Bravo LBO heritage and an IPO one year later, which now carries zero debt, $1,097.2M of cash and an undrawn $400M revolver on $2,018.4M of revenue (dt-dynatrace-10k.md); Datadog holds $983.4M of 0.00%/0.125% convertibles against $4,474.8M of cash and securities, and PagerDuty $395.7M of 1.50% converts (ddog-datadog-10k.md, pd-pagerduty-10k.md). No mature observability peer carries cash-pay leverage. That said, the life-cycle logic cuts both ways: a 48.3% Adjusted EBITDA margin business growing 3.6% a year with 97% maintenance renewal and 99% net retention is a textbook debt-carrier, so the more interesting anomaly may be the peers running growth-stage balance sheets on mature-stage businesses.

- **Lesson 17, Session 17 · Part 2 — "The Trade-Off on Debt: Tax Benefits, Discipline, Bankruptcy and Agency Costs"** ([transcript](../../02-investment-returns-and-financing/lesson-17/session-17-part-2.md))
    - Quote: "The biggest plus of using debt is the tax code rewards you for borrowing money."
    - Question for SWI: Given SWI's 25% marginal tax rate (per the repo) and its intangible-heavy balance sheet (making lenders less able to monitor collateral, per the lecture's agency-cost point) now that it's a privately held Turn/River portfolio company needing continued SaaS-transition R&D flexibility — do the tax benefits of the ~$1.235B term loan actually exceed its agency costs (covenants) and lost-flexibility costs?
    - Sources needed: Term loan covenant terms (financial maintenance covenants, restricted payments/R&D baskets); R&D spend trend; intangible assets as a percentage of total assets.
    - Where to find: SEC-EDGAR — SWI 10-K FY2024 (debt footnote covenants; balance sheet intangibles/goodwill %); DEAL-DOCS (Turn/River acquisition financing/credit agreement terms, if disclosed via 8-K).
    - Answer: Tax benefits win, and it isn't close — because SWI's lenders never actually imposed the agency costs the theory predicts. The annual shield is $112.435M of interest expense × 25% = $28.1M, worth roughly $395M capitalized at the loan's 7.11% year-end rate, and it is a live cash shield: SWI paid $61.5M of cash taxes on $103.8M of pre-tax income (swi-10k-fy2024.md, Note 16 and Statements of Cash Flows). Against that, the credit agreement carries exactly one financial covenant — a springing first lien net leverage test of 7.40:1.00 that applies only if revolver borrowings exceed 35% of the $130.0M commitment, and the revolver was undrawn at year-end, so no maintenance covenant was even in force (swi-10k-fy2024.md, Note 9 — Debt). The collateral-monitoring argument fails empirically: goodwill plus net intangibles are 78.7% of total assets ($2,492.1M of $3,168.3M) against $16.0M of PP&E, yet lenders priced the loan at SOFR + 2.75% and the restricted-payments basket was permissive enough to allow a $168.2M special dividend in April 2024. R&D rose from $92.3M (FY2022) to $100.2M (FY2023) to $108.6M (FY2024) while fully levered — no evidence of debt-starved innovation. The real cost is flexibility, not agency: $104.5M of cash interest consumed 55.5% of $188.3M of operating cash flow, and Turn/River's own answer to this question was to raise the stack to $2.225B first lien plus $525M second lien plus a $200M revolver (swi-8k-2025-04-16-merger-closing.md).

## Notes

Lesson 17 opens Module 2's financing-mix arc with two closely related questions: does the debt level fit the life-cycle stage, and does the tax-benefit/agency-cost trade-off favor debt. For SWI, the answer to the first is that ~23–30% leverage is inherited 2016-LBO residue rather than a chosen ratio — the debt has only ever declined since 2016, never been re-raised organically — while the second finds the tax shield clearly wins because SWI's lenders never actually exercised the monitoring/covenant discipline the agency-cost theory predicts (one springing covenant, never triggered). This sets up Lesson 18's explicit calculation of where SWI's WACC-minimizing debt ratio actually sits, and the observation that mature observability peers (Dynatrace, Datadog, PagerDuty) all run near-zero leverage is revisited directly in Lesson 21 Part 2's "relative approach" sector comparison.
