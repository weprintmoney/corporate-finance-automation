# SolarWinds (SWI) — Lesson 22: Moving to the Optimal Debt Ratio

### Lesson 22 — Moving to the Optimal Debt Ratio

- **Lesson 22, Session 22 · Part 1 — "Under-Levered vs. Over-Levered: Takeover Risk, Bankruptcy Threat, and the 'Good Projects' Test"** ([transcript](../../03-financing-mix-and-dividends/lesson-22/session-22-part-1.md))
    - Quote: "Having too little debt might make you the target of a hostile acquisition."
    - Question for SWI: SWI was acquired (again) by Turn/River in April 2025 while carrying only ~23% adjusted D/V and a B+ rating — fitting the lesson's profile of an under-levered acquisition target. Using the lesson's own diagnostic tools (Jensen's alpha / stock-performance history, size, insider concentration), was SWI's under-leverage relative to its intrinsic debt capacity a material driver of the take-private, and could a pre-emptive debt-funded buyback have defended against it the way the lesson recommends for vulnerable under-levered firms?
    - Sources needed: SWI total shareholder return/Jensen's alpha 2018–2025 vs. a market or sector benchmark; insider/institutional ownership concentration; deal background narrative on valuation and timing rationale.
    - Where to find: SEC-EDGAR — SWI 10-Ks/DEF 14A proxies (stock performance graph, beneficial ownership tables) and the going-private Schedule 13E-3/DEFM14A ("Background of the Merger" section, which narrates rationale and timing); REPO-RATES (risk-free rate history for the Jensen's alpha calc); DEAL-DOCS (Turn/River press release + fairness opinion) for the deal's stated premium/rationale; NEWS-WEB for analyst commentary on whether the take-private was opportunistic.
    - Answer: No — under-leverage was not a driver, because SWI was not under-levered, and the takeover-threat mechanism was structurally disabled. It carried 3.21x gross debt / 2.54x net debt to adjusted EBITDA and 1.85x interest coverage, more leverage than any peer. Jensen's alpha is deeply negative on the anchors that are computable (the daily price series is unattainable per the sources README): the Oct 2018 IPO priced at $15.00, or $30.00 split-adjusted for the July 2021 2:1 reverse split (`swi-10k-fy2018.md`, `swi-10k-fy2023.md`), and an IPO holder ultimately received $18.50 of merger cash plus $1.50 (Aug 2021) and $1.00 (Apr 2024) special dividends plus $13.00 of in-kind N-able stock = **$34.00, a 13.3% total return over 6.5 years, ~1.9% annualized** against a ~4% risk-free rate and an 11.75% cost of equity. That fires the underperformance diagnostic — but it points at SUNBURST and the subscription transition, not the balance sheet. Meanwhile `swi-defm14c-2025-merger-information-statement.md` shows no predator: 13 parties canvassed in the Initial Market Check (12 sponsors, 1 strategic), 9 NDAs, only 3 indications of interest all "significantly lower than $18.50 per share," then 20+ financial and 14 strategic contacts between Feb 2024 and Jan 2025 with zero indications, with withdrawals attributed to "the relative early stage of the Company's ongoing transition to a subscription-first business model." SWI was shopping itself, not being hunted, and the sponsors' 66.34% stake plus a stockholders' agreement requiring written consent from both Silver Lake and Thoma Bravo affiliates made a hostile bid impossible. A debt-funded buyback was therefore the wrong defense; SWI ran the cash version anyway ($168.2M dividend plus $26.5M of repurchases in 2024) and it preceded the sale by ten months.

## Cumulative Project — Questions for This Lesson

- **Is your firm under, over or correctly levered?**
  Modestly over-levered to roughly correctly levered, depending on which of this project's own optimal-ratio estimates is used — and that spread is itself the honest answer. Against Lesson 18's EBIT-based cost-of-capital optimum (13%), the corrected current ratio of 28.8% (Lesson 13) reads as over-levered; against the EBITDA-based version of the same schedule (25%) or Lesson 36's simpler ~30% coverage-cap estimate, it reads as close to correct. Every version of the exercise agrees on direction relative to peers, though: Lesson 21's finding that observability peers (Dynatrace, Datadog, PagerDuty) run near-zero leverage is the wrong comparison group (the "Vale case," not the "Disney case" — none of them could service SWI's debt at all), so "over-levered relative to sector" is not the same finding as "over-levered relative to SWI's own optimum."

- **If under levered, is it under threat of a hostile acquisition? If over levered, how imminent is the bankruptcy threat?**
  SWI is not meaningfully under-levered by any measure here, so the hostile-acquisition branch doesn't apply — and as established above, a hostile bid was structurally impossible regardless: the sponsor bloc's ~65%–66% combined stake and stockholders'-agreement written-consent rights (established in Lessons 2 and 33) meant no acquirer could have forced a deal without sponsor consent. On the over-levered branch, the bankruptcy threat is not imminent: SWI ran its entire public life below 1.0x EBIT-based net interest coverage in most years (established in Lesson 19) without ever breaching its one springing covenant or coming close to default — the theoretical fragility this project's synthetic-rating exercises flag (Lesson 19: a 20% EBIT decline would trigger a two-notch synthetic downgrade) never translated into an actual covenant or liquidity event.

- **How good are the potential projects that the company can invest in?**
  Poor, on the evidence this project has already built. Lesson 31 finds SWI's fundamental growth rate (reinvestment rate × ROIC) was effectively zero to slightly negative in FY2023–FY2024, and its capitalized sales-to-capital ratio of 0.34 (14.9 on an incremental basis) lags Datadog's 14.2 despite SWI spending more per incremental revenue dollar once R&D is properly capitalized ($2.91 vs. Datadog's $2.08, per Lesson 31 Part 2). Combined with Lesson 14's finding that ROIC (6.7% in FY2024) never clears the ~10.1%–10.33% WACC, the available reinvestment opportunities inside SWI do not clear the hurdle rate — which is precisely the condition under which this lesson's own framework (and Lesson 27's "trust test") says cash should be returned rather than reinvested, and which SWI's board in fact did via its two special dividends.

- **What is the best path that you see for your firm to get from its actual to its optimal debt ratio?**
  Gradual paydown from free cash flow, not urgent restructuring — because, as established immediately above, there is no imminent distress forcing a faster move. SWI's own sponsors already ran exactly this playbook once: a $664.4M voluntary-plus-refinancing paydown in FY2022 (established in Lesson 26 Part 2) that took gross leverage down meaningfully without any external pressure. That template — pay down debt with organic FCFF while continuing to distribute any true excess via special dividends rather than a regular payout — was the best available path from 28.8% toward the 13%–25% optimal band. It became moot once Turn/River acquired the company: the 2025 recapitalization moved leverage the opposite direction, to roughly 58%–60% of capital (established in Lessons 19, 20, 34), because the new owner's objective (fund-level IRR on a $1,670.0M equity check) is not the same optimization problem as minimizing SWI's own WACC.

## Notes

Lesson 22 asks whether under-leverage exposes a firm to hostile takeover, and whether that risk should be defended against with a debt-funded buyback. For SWI the premise doesn't hold: this project has already shown SWI is over-levered relative to peers (Lessons 17, 20–21), not under-levered, and the takeover-threat mechanism itself was structurally disabled by the sponsors' written-consent control — a hostile bid was never possible regardless of the debt level. The Jensen's-alpha diagnostic does fire (a 13.3% total return over 6.5 years against an 11.75% cost of equity is genuine underperformance), but the record shows SWI shopping itself through a two-round market check that drew no competing bids, not a predator circling an under-levered target. This lesson's finding closes the loop on the "was this a hostile-takeover-defense situation" question that Lesson 33 and Lesson 36 both revisit when explaining why the float didn't contest the deal.
