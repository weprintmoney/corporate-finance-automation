# SolarWinds (SWI) — Lesson 02: The Objective: Utopia and Let Down

### Lesson 02 — The Objective: Utopia and Let Down

- **Lesson 02, Session 2 · Part 1 — "The Four Linkages Behind the Utopian World of Stock Price Maximization"** ([transcript](../../01-foundations-and-discount-rates/lesson-02/session-2-part-1.md))
    - Quote: "Finally, in this Utopian world, there are no social costs or social benefits."
    - Question for SolarWinds: For each of the four linkages, where is SolarWinds weakest? In particular, how are its lenders protected (covenants, change-of-control terms), and has any recent event tested that protection?
    - Sources needed: credit agreement terms; board and ownership; float and trading; the social-cost event.
    - Where to find: ledger `debt.covenants`, `governance.board`, `governance.controlling_holders`; `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md` (Note 9, Item 1A, cover page); `sources/deal-docs/swi-8k-2025-04-16-merger-closing.md` (Item 1.02).
    - Answer: Weakest is the link between managers and the minority stockholders, because the two sponsors decide. Silver Lake held 36.56% and Thoma Bravo 29.79% (`governance.controlling_holders`), 66.35% together at the proxy date (the sum, `facts.sponsors-pct-2024-proxy`), and about 65% of the votes at the 2025-02-07 written consent, a later date (`facts.sponsor-voting-power-2025-02`), against 1.95% for all officers and directors (`facts.officers-directors-pct`). Three of the nine directors are Silver Lake designees (`governance.board`). Each sponsor also held a written-consent veto over a change of control, new debt above $300.0M, asset deals above $300.0M and the choice of chief executive (`facts.sponsor-consent-rights`). Lenders are the best-protected link. The only borrowing is a first-lien term loan with a face value of $1,235.7M (`company.debt_face`). Its credit agreement restricts new debt, liens, mergers, dividends and investments, treats a change of control as an event of default, and carries a single financial test that springs only if the revolver is more than 35% drawn, at first-lien net leverage of 7.40 to 1 (`debt.covenants`). The merger tested it. At closing on 2025-04-16 the company paid all fees and terminated the commitments under the 2016 credit agreement, and Turn/River's vehicle took on a new $2,225.0M first-lien term loan, a $200.0M revolver and a $525.0M second-lien loan (`facts.new-debt-2025`). The lenders were paid off, not harmed. The market link is thin: stock held by non-affiliates was worth $661.3M on 2024-06-28 (`facts.float-nonaffiliate-value`), against equity value of $2,604.9M at the analysis date (`company.equity_value`). The social-cost link was tested by the SUNBURST attack in December 2020, which cost customers and the company and was not priced in advance (lesson 3).

- **Lesson 02, Session 2 · Part 2 — "Golden Parachutes, Poison Pills, and Managerial Self-Interest at Shareholder Expense"** ([transcript](../../01-foundations-and-discount-rates/lesson-02/session-2-part-2.md))
    - Quote: "Given a choice between their interest and what's in the best interest of stockholders, managers often put their interest over stockholder interests."
    - Question for SolarWinds: What do SolarWinds' executive pay and change-of-control agreements reward: closing a deal, the share price, or long-run value? Are there golden parachutes, poison pills, or other devices that protect managers rather than stockholders?
    - Sources needed: golden-parachute table; bonus and equity plan metrics; charter takeover defenses.
    - Where to find: ledger `governance.change_of_control_payouts`; `sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md` (Golden Parachute Compensation); `sources/filings/swi-def14a-2024.md` (Compensation Discussion and Analysis); `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md` (Item 1A, anti-takeover provisions).
    - Answer: Mostly the share price, with a small payment for closing at any price. The three named executives' parachutes total $38.96M: Ramakrishna $22.99M, Black $8.54M, Bliss $7.43M (`governance.change_of_control_payouts`). Of that, $34.4M is stock units accelerated and valued at $18.50 a share (`facts.parachute-equity-total`), so it rises and falls with the price, and all of it needs a change of control plus a qualifying termination. Cash severance is $3.2M (`facts.parachute-cash-severance-total`). The exception is $1.2M of transaction bonuses ($750,000, $100,000 and $350,000), payable on closing with no termination required (`facts.transaction-bonuses-total`). That is paid for any closing at any price. The whole package is 1.2% of the roughly $3,206M paid to stockholders (`facts.deal-total-consideration`; arithmetic, $38.96M ÷ $3,206M). Day to day, pay tracks operating metrics rather than the share price: the 2023 bonus plan paid on revenue and adjusted EBITDA, 50% each, and the stock-unit awards on Subscription ARR (70%) and revenue (30%) (`facts.pay-metrics-2023`). The proxy's own pay-versus-performance table uses revenue as the company-selected measure. Defenses: the charter has a classified board with three-year staggered terms, director removal only for cause once the sponsors fall below 30%, and a ban on stockholder written consent once they fall below 40% (10-K FY2024, Item 1A). Neither the 10-K nor the proxy mentions a poison pill (shareholder rights plan). The more consequential device protects the sponsors: the charter renounces corporate opportunities presented to them and their designated directors (proxy, Stockholders' Agreement).

- **Lesson 02, Session 2 · Part 3 — "Where the Power Lies — Reading Top Shareholders to Judge Minority Protection"** ([transcript](../../01-foundations-and-discount-rates/lesson-02/session-2-part-3.md))
    - Quote: "Institutional investors, when they don't like the way a company is run, tend to sell their shares and move on."
    - Question for SolarWinds: Who are SolarWinds' top shareholders, and what protections exist for minority holders (independent committees, majority-of-minority votes, limits on controlling votes)? Do the institutional holders fight when they disagree, or just sell?
    - Sources needed: beneficial ownership table; share classes; merger approval mechanics; committee structure.
    - Where to find: ledger `governance.controlling_holders`, `governance.share_classes`; `sources/filings/swi-def14a-2024.md` (beneficial ownership); `sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md` (Background of the Merger; Written Consent; appraisal rights).
    - Answer: The top two holders are Silver Lake (61,473,871 shares, 36.56%) and Thoma Bravo (50,090,648 shares, 29.79%) (`governance.controlling_holders`). The proxy lists no other 5% holder. There is one class of stock with one vote a share (`governance.share_classes`), so there is no dual-class cap on control; the control is the two blocks. Minority protection in the merger was thin. The two sponsors, together holding 111,564,519 shares or about 65% of the vote, signed a written consent on 2025-02-07, right after the merger agreement, so no stockholder vote was held (`facts.sponsor-voting-power-2025-02`). There was no majority-of-minority condition. A three-person Transaction Committee (Bock, Sundaram, Widmann) handled the process from 2023-08-29; the filing says it was formed "for convenience and efficiency, and not due to any actual or perceived conflict of interest", and one of its members, Widmann, is a Silver Lake managing director (`facts.transaction-committee`; `governance.board`). What the minority did get: two fairness opinions (Goldman Sachs and Jefferies) and appraisal rights under Delaware law for holders other than the sponsors. Whether institutions fought or sold: the sources do not say. No institution held 5%, the consent left no vote to fight in, and the filings record no public opposition. Not answerable from `sources/`.

## Module 1 Step — Questions for This Lesson

- **Corporate governance**
  - Who sits on the board of directors? Nine directors (`governance.board`): Sudhakar Ramakrishna (President and CEO), William Bock (Chairman), Kenneth Hao, Douglas Smith and Michael Widmann (Silver Lake designees), and Cathleen Benko, Dennis Howard, Catherine Kinney and Easwaran Sundaram (no sponsor affiliation). Eight of nine count as independent under NYSE rules; only the CEO does not.
  - Whose interests are they most likely to serve? The sponsors'. Eight "independent" directors include all three Silver Lake designees, and the seats reserved for Thoma Bravo stayed empty while it kept its veto by contract. The remaining directors, including the Chairman, have no sponsor tie in the proxy.
  - Where does power lie, and what measure of governance strength supports that? In the two inside shareholders. Evidence: 66.35% of the shares at the 2024 proxy (`facts.sponsors-pct-2024-proxy`) and about 65% of the votes at the 2025-02-07 consent (`facts.sponsor-voting-power-2025-02`); the nomination right for three directors each while each holds 20% or more; the consent veto (`facts.sponsor-consent-rights`); and a sale approved by their written consent alone. Managers hold 1.95%, so managers do not have the power. The proxy gives no governance score, so the measure is the ownership and consent evidence itself.

## Notes

The governance picture is controlled, not dispersed. Silver Lake and Thoma Bravo are separate holders, and the deal needed each to agree. That sets up lesson 4 (the marginal investor is the diversified public float, not the sponsors), lesson 22 (the 2021 and 2024 special dividends paid the sponsors pro rata), and lesson 36's question of whose interests drove the sale. Lenders were protected by structure, not covenants. The sponsors' stakes are 36.56% and 29.79%.
