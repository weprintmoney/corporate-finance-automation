# SolarWinds (SWI) — Lesson 18: The Cost of Capital Approach to the Optimal Debt Ratio

### Lesson 18 — The Cost of Capital Approach to the Optimal Debt Ratio

- **Lesson 18, Session 18 · Part 1 — "The Cost of Capital Approach to the Optimal Debt Ratio"** ([transcript](../../02-investment-returns-and-financing/lesson-18/session-18-part-1.md))
    - Quote: "The optimal debt ratio for this company, if I stay true to the notion of minimizing cost of capital, is 40% debt."
    - Question for SWI: Running the same cost-of-capital-schedule exercise (levering SWI's unlevered beta of 1.27 via Hamada at each debt ratio, and estimating synthetic ratings/cost of debt from interest coverage at each level per Damodaran's lookup table), where does SWI's WACC bottom out — and does its current ~23–30% debt weight sit below, at, or above that minimizing point?
    - Sources needed: SWI's EBIT/EBITDA held constant under the "recapitalization" assumption at each hypothetical debt level; Damodaran's current interest-coverage-to-rating lookup table (smaller-firm version); unlevered beta.
    - Where to find: LESSON-MATERIALS (lesson-18 spreadsheet-cost-of-capital-optimal.md — the optimal-debt-ratio template); REPO-BETAS (industry-betas.json); REPO-SWI-VAL (Step 10 unlevered beta 1.2725, Step 12 EBIT $208.4M); DAMODARAN-SITE (current synthetic rating table).
    - Answer: SWI's WACC bottoms at roughly 13% debt and its actual ~23% sits well past the minimum — SolarWinds is over-levered, not under-levered. Holding EBIT at $208.4M, firm value at $5,717M, unlevered beta at 1.2725, rf at 4.68%, ERP at 4.56% and t at 25%, and iterating the smaller-and-riskier-firm coverage table (spreadsheet-cost-of-capital-optimal.md, Default Spreads and Ratios), WACC runs 10.48% at 0% debt, 10.30% at 10%, a minimum of 10.26% at 13% (coverage 4.73x → A3/A-, spread 1.25%), then 10.43% at 15%, 10.78% at 20% and 10.83% at 23% (coverage 1.56x → B3/B-, spread 5.50%). The curve is sharply asymmetric: moving from 13% to 23% costs about 57bp of WACC, while moving from 13% all the way to zero debt costs only 22bp — so the penalty for over-borrowing here is nearly three times the penalty for under-borrowing. In dollar terms SWI carries roughly $542M of debt above the optimum, and the value-maximizing financing action is to retire debt, not add it. One consistency note: this schedule uses synthetic spreads throughout, which is why it puts SWI's current WACC at 10.83% rather than the repo's 10.33% — the repo substituted the actual B+ market spread of 2.74%.

- **Lesson 18, Session 18 · Part 2 — "The Iterative Rating Loop and the Kink in the Cost of Capital Curve"** ([transcript](../../02-investment-returns-and-financing/lesson-18/session-18-part-2.md))
    - Quote: "My advice to companies is if you're going to make a mistake, be under-levered rather than over-levered."
    - Question for SWI: The repo's own Step 12 shows SWI's EBIT-based synthetic rating (B-/B3) sitting two notches below its actual B+ rating because EBITDA-based coverage is more favorable — when running the iterative interest-coverage-to-rating loop across hypothetical debt ratios for SWI, should the lookup use EBIT-based or EBITDA-based coverage, since that choice could shift where SWI's optimal-debt "kink" appears?
    - Sources needed: Damodaran's current interest-coverage-to-rating lookup table (EBIT-based and any EBITDA-adjusted variant); rating agencies' actual methodology commentary on SWI post-Turn/River.
    - Where to find: DAMODARAN-SITE (live synthetic rating/ratings lookup table); SEC-EDGAR — SWI 8-K (S&P/Moody's rating action rationale post-Turn/River deal); REPO-SWI-VAL (Step 12, EBIT vs. EBITDA divergence discussion).
    - Answer: Use EBIT for the decision and treat the EBITDA run as the upside case — but the choice is decisive, not cosmetic, and pretending otherwise is the real error. Re-running the same iterative loop with Adjusted EBITDA of $384.7M as the numerator moves the WACC minimum from 13% debt (10.26%) to 25% debt (10.06%) and places SWI's current 23.1% essentially at the optimum (10.09%, coverage 4.91x → A3/A-). A 12-percentage-point swing in the answer from a single input choice is the whole lesson. SWI does have a legitimate claim on a middle numerator, because $52.9M of its $74.4M of D&A is amortization of 2016-LBO acquired intangibles that will never be repurchased in cash; using EBIT plus acquisition amortization ($261.3M) puts the optimum at 17% with a WACC of 10.20%. The market's verdict sits in that same middle: S&P rated SWI B+ (2.74% spread) against an EBIT-synthetic B- (5.50%) and an EBITDA-synthetic A-, and the term loan actually priced at SOFR + 2.75% for a 7.11% year-end rate (swi-10k-fy2024.md, Note 9). Given "if you're going to make a mistake, be under-levered," take the EBIT-to-cash-EBIT answer of 13–17% and call SWI over-levered — the kink between 13% and 15%, where the synthetic rating falls three notches from A3/A- to Ba2/BB and the spread jumps from 1.25% to 3.00%, is precisely the cliff the advice is about.

## Cumulative Project — Questions for This Lesson

- **What is your firm's current debt ratio (and cost of capital)?**
  Using the corrected market weights established in Lesson 13: debt $1,285.0M against equity $3,174.0M gives a current debt ratio of **28.8%**, at a WACC of roughly **10.1%** (cost of equity 12.0%, after-tax cost of debt 5.33%). This is the figure to use going forward in this project rather than `valuation-report.md`'s uncorrected 23.1%/10.33%, which mis-stated market equity as the $4.4B enterprise value (see [`README.md`](README.md)).

- **Based upon the cost of capital approach, what is the optimal debt ratio for your firm?**
  As established above, the answer is genuinely sensitive to one modeling choice: iterating the synthetic-rating/Hamada schedule on EBIT-based interest coverage puts the WACC-minimizing point at **13% debt** (10.26%), while the same schedule on Adjusted-EBITDA-based coverage moves it to **25% debt** (10.06%) — a 12-point swing from a single input. Splitting the difference with an EBIT-plus-acquisition-amortization numerator (justified because $52.9M of SWI's D&A is 2016-LBO purchase-accounting amortization that will never be repurchased in cash) lands the optimum at **17%** (WACC 10.20%). Against the corrected current ratio of 28.8% above, SWI reads as modestly over-levered under the EBIT and blended measures, and roughly at its optimum only under the more generous EBITDA measure — the same conclusion this lesson's own Part 2 answer reaches, now cross-checked against the corrected (not the repo's) current-ratio figure.

## Notes

This lesson runs the full cost-of-capital-minimizing exercise: Hamada-relever beta at every hypothetical debt ratio, look up a synthetic rating from interest coverage, and find where WACC bottoms out. For SWI the finding is genuinely sensitive to a single modeling choice — EBIT-based coverage puts the optimum at 13% debt (SWI over-levered at ~23%), while EBITDA-based coverage moves it to 25% (SWI essentially at the optimum) — and Part 2's answer explicitly picks EBIT as the more defensible measure while noting the "under-levered is the safer mistake" advice supports calling SWI over-levered either way. This is the most quantitatively important lesson in Module 2: it directly produces the "SWI carries roughly $542M of debt above the optimum" finding that Lesson 19's stress test and Lesson 21's APV cross-check both build on, and the EBIT/EBITDA sensitivity it surfaces is the same tension flagged back in Lesson 12's ratings discussion.

## Corporate Life Cycle, Chapter 18 — "Managing Across the Life Cycle": Questions for SWI

The three questions below come from Damodaran's *Corporate Life Cycle* book, Chapter 18 (`03-5-damodaran-online/03-5-3-papers/ch18.md`) — a different source from the Session 18 video-lecture transcripts above. They're filed in this lesson because the chapter number happens to match the lesson number, not because the topics relate to optimal-debt-ratio material. (Same caveat applies to the Ch. 13 sum-of-the-parts note added to `lesson-13.md` — that chapter is actually "Valuing Declining Firms," coincidentally numbered, not the WACC-weights topic Lesson 13 covers.)

- **Who is your real catalyst, and do you know it?**
    - Quote: "Public companies: Once companies become publicly traded, the management change process must go through the board of directors of the company. To the extent that board members are picked by incumbent managers, there will be a built-in bias towards preserving existing management" — and, on the stage-by-stage catalyst: "With declining firms, private equity firms become catalysts pushing to buy out these firms, and make them private, while they make operating and financial changes at these firms" (ch18.md).
    - Question for SWI: across SWI's three major discontinuities — the 2016 take-private, the December 2020 CEO succession, and the 2025 Turn/River sale — was the board ever the actual catalyst, or was it always the sponsor bloc acting through board control?
    - Sources needed: stockholders' agreement terms (board-nomination thresholds, sponsor approval rights); the 8-K disclosing the CEO transition; the merger document's approval mechanics.
    - Where to find: SEC-EDGAR — `swi-def14a-2024.md` (stockholders' agreement, director designations, sponsor approval rights); `swi-8k-2020-12-09-sunburst-disclosure.md` (Item 5.02, CEO succession); `swi-defm14c-2025-merger-information-statement.md` (written-consent approval).
    - Answer: Always the sponsor bloc — the board was the nominal decision-maker, but structurally captured at every turn. The stockholders' agreement ties board-nomination rights directly to ownership tiers (≥20% of shares = 3 director nominees each for Silver Lake and Thoma Bravo, plus one mutually nominated seat), and separately gives the Sponsors a *veto*: as long as they collectively hold ≥30% of shares, major corporate actions require both funds' written consent regardless of how the rest of the float votes (`swi-def14a-2024.md`, "Silver Lake and Thoma Bravo Approvals"). As of the 2024 proxy, Silver Lake had seated all three of its nominee seats (Hao, Smith, Widmann — Hao is Silver Lake's Chairman) while Thoma Bravo's three seats and the mutual seat sat vacant, even though Thoma Bravo retained the *right* to fill them at 29.79% ownership. That's the catalyst mechanism made visible: filled board seats aren't even necessary for control when the veto right and the nomination right both run through the same two names. The December 2020 CEO succession confirms the pattern from a different angle — Sudhakar Ramakrishna's appointment and Kevin Thompson's resignation were disclosed in the *same* Item 5.02 of the same December 9, 2020 8-K that disclosed SUNBURST, meaning the transition was already arranged, not a reactive board scramble triggered by the hack becoming public. And the 2025 sale closed via a Schedule 14C information statement — written stockholder consent, no proxy vote — which is only mechanically possible because the sponsor bloc's aggregate stake was sufficient to constitute majority consent without soliciting the dispersed float at all. None of Ch. 18's "boards rarely self-initiate" caveat needed testing here, because the board never had to be the one to act — the real decision-maker was legible the entire time in a document (the stockholders' agreement), not in the boardroom. On the "step 10" mapping: I don't have that playbook file loaded to quote its wording, but the general point holds — if a resolution-ownership exercise routes an item to "the board" for a sponsor-controlled company, it's aiming at a body that structurally defers to two named funds; naming Silver Lake and Thoma Bravo directly, rather than "the board," would have been the accurate answer at every one of SWI's inflection points.

- **Does entrenchment belong in the constraint profile as distinct from ownership %?**
    - Quote: "With mature companies that are publicly traded, the catalyst for change will be activist investors and hedge funds, often in response to disappointing operating and stock market performance" (ch18.md) — a catalyst SWI never needed, because entrenchment mechanisms did the job dual-class shares usually do elsewhere.
    - Question for SWI: does SWI show ownership % and actual governance capacity moving independently of each other?
    - Sources needed: share-class structure; board classification/term structure; the stockholders' agreement's nomination and approval thresholds; the sponsor ownership trajectory over time.
    - Where to find: `valuation-report.md` Step 4 (share structure); `swi-def14a-2024.md` (board classification, stockholders' agreement); Lesson 4's established ownership trajectory (88.8% combined at 12/31/2018 → 78.3% at 12/31/2020 → 67.0% at 12/31/2023 → 65.0%/65.35% split 36.56%/29.79% at 2024).
    - Answer: Yes, and it's a cleaner example than dual-class because SWI has *no* dual-class structure at all — one share class, one vote per share (`valuation-report.md`, Step 4). The entrenchment runs through two other levers entirely. First, a classified board: nine directors split into three classes with staggered three-year terms, so control of the board can't be contested in a single annual meeting even if the float wanted to try (`swi-def14a-2024.md`). Second, the stockholders' agreement's approval right is a step function, not a continuous one — it doesn't degrade smoothly as sponsor ownership falls; it's simply "on" at every ownership level down to a combined 30% floor, then presumably off below it. Combined sponsor ownership fell about 24 points over six years (88.8% → 65.0%), a real and substantial dilution — but every one of those years stayed above the 30% floor, so the veto right and the board-nomination rights held constant the entire time. Two companies with that same 24-point ownership decline could have completely different capacities to act: one where the floor was set at 60% (control lost partway through the decline) and one like SWI where the floor sat at 30% (control never at risk). Ownership % alone would have badly understated SWI's governance stability across 2018–2024; the entrenchment terms are what actually determined whether change was possible, and they only mattered once — right at the very end, when the sponsors themselves chose to sell rather than being forced to.

- **How do you guard against your own version of "great CEO" survivorship bias?**
    - Quote: Ch. 18's "great CEO" myth argument, applied here: the same operating traits get relabeled from strength to flaw purely based on the outcome, not on any independent re-assessment of the trait itself.
    - Question for SWI: is there a documented "before/after" flip in how Kevin Thompson's management style is told, split by SUNBURST?
    - Sources needed: the S-1 IPO narrative (pre-SUNBURST framing of the operating model); the GAAP operating-margin trend under Thompson; the 8-K disclosing his exit.
    - Where to find: `swi-s1-2018-ipo-prospectus.md`; `swi-multi-year-financials.md` (FY2017–2020 operating income); `swi-8k-2020-12-09-sunburst-disclosure.md`.
    - Answer: Yes — same traits, opposite valence, split by SUNBURST, and the underlying fundamentals were already softening before the flip. At the 2018 IPO, SWI marketed itself explicitly on operating leanness: "we utilize a cost-efficient, integrated global product development model," "we do not utilize an outside sales force or provide professional services," a "low-touch, high-velocity" sales motion — "The SolarWinds Model" was the pitch, not an incidental detail (`swi-s1-2018-ipo-prospectus.md`). That same lean-cost-structure story is the one retroactively read, post-SUNBURST, as explaining *why* a nation-state actor could sit undetected in the build pipeline for months — under-resourced security engineering dressed up as operating discipline. Notably, the fundamentals were already declining under Thompson before SUNBURST gave the story a villain: GAAP operating margin ran 13.8% in FY2018, 11.9% in FY2019, and 7.3% in FY2020 (`swi-multi-year-financials.md`) — a real, pre-hack erosion that a "great CEO" narrative would have had to explain on its own terms, if anyone had been looking for a downside case at the time. Instead, the causal story arrived only after the outcome was known: Thompson's resignation was folded into the very same December 9, 2020 8-K Item 5.02 that disclosed SUNBURST, so the "he was pushed out because of the hack" reading and the "he was already leaving, independent of the hack" reading are both consistent with the same filing — the record doesn't cleanly support picking one over the other, which is itself the tell. The honest guard here: this project's own account of Thompson is built entirely from 10-Ks, S-1s and 8-Ks filed *after* the fact — there's no pre-2020 analyst or press commentary compiled in `sources/` that would show whether his traits were praised in real time, independent of how SUNBURST turned out. That's not a solved problem; it's the same survivorship-bias exposure Ch. 18 names, just also present in how this project itself was built, not only in the CEO-mythology literature it's critiquing.

## Rerun (2026-10-06): optimum is 17%, not 13%

The 13% optimum above was computed on a firm value of $5,717M, which carries the report's equity-vs-enterprise-value error. On the corrected ~$4.4B firm value, the same smaller-firm table and Hamada loop put the optimum at **17%** (EBIT coverage, Capital IQ's $218.7M EBIT) and **21%** (Capital IQ's standardized EBITDA of $273.5M, or EBIT plus the $52.9M of 2016-LBO amortization, which give nearly the same figure). The 25% EBITDA-case optimum above relied on company-adjusted EBITDA of $384.7M, which adds back stock-based compensation. Read the range as **17%–21%**, not 13%–25%. SWI's ~29% is over-levered on every defensible measure. Full comparison and script: [`capital-iq-rerun.md`](capital-iq-rerun.md).
