# SolarWinds — How this valuation was built

## How to read this

This document walks through the valuation of SolarWinds one step at a time, in the order the course teaches it. Every number in it was either read from a named document or computed by the model from those documents; nothing is typed in from memory. Each step has the same six parts: what we wanted to know, why it matters, what we did, the numbers and where they came from, what we decided, and how sure we are. Terms are explained the first time they appear and collected in the glossary at the end.

## The valuation in ten sentences

SolarWinds sells software that helps companies watch over their computer networks. In 2024 it took in $796.9 million and made $208.4 million from operations, growing about 5% a year. Two private-equity firms, Silver Lake and Thoma Bravo, owned 36.56% and 29.79% of it. On 2025-02-07 they agreed to sell the whole company to Turn/River Capital for $18.50 a share, when the stock had last traded at $15.18. We valued SolarWinds as of 2025-02-05, using the interest rates and market conditions of that day, so the two prices could be judged fairly. Investors in a business this risky, carrying this much debt, needed a return of 10.23% a year on the money tied up in it (the cost of capital: the yearly return that lenders and shareholders together require). Discounting management's own forecast of cash at that rate gives $13.00 a share. That figure subtracts the $187.0 million claim of employees' stock options and units, and it assumes SolarWinds must keep reinvesting to keep growing, rather than taking management's final-year cash flow as given. That is less than both the market price and the sale price. The sale price is reached only by assuming the company grows forever without reinvesting, which gives $18.67 a share (steps 32 and 36). Comparing SolarWinds with similar public companies gives a wide span of $8.34 to $19.95 a share, so the comparisons do not settle the question. The company also carried more debt than suited it, 33% of its value against a best level of 21%, a mismatch worth between $0.52 and $1.28 a share depending on how it is measured. For the public shareholders, who were never asked to vote, $18.50 in cash was a good price because it was well above our $13.00 and above the $15.18 market price.

## Where the numbers come from

*The company's own reports.* SolarWinds' annual reports (Form 10-K) filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), the US regulator, for 2024, 2023, 2020 and 2018. The 2024 report, filed on 2025-02-19, is the main one. Also its 2024 proxy statement, the document sent to shareholders before the annual meeting, which lists the directors, their pay and who owns the shares. Files: `sources/filings/`.

*The sale documents.* When Turn/River Capital agreed to buy SolarWinds, the company filed an information statement explaining the deal. It contains the history of the negotiations, two banks' opinions on whether the price was fair, and management's own forecasts for 2025 through 2030. Files: `sources/deal-docs/`.

*Market data from the day before the deal leaked.* The valuation is dated 2025-02-05, the last trading day before the sale was announced. The interest rate on 10-year US government bonds that day comes from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis's public database (FRED). The extra return investors demanded for owning stocks, and the industry averages used for risk, come from Professor Damodaran's published datasets for January and February 2025. Files: `sources/market-data/`.

*Eight comparable companies.* Financial figures for eight other software companies, taken from their own SEC filings, and their share prices on 2025-02-05. These are the companies one of the deal's banks used as comparisons. File: `sources/peer-filings/peer-facts-2025-02-05.md`.

## Step 1: What kind of company is SolarWinds, and how old is it in business terms?

*What we wanted to know.* Which company we are valuing, and where it sits in a business life cycle. Companies, like people, move from start-up to fast growth to maturity to decline, and the right behavior changes at each stage.

*Why it matters.* A young company should spend its cash on growth. A mature company should hand spare cash back to its owners. If we misjudge the stage, we will misjudge how much of the company's value comes from future growth, and every later forecast will be off.

*What we did.* We looked at four things: how fast revenue grows, how much profit the company makes on each dollar of sales, how much it reinvests in itself, and what it does with its cash. Revenue growth is the new year's revenue divided by last year's, minus one. For SolarWinds, $796.9 million in 2024 divided by $758.7 million in 2023, minus one, is 5.0%. We then asked whether management's behavior fits the stage the numbers suggest.

*The numbers and where they came from.* All from SolarWinds' 2024 annual report (Form 10-K, the yearly report public companies file with the US Securities and Exchange Commission), filed 2025-02-19. File: `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`. Revenue was $796.9 million. Operating income, meaning profit before interest and taxes, was $208.4 million, a margin of 26.2%. The company spent $20.5 million on equipment and software it built, against $74.4 million of depreciation, the yearly accounting charge for wearing out equipment that was bought earlier. It spent $108.6 million on research and development. It paid shareholders a special dividend (a one-off cash payment) of $168.2 million in April 2024, $1.00 a share. The 2021 special dividend was $237.2 million, $1.50 a share, from the 2023 annual report (`sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2023.md`). In 2023 the company and its banks approached 13 possible buyers; three gave non-binding indications (the highest, $13.00 a share, was revised down from about $14.00) and all three withdrew; the story is told in the sale document (the information statement for the merger, `sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md`). Management's own forecast, in the same document, has revenue growing 8.12% a year to 2030. The model's table of free cash flow to equity (FCFE: cash left for shareholders after reinvestment and debt payments, model-output.md §5) shows $127.0 million available in 2024 against $194.7 million paid out.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* SolarWinds is a mature company. Slow growth, solid margins, capital spending of $20.5 million that is small next to revenue of $796.9 million, and big cash payouts all point the same way, and the failed 2023 sale process and the 2025 sale agree. This is a judgment, but all the evidence points one direction. One piece does not fit: management's forecast of 8.12% growth is well above the 5.0% just reported. We keep the label "mature" and test the forecast later, in the value calculation.

*How sure we are.* Fairly sure of the stage, because every number is read directly from a filing. The company reports one business segment, so we cannot split the old software from the newer subscription products; any split is a proxy. The forecast is management's, not ours.

## Step 2: Who really controls SolarWinds?

*What we wanted to know.* Who sits on the board, whose interests they serve, and who holds the power. Corporate governance means the system of who decides what in a company.

*Why it matters.* The course assumes managers work for shareholders. If a few big owners decide everything, small shareholders have little say, and a sale price may reflect the big owners' wishes. That changes how much weight we give a deal price as evidence of value.

*What we did.* We read the list of directors and the table of who owns the shares, then the contract between the company and its two largest owners (the stockholders' agreement), then the account of how the 2025 sale was approved. We asked three questions. Who is on the board? Who could each director owe loyalty to? Who could veto a major decision?

*The numbers and where they came from.* The 2024 proxy statement (the document sent to shareholders before the annual meeting), filed 2024-04-11, lists nine directors: eight called independent under New York Stock Exchange rules, and the chief executive, Sudhakar Ramakrishna. File: `sources/filings/swi-def14a-2024.md`. Three of the eight independents are designees of Silver Lake, a private equity firm, so "independent" includes the owner's people. Silver Lake held 36.56% of the shares and Thoma Bravo, another private equity firm, held 29.79%. Together they held 66.35%. All officers and directors together held 1.95%. Under the stockholders' agreement, each firm had to give written consent before a change of control, a debt deal above $300.0 million, an asset deal above $300.0 million, or the hiring or firing of the chief executive. The sale: the deal document (`sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md`) says the two firms, with 111,564,519 shares, about 65% of the votes at that later date, signed a written consent on 2025-02-07, right after the merger agreement. No shareholder vote was held. A three-person committee of directors ran the process: William Bock, Easwaran Sundaram and Michael Widmann. Widmann is a managing director of Silver Lake. The filing says the committee was formed "for convenience and efficiency, and not due to any actual or perceived conflict of interest." Executives also receive golden parachutes, payments triggered by a change of control: $38.96 million for three executives, of which $1.2 million is a bonus for closing the deal at any price and the rest rises or falls with the $18.50 share price. The sale was also protected by two banks' opinions that the price was fair, and by appraisal rights, which let other shareholders ask a court to set a fair price.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* Power sits with the two big owners, who are separate holders and each held a veto. Managers hold little. We treat the $18.50 deal price as a real offer but not as proof of value, because the people who approved it were also sellers. The filings show Silver Lake at 36.56%.

*How sure we are.* High on who owns what and who approved the deal; the proxy and the deal document state them directly. We could not tell from the filings whether large institutions objected. None held 5%, and with no vote there was nothing to vote against.

## Step 3: What is SolarWinds trying to achieve, and did anything ever hold it to account?

*What we wanted to know.* What goals management states, which measures it actually steers by, and whether a crisis or scandal ever forced a correction. An objective is only useful if hitting it makes the company worth more, and the course says markets and owners eventually punish companies that break the rules.

*Why it matters.* If management chases a number that does not create value, such as sales growth bought with deep discounts, the company can look healthy while its worth falls. If a past shock left a lasting discount, we must decide whether our forecast should carry it, or we would overvalue the company.

*What we did.* We read the stated strategy in the annual report, then the pay plans in the proxy statement, because pay shows what management is rewarded for, and checked each measure against the underlying figures. Then we built a timeline of the cyberattack, the lawsuits and the leadership change, and compared how investors valued SolarWinds against similar software companies before and after. The measure is enterprise value, the price of the whole business with debt included, divided by the next twelve months of expected EBITDA, a rough measure of yearly cash profit. A multiple of 10 means buyers pay ten dollars for each dollar of expected cash profit.

*The numbers and where they came from.* The 2024 annual report (Form 10-K), Item 1, says the company intends to "extend our leadership in observability and IT infrastructure management and grow our market share in adjacent areas" through a "subscription-first approach." Observability means software that monitors computer systems. File: `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`. The 2024 proxy statement describes the 2023 pay plans: the annual cash bonus paid 50% on revenue and 50% on adjusted EBITDA, and the stock-unit awards paid 70% on Subscription ARR and 30% on revenue. File: `sources/filings/swi-def14a-2024.md`. ARR, annual recurring revenue, is the yearly value of contracts that renew. Subscription ARR grew 33.7%, from $233.2 million to $311.7 million. Total ARR, which adds older maintenance contracts on perpetual licenses, grew only 6.6%, from $684.1 million to $729.0 million (annual report, Item 7). So maintenance ARR fell by $33.6 million: subscription growth was $78.5 million, total growth $44.8 million, and more than two fifths of the headline growth was existing customers switching products. Net retention, the share of last year's subscription dollars still there this year, was 99% in 2024 and 97% in 2023. Sales and marketing spending fell 9.8%, from $249.3 million to $224.9 million. The fall includes $13.8 million from moving a team to another budget line and $11.4 million of cut marketing programs. Adjusted EBITDA, the company's own profit measure, was $384.7 million. The standardized measure we use is $282.8 million: operating income plus depreciation and amortization, with no add-back for stock-based pay.

The attack, known as SUNBURST, was disclosed in December 2020: malicious code had been inserted into updates of the Orion software. The company told about 33,000 Orion customers and believed fewer than 18,000 had an affected installation (`sources/filings/swi-8k-2020-12-14-sunburst-followup.md`). It has over 300,000 customers in all. Net costs after insurance were $26.2 million in 2022, a $2.1 million gain in 2023 and $10.3 million in 2024. A shareholder class action settled for $26 million, paid by insurance. The US Securities and Exchange Commission sued the company and its security chief on 2023-10-30; the court dismissed most of the claims and one remained when the annual report was filed on 2025-02-19. The CEO change was decided on 2020-12-07, a week before the attack was public (`sources/filings/swi-8k-2020-12-09-sunburst-disclosure.md`), so it was not a market correction. Goldman Sachs's table in the sale document (`sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md`) shows SolarWinds' average multiple at 15.9 in 2019 and 15.7 in 2020, against 10.9 and 10.8 for software peers, then 8.8 in 2024 against 12.8. A $100 investment at the end of 2019 was worth $51 at the end of 2023, against $211 for the proxy's peer group. The $18.50 offer was a 21.9% premium to the $15.18 price (arithmetic), just above the 25th percentile of 19% in Goldman's study of software deals (a percentile ranks a result among all the deals: a quarter of deals paid a premium of 19% or less; the 75th percentile, 50%, is the premium that three quarters of deals did not exceed).

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* Management states growth and market position, not value. Its measures are real but flattering. Subscription growth overstates new demand, and adjusted EBITDA is $101.9 million above our measure, mostly because it ignores $78.3 million of stock-based pay including employer payroll taxes ($76.5 million of stock-based pay alone), which is a real cost to owners. We use the standardized measure. We found no evidence of discounting or bundling to hit targets, and the filings give no way to test it, so we make no claim either way. The market's penalty for the crisis was large and lasted: SolarWinds went from a premium of about five times over peers (a multiple of 15.9 against 10.9) to a discount of about four times (8.8 against 12.8). We did not decide that SUNBURST caused all of it. The three bidders who withdrew in November 2023 gave "the relative early stage of the Company's ongoing transition to a subscription-first business model" as their reason, and the 2021 spin-off of the N-able business also changed the company. We treat the attack as a risk to cash flows, reflected through management's own forecast, and add no separate penalty.

*How sure we are.* High on the metrics, ARR figures, dates, costs and multiples, which are disclosed. Low on whether the company bought growth with price cuts, because no document gives discount data, and low on the cause of the lasting discount, because three explanations overlap.

## Step 4: Who sets SolarWinds' share price, and does that investor spread their bets?

*What we wanted to know.* How many kinds of shares exist, who the biggest shareholders are, and who the marginal investor is. The marginal investor is the buyer or seller whose trades set the price at the margin.

*Why it matters.* The cost of capital depends on how risky the company looks to the investor who sets its price. A diversified investor, who owns hundreds of stocks, cares only about risk that moves with the whole market. An investor with everything in one stock cares about all of it. We have to pick the right viewpoint.

*What we did.* We counted the share classes, read the ownership table, and asked who actually trades the stock. Then we asked whether the risks that hit SolarWinds were firm-specific or market-wide, since only market-wide risk is rewarded in the model we use.

*The numbers and where they came from.* The 2024 annual report (Form 10-K) says there is one class of common stock with one vote a share, and 50 million preferred shares authorized but none issued. File: `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`. Basic shares were 171.6 million on the record date in the sale document (`sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md`). The 2024 proxy statement ownership table lists Silver Lake at 36.56% and Thoma Bravo at 29.79%, with no other 5% holder (`sources/filings/swi-def14a-2024.md`). That leaves a public float of about 34%. The cover page of the annual report puts the stock held by non-affiliates at $661.3 million on 2024-06-28. In 2021 Goldman's table shows SolarWinds' multiple at 14.0 and peers at 12.0; in 2022 SolarWinds was at 9.9 and peers at 11.0. The peers' multiple dropped by 1.0, which was market-wide, and SolarWinds' extra drop of 3.1 was its own (arithmetic). In 2022 the company also wrote down goodwill, the premium paid in the 2016 buyout, by $891.1 million.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* The marginal investor is an institution: diversified funds holding the public float. The two sponsors hold two thirds of the shares but are not who trades at the margin, so they do not set the market price. That means a market-based measure of risk, beta (how far a stock moves when the whole market moves), is the right tool, and the cyberattack and the lawsuit are firm-specific risks that belong in the forecast of cash flows, not in the discount rate. We did not use a model that adds a premium to the cost of equity for small companies or for companies whose share price is low relative to book value (the accounting value of the equity). SolarWinds' equity value of $2,604.9 million puts it among small firms, the group Damodaran's small-firm rating table covers, and its price is 1.86 times book value against 4.58 times for peers. But the book value includes $2,363.2 million of goodwill from the 2016 buyout, so the low ratio says more about accounting than risk.

*How sure we are.* High on share classes and ownership, which are disclosed. The marginal investor is an inference: the filings do not list who traded the stock. The judgment that diversified institutions set the price is standard for a listed company with a one-third float.

## Step 5: What return could an investor earn with no risk at all, in dollars, on the day we value SolarWinds?

*What we wanted to know.* The risk-free rate: the yearly return on an investment with no chance of default. Every other return we estimate is built as this rate plus a reward for risk.

*Why it matters.* The risk-free rate is the base of the cost of equity, the return shareholders demand. A one-point error here moves the cost of equity by one point and changes the final value by hundreds of millions of dollars.

*What we did.* We chose the currency first, because there is no single global risk-free rate; each currency has its own. Then we took the matching government's 10-year bond rate on the analysis date, 2025-02-05, and subtracted any default spread, the extra yield investors demand because the government might not pay. The rule is: risk-free rate equals government bond rate minus default spread. For SolarWinds, 4.43% minus 0.00% is 4.43%. The date is the last trading day before the sale was announced on 2025-02-07, so the value can be compared with the prices at the time.

*The numbers and where they came from.* SolarWinds reports in US dollars, and its forecast and its loan are in dollars (2024 annual report, Form 10-K, Note 9 and the income statement; `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`). Part of its sales are in euros, British pounds and Australian dollars (annual report, Item 7A), and the company's European subsidiaries use the euro. The 10-year US Treasury closed at 4.43% on 2025-02-05, from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis's public database (FRED series DGS10). Moody's, a credit rating agency, rated the United States Aaa, its top grade, until 2025-05-16, so the default spread is 0.00% (Damodaran's January 2025 country table, `sources/market-data/country-risk-2025-01-01.json`). The rates are written up in `sources/market-data/rates-2025-02-05.md`. In the same table Germany and Australia are Aaa with a 0.00% spread and the United Kingdom is Aa3 with 0.59%. The buyer, Turn/River, financed the deal with dollar loans of $2,225.0 million, $200.0 million and $525.0 million (closing report, `sources/deal-docs/swi-8k-2025-04-16-merger-closing.md`). One date gap: the equity risk premium used in the next step is measured on Professor Damodaran's start-of-month Treasury rate of 4.54%, which is 0.11 points above the daily close of 4.43%.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* The risk-free rate is 4.43% in US dollars. This follows from the choices above. Foreign sales do not change the currency: what matters is that the cash flows and the discount rate are in the same currency, and the forecast is in dollars. The buyer's new debt changes how much the company borrows, which is handled elsewhere, not the risk-free rate. The 0.11-point date gap is inside the 0.25-point tolerance the valuation method sets for when the daily and monthly rates differ (a larger gap would require showing the cost of capital at both rates), so we use the daily close and do not adjust. We did not net a spread from the foreign slice, because the annual report gives no revenue split by currency.

*How sure we are.* High on the 4.43%, a market rate read from the public database. The one judgment is the currency, and every document points to dollars. The rate would be different, and a default spread would appear, if we valued on a date after the May 2025 downgrade.

## Step 6: How much extra return do investors demand for owning stocks instead of risk-free bonds?

*What we wanted to know.* The equity risk premium: the extra yearly return investors expect from stocks as a whole above the risk-free rate. It is the price of market risk.

*Why it matters.* The cost of equity is the risk-free rate plus the company's risk measure times this premium. A premium that is too high makes the company look riskier and its shares worth less.

*What we did.* There are three ways to estimate the premium. A survey asks investors. A historical premium averages what stocks earned above bonds in the past. An implied premium works backward from today's stock prices and expected cash flows: it is the discount rate that makes the expected cash flows of the market equal today's index level. We chose the implied premium, because it is forward-looking and tied to the valuation date. We then compared the cost of equity under the others. The formula is: cost of equity equals risk-free rate plus beta times the premium. Beta is the stock's sensitivity to the market; for SolarWinds it is 1.6714. With a premium of 4.27%, the cost of equity is 4.43% plus 1.6714 times 4.27%, which is 11.57%.

*The numbers and where they came from.* The implied premium is 4.27%, from Professor Damodaran's February 2025 workbook, with the S&P 500 index at 6040.53 (`sources/market-data/market-rates-2025-02-05.json`, dated 2025-02-01). The historical premium for 1928 to 2022, stocks over Treasury bonds, is 6.64% as an arithmetic average with a standard error of 2.15% (the typical size of the estimate's error), and 5.06% as a compounded (geometric) average, from the course spreadsheet (`01-foundations-and-discount-rates/lesson-06/spreadsheet-histretsp.md`, which stops at 2022). On those, the cost of equity is 15.53% and 12.89%. Survey premiums for February 2025 are not in our files. The next step adjusts the premium for where the company sells; the final premium is 4.92% and the cost of equity 12.65% (model-output.md §1). Country premiums are the mature-market premium plus a scaled default spread: each country's default spread times 1.3475, the ratio of equity risk to bond risk in Damodaran's January 2025 table (`sources/market-data/country-risk-2025-01-01.json`). The United States spread is 0.00%, so the scaling does nothing to US sales. For the international slice, using raw spreads instead would lower the weighted premium from 4.92% to about 4.75% and the cost of equity by about 0.28 points.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* We use the implied premium of 4.27% as the mature-market base. That is a judgment among three methods. The historical figures are too noisy to rely on: the 6.64% average carries a 2.15% standard error, about a third of the premium. We kept the implied premium even though SolarWinds is now private, because at the valuation date it was listed and the premium measures the market's price of risk, not the company. We did not compute a survey-based cost of equity, since no survey figure was available. The scaled-spread method is Damodaran's and we adopted it unchanged.

*How sure we are.* Moderate. The 4.27% is a published figure, but any premium is an estimate. The range from 4.27% to 6.64% moves the cost of equity from 11.57% to 15.53%, so this is one of the largest judgments in the valuation, and its choice is explained rather than proven.

## Step 7: Does where SolarWinds sells change the price of risk?

*What we wanted to know.* How much of SolarWinds' business is outside the United States and what extra risk premium that adds. A company's risk comes from where it does business, not where it is incorporated.

*Why it matters.* Sales in riskier countries should carry a higher premium. If we ignore a large international share, the cost of equity is too low and the value too high.

*What we did.* We split revenue by region, assigned each region an equity risk premium, and weighted the premiums by revenue. The formula is: weighted premium equals the sum of each region's revenue share times its premium. With SolarWinds' numbers, 64.82% times 4.27% plus 35.18% times 6.11% is 4.92%. We chose revenue as the measure because it is the only split the annual report gives that follows customers.

*The numbers and where they came from.* The 2024 annual report (Form 10-K), Note 16, gives revenue by customer shipping address: United States $516.6 million and international $280.3 million out of $796.9 million. No country other than the United States reached 10% of revenue. File: `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`. That is 64.82% and 35.18%. The US premium is the mature-market premium, 4.27%, because the US had a default spread of 0.00%. For the international slice we added 1.84 points, the EMEA (Europe, Middle East and Africa) regional premium of 6.17% less the mature-market premium of 4.33%, both in Damodaran's January 2025 country workbook (`sources/market-data/country-risk-2025-01-01.json`). Damodaran measures each regional premium against his January mature-market premium of 4.33%, so we add that 1.84-point difference to the February mature-market premium of 4.27%. That makes 6.11% for the international slice. The annual report also says the company has 2,107 employees, 1,379 of them outside the United States and 728 inside. The disclosed foreign currencies are the euro, pound and Australian dollar.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* The weighted premium is 4.92%. The international premium is a judgment: the annual report does not say which regions the sales are in, so we used the EMEA premium for all of it because most international offices are in Europe. The alternatives were the Western Europe premium of 1.12 points, which gives a weighted premium of 4.66%, and the global average of 1.43 points, which gives 4.77%. We chose EMEA because it is the cautious choice. Western Europe may fit the disclosed currencies better. The gap between 4.66% and 4.92% is about 0.4 points of cost of equity. We did not weight by staff, even though 65% of employees are abroad, because customers' economies drive cash flows, and staff location matters more for costs and currency.

*How sure we are.* High on the 64.82% and 35.18% split, which is disclosed. The premium for the international slice is estimated, since the region is assumed. The weighted premium would change if the company disclosed regional sales, if Damodaran updated his premiums, which he does each January and July, or if the US rating fell, as it did in May 2025, putting a default spread on the 64.82% US slice.

## Step 8: Can SolarWinds' own share-price history tell us how risky it is?

*What we wanted to know.* SolarWinds' regression beta: the slope from comparing the stock's past returns with the market's. Beta measures how much a stock moves when the market moves. We also wanted to know whether such an estimate is reliable enough to use.

*Why it matters.* Beta sets the cost of equity. If we use a noisy beta, the cost of equity, and so the value, can be wrong by a wide margin. This is the one input in the cost of equity that the company's own history could inform.

*What we did.* A regression beta needs four choices: an index to compare against, how often to measure returns, how far back to go, and whether to include dividends. The usual choices are the S&P 500, weekly or monthly returns, five years, and dividends included. We checked whether SolarWinds' history allowed those choices and whether the series was clean. We could not get the price history, so no regression was run.

*The numbers and where they came from.* SolarWinds stock traded on the New York Stock Exchange from 2018-10-19 until the sale closed on 2025-04-16 (2018 annual report cover, `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2018.md`; closing report, `sources/deal-docs/swi-8k-2025-04-16-merger-closing.md`). Several events break the series. The company spun off N-able, a separate business, on 2021-07-19, did a one-for-two reverse split on 2021-07-30, and paid a $1.50 special dividend in 2021 and a $1.00 special dividend of $168.2 million in 2024 (annual reports, `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2023.md` and `swi-10k-fy2024.md`). After the sale was announced on 2025-02-07, the price stayed near the $18.50 offer, so a usable window ends on the last trading day before, 2025-02-05. Stock held by non-affiliates, meaning not by the owners with control, was worth only $661.3 million on 2024-06-28, so trading was thin. Free sources no longer serve the history of a delisted stock, and Capital IQ's chart did not load (`sources/README.md`). The ledger lists the regression beta, its standard error (the typical size of its error), its R-squared (the share of the stock's movements the market explains), and Jensen's alpha (the return the stock earned above what its beta predicts) as omitted (`inputs.yaml`, `meta.omitted`).

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* We did not use a regression beta. The decision does not depend only on missing data. Even with data, a window after the spin-off would be about three and a half years, and it would include the cyberattack, a thin float and a price pinned by a pending deal. We instead use the bottom-up beta: the unlevered beta of the software industry (the industry figure explained in step 10, 1.22), from Damodaran's January 2025 data on 333 firms, relevered, meaning adjusted upward for the risk SolarWinds' own debt adds, to 1.6714. This is a judgment; the course prefers it anyway. We did not fill the gap with the four-year pay-versus-performance table in the proxy, since four points cannot support a regression.

*How sure we are.* High that no usable regression exists. The 1.22 industry beta is an average, so SolarWinds' own risk could differ, and the relevered figure rests on the debt ratio of 33.03% (model-output.md §1). A price history, if found, would be a cross-check and not a replacement.

## Step 9: Why is SolarWinds' stock risky, and how much of that risk comes from its debt?

*What we wanted to know.* How much a SolarWinds share would swing when the stock market swings, and what causes that. The measure is called beta. A beta of 1.0 means the stock moves in step with the market; above 1.0 it moves more, below 1.0 it moves less.

*Why it matters.* Beta sets the return shareholders demand, and that demand is the largest piece of the discount rate used to value the company. A beta that is too low makes every later answer too high.

*What we did.* The course says three things set a beta: what the company sells, how many of its costs are fixed, and how much it borrows. We asked about each in turn. For the third, the formula is: levered beta = unlevered beta × (1 + (1 − tax rate) × debt ÷ equity). Unlevered beta is the risk of the business alone, with no debt. Levered beta adds the risk that borrowing piles on top, because interest must be paid even in a bad year, which makes the shareholders' leftover profit swing harder. With SolarWinds' numbers: 1.22 × (1 + (1 − 0.25) × 0.4933) = 1.6714.

*The numbers and where they came from.* The 2024 annual report (Form 10-K, filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) on 2025-02-19) shows what customers do. Subscription customers renewed and spent more at a net retention rate of 99%, and customers of its older perpetual-license products renewed their maintenance contracts at 97%. Recurring revenue (subscriptions plus maintenance) was 93.5% of the $796.9 million of revenue. The company had more than 300,000 customers. The same report shows the cost structure: the direct cost of delivering the product was $83.3 million, about 10.5% of revenue, while the running costs of the company (mostly salaries for 2,107 employees) were $505.2 million, 63.4% of revenue. File: `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`. Debt of $1,285.0 million and equity of $2,604.9 million give debt to equity of 0.4933 (the model's output, section 1; `model-output.md`). The tax rate is 25%, federal plus state, from Professor Damodaran's January 2025 tax table (`sources/market-data/country-risk-2025-01-01.json`). The unlevered beta of 1.22 is the average of 333 software companies from his January 2025 beta file (`sources/market-data/industry-betas-2025-01-05.json`).

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* Levered beta is 1.6714. Debt adds 0.4514 to the business beta of 1.22. That is 37.0% on top of it, and 27.0% of the final figure. The product is not the source of the risk: customers keep it. Two things raise the beta. The first is fixed costs. When revenue grew 5.0% from 2023 to 2024, operating income grew 38.6%, from $150.4 million to $208.4 million. The same leverage works in reverse in a bad year. The second is borrowing. Separating business risk from fixed-cost risk is a judgment, because both sit inside the industry's 1.22; only the debt part is arithmetic. We did not lower 1.22 to reflect the sticky product. One company's renewal rates are a reason to doubt the industry figure, not a measurement that replaces it. We also have no beta from SolarWinds' own price history: the stock was delisted in April 2025, free price sources no longer serve it, and the company's business and debt changed too much since the 2016 buyout, the 2018 re-listing and the 2021 spin-off of its managed-service-provider business, N-able, for an old regression to describe it.

*How sure we are.* The retention, revenue, cost and debt figures are read directly from the annual report. The 1.22 is an industry estimate, not a SolarWinds measurement. A different peer group, such as Software (Internet) at 1.60, would give a much higher beta, and a beta below 1.0 is hard to defend for a leveraged software firm.

## Step 10: How risky is SolarWinds' business, measured from similar companies?

*What we wanted to know.* The risk of SolarWinds' business before any debt (its unlevered beta), estimated from other companies in the same line of work rather than from SolarWinds' own price history.

*Why it matters.* This one number is the base of the cost of equity. It is multiplied up for debt in step 9's formula, so an error here flows into every value in the project.

*What we did.* The course prefers a bottom-up beta: take the unlevered betas of many companies in the same business and average them. An average of many firms has less noise than one firm's price history, and it describes the business as it is now. Then we asked whether SolarWinds is one business or several. If it were several, each would need its own average, weighted by its share of the company's value. Professor Damodaran's data sets group companies by industry and give each group's average beta, already stripped of debt and corrected for cash. Cash is removed because cash is nearly riskless and would hide the business's true risk.

*The numbers and where they came from.* The 2024 annual report (Form 10-K), note 16: "We operate as a single reportable segment." The chief executive makes decisions on the consolidated company. The only split is by type of contract: subscription $304.5 million (38.2% of revenue), maintenance $440.6 million (55.3%) and license $51.8 million (6.5%). File: `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`. The beta comes from Damodaran's January 2025 file for US software (system and application) companies: 333 firms, a levered beta of 1.24, average debt to equity of 4.9%, an unlevered beta of 1.1960, and cash at 1.74% of firm value. Taking cash out gives 1.22. File: `sources/market-data/industry-betas-2025-01-05.json`.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* One business, one beta: 1.22, applied to the whole company. The contract split describes how customers pay, not what they buy, and no filing says how much profit or capital each product uses, so any segment weights would have been invented. There is a plausible candidate for a riskier piece: the observability subscription business grew its revenue 30.0% in 2024, while maintenance fell 4.6%. A group like Software (Internet), with an unlevered beta of 1.60, shows how far apart the two could be. We use that figure only as an illustration of the gap, not as an input. A single rate treats the two alike: the new product's cash flows would be discounted at too low a rate for their risk, and the renewals' at too high a rate. The company-wide figures are a cost of equity of 12.65% and a cost of capital of 10.23% (step 13). We chose the January 2025 file because the valuation date is 2025-02-05.

*How sure we are.* The company's single-segment reporting is disclosed, so that part is solid. The 1.22 is an estimate: the choice of industry group is a judgment, and the average could move if the group were drawn differently.

## Step 11: Is the market-wide measure of risk the right one for SolarWinds, and is the sample big enough?

*What we wanted to know.* Two checks on the beta from step 10. First, should the group of comparison companies be drawn from the whole world instead of only the US? Second, is the usual beta the right kind of risk measure for SolarWinds' owners, or should a bigger one be used because they could not spread their money across many investments?

*Why it matters.* A wrong choice here would move the cost of equity by many percentage points. The second question can multiply the beta several times over.

*What we did.* On the first, the course goes global when a home country has too few companies. Professor Damodaran did that for Brazilian companies. We checked how many US companies are in SolarWinds' group and where its customers are. On the second, ordinary beta measures only market risk, the part of a stock's risk that cannot be removed by owning many stocks. That is right for investors who own many stocks. An owner with all their money in one company faces all of its risk, and for them the course uses total beta: market beta divided by the correlation between the stock and the market. Correlation runs from 0 to 1. Dividing by a number well below 1 makes the beta much larger. So we asked who owned SolarWinds and who set its price.

*The numbers and where they came from.* The US software group has 333 firms (`sources/market-data/industry-betas-2025-01-05.json`). The 2024 annual report (Form 10-K, note 16) gives US revenue of 64.82% of the total and international revenue of 35.18% (`sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`). The 2024 proxy statement, the document sent to shareholders before the annual meeting, shows Silver Lake owned 36.56% and Thoma Bravo 29.79% (`sources/filings/swi-def14a-2024.md`). Together that is 66.35%. The remaining float of roughly 34% was held by diversified institutions. A global beta file was not compiled for this project. For an illustration of total beta, the only correlation available is Damodaran's January 2024 industry average for this group, 0.3374, from the course spreadsheet `01-foundations-and-discount-rates/lesson-10/spreadsheet-totalbeta24.md`.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* We stayed with US companies. The sample of 333 is large, the product risk is the same everywhere SolarWinds sells, and a global group would add companies with other accounting rules and regulation. The 35.18% of sales abroad is handled where it belongs, in the equity risk premium of 4.92% (the extra return investors demand for owning stocks instead of government bonds, weighted by where the revenue is earned). We used ordinary beta throughout: levered beta 1.6714 (model output, section 1). The price was set by diversified institutions, and Silver Lake and Thoma Bravo are private-equity firms that each own many companies. As an illustration only, total beta would be 1.6714 ÷ 0.3374 = 4.95, and a cost of equity of 4.43% + 4.95 × 4.92% = 28.8%, against 12.65% in the model. We did not use it. It mixes a 2024 correlation with 2025 inputs, and it describes an owner who holds nothing else. No SolarWinds owner fits that description: not the sponsors in 2016 to 2018, not the public shareholders, and not Turn/River Capital, the private-equity firm that agreed to buy it in 2025. The closest argument for a partial adjustment is the public period, when two holders with 66.35% could not easily sell. That shows up in the price someone will pay for control (the deal premium), not in the discount rate.

*How sure we are.* Ownership figures are disclosed. The choice of ordinary beta is a judgment with a strong basis. The total-beta figures are an illustration built on an old industry average, not a measurement of SolarWinds, because the company has no price history to measure its own correlation.

## Step 12: How much does SolarWinds really owe, and what does it pay to borrow?

*What we wanted to know.* The company's total debt, and the interest rate on it. Debt matters because it makes the company riskier and because lenders get paid before shareholders.

*Why it matters.* Debt sets the weights in the cost of capital and is subtracted from the company's value to find what the shares are worth. Too low a debt figure makes the shares look more valuable than they are. The interest rate sets the cost of the borrowed third of the funding (33% of the company's value).

*What we did.* Professor Damodaran's test: a commitment is debt if the payments are fixed, they are tax-deductible, and missing them can cost the owners control of the company. We tested each item that looked like debt. Face value is the amount the lender is owed. Book value is the accounting figure after fees and discounts have been subtracted. We used face value, because the lender is owed the full principal. For the interest rate, there are three routes. An agency rating (from S&P or Moody's, firms that grade borrowers) comes first if one exists. Next is a synthetic rating built from interest coverage: operating income divided by interest expense, matched to a table of ratings and the extra interest each rating pays above a safe government bond. Best of all is the rate the company actually pays, because lenders set it.

*The numbers and where they came from.* All from the 2024 annual report (Form 10-K) filed with the SEC on 2025-02-19: balance sheet and notes 7 (leases), 9 (debt) and 15 (commitments). File: `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`. The term loan has a face value of $1,235.7 million; the balance sheet carries it at $1,206.6 million after $29.1 million of unamortized fees and discount. Lease liabilities are $49.4 million. Deferred revenue is $389.3 million. The company has minimum purchase commitments for cloud hosting of $68.4 million. The loan pays the secured overnight financing rate (SOFR, a benchmark short-term rate) plus 2.75%, and its effective rate at year-end 2024 was 7.11%. At the end of 2023 it was 9.11%. The $130.0 million revolving credit line was unused. Operating income was $208.4 million, interest expense $112.4 million. Acquisition amortization (the accounting charge for intangible assets bought in deals) was $52.9 million. The ratings table is Damodaran's January 2025 edition for firms with a market value under $5 billion (`sources/market-data/ratings-spreads-2025-01-01.json`). The risk-free rate that day was 4.43%.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* Total debt is $1,285.0 million: $1,235.7 million of loan at face plus $49.4 million of leases (arithmetic once the inputs are chosen). Leases pass the test: SolarWinds rents all its offices, the payments are fixed, and walking away is not an option. Deferred revenue is not debt. It is cash customers paid in advance for software, so SolarWinds owes them service, not cash. The cloud purchase commitments are a judgment call, and we ruled them out. They are payments for a service the company uses, and missing one is a contract dispute, not a default that hands lenders control. Counting them would add $68.4 million, 5.3% to debt. No guarantees of other companies' debt exist, and no interest was capitalized into construction. Depreciation lives for equipment, 3 to 5 years, did not change. The loan floats with SOFR, so its market value is its face value. No agency rating is disclosed, so we built the synthetic one. The answer depends on which earnings figure goes on top of the ratio. With operating income, coverage is 1.85 times and the rating is B-, implying a cost of debt of 8.85%. Adding back acquisition amortization gives $261.3 million, 2.32 times, B and 7.43%. Using standardized EBITDA of $282.8 million, which also adds back depreciation, gives 2.52 times, B+ and 7.04%. We chose operating income for the other steps, because stock compensation and the like are real costs. None of the three is used for the cost of debt. We used the 7.11% the loan actually pays, because lenders priced it with the credit agreement in hand. The synthetic 8.85% is 1.74 points higher. The loan is secured and ranks first among creditors, lenders to software companies with 93.5% recurring revenue look at cash earnings (EBITDA), and the margin was negotiated, not set by formula. The tax rate for the after-tax cost of debt is 25%, so the cost is 7.11% × (1 − 0.25) = 5.33%.

*How sure we are.* High on the debt: every figure is disclosed. The $68.4 million treatment is the one open call, and its effect is stated. The 7.11% is a year-end rate, close to the 7.08% implied by SOFR on the analysis date.

## Step 13: What mix of debt and equity funds SolarWinds, and what does the blend cost?

*What we wanted to know.* The company's cost of capital: the average return that the people who funded SolarWinds, lenders and shareholders, require together. Also whether different parts of the business should be held to different rates.

*Why it matters.* The cost of capital is the hurdle rate. Every future cash flow is discounted at it, so a point of error moves the final value a lot. It also sets the bar any investment has to clear.

*What we did.* The weighted average cost of capital is: share of equity × cost of equity + share of debt × cost of debt after tax. The shares are measured at market value, what investors would pay today, not book value, the accounting figure for what was put in. The course says there is no good reason for book weights, because the cost of capital is what the claims are worth now. Debt includes leases. With SolarWinds' numbers: 66.97% × 12.65% + 33.03% × 5.33% = 10.23%. The cost of equity is the risk-free rate plus beta times the equity risk premium: 4.43% + 1.6714 × 4.92% = 12.65%.

*The numbers and where they came from.* Debt of $1,285.0 million comes from step 12. Equity of $2,604.9 million is 171.6 million shares times $15.18, the closing price on 2025-02-05, the last day before the sale to Turn/River Capital was announced (share count from the information statement filed on 2025-03-27, `sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md`; price from the Goldman Sachs opinion in the same document). Book equity of $1,400.7 million and book debt of $1,206.6 million are from the 2024 annual report balance sheet (`sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`). The interest rate of 7.11% is the loan's own rate, and the tax rate is 25%. The beta of 1.6714 is from step 9, the 4.92% premium is the US and international blend of Professor Damodaran's January 2025 figures, and the risk-free rate is the 10-year Treasury close of 4.43% on 2025-02-05 (the US Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis database). The results, a cost of capital of 10.23% and a cost of equity of 12.65%, are the model's, section 1 (`model-output.md`).

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* Debt is 33.03% of the firm's $3,889.9 million total value, and equity is 66.97%. The cost of capital is 10.23%. This is arithmetic once the inputs are fixed. At book weights, debt would be 47.28%, counting leases, and the cost of capital 9.19%, 1.04 points lower. The book version leans too much on debt, the cheaper source, because the market values the equity at 1.86 times its book value. Leases add $49.4 million, 1.3 points of the debt weight, small enough that they do not change the answer, but we count them as the course teaches. The $187.0 million is the value of the stock options and stock units (promises of shares) that employees hold, which would become shares in a sale, and it is kept out of the weights and subtracted separately in the valuation, a judgment call. We used the $15.18 undisturbed price, not the $18.50 deal price, which already included the buyer's premium. SolarWinds reports one segment, so there are no divisional rates. If the observability subscription business were split out, it would carry a higher rate than the maintenance base, so we treat 10.23% as the company-wide bar and apply judgment on top. SolarWinds has no preferred stock or convertible debt to divide between debt and equity.

*How sure we are.* The weights and the debt figure are disclosed or are arithmetic on disclosed numbers. The weak points are the inputs: the beta of 1.22 before debt is an industry estimate, and the 4.92% premium includes a judgment that all international revenue carries the premium for Europe, the Middle East and Africa. A 1.0 point change in the premium moves the cost of equity by about 1.7 points.

## Step 14: Did SolarWinds earn more on its investments than they cost?

*What we wanted to know.* How much profit SolarWinds made last year for every dollar invested in the business, and whether that beat the 10.23% cost of capital. Also how operating profit turns into cash.

*Why it matters.* A company that earns less than its cost of capital is shrinking the value of what owners put in, even while reporting a profit. It tells us how much to trust management's forecast of good returns.

*What we did.* Return on invested capital (ROIC) is after-tax operating income divided by invested capital, the money tied up in the business. Operating income is profit before interest and taxes. We taxed it at the 25% rate: $208.4 million × (1 − 0.25) = $156.3 million. Then ROIC = $156.3 million ÷ $2,348.0 million = 6.66%. We compared it with the 10.23% cost of capital. Return on equity is net income divided by the book equity. We also turned operating income into cash, the course's rule: free cash flow to the firm equals after-tax operating income, plus depreciation and amortization (expenses that take no cash), minus capital spending, minus any rise in working capital (the cash tied up in day-to-day operations).

*The numbers and where they came from.* All from the 2024 annual report (Form 10-K) filed with the SEC on 2025-02-19, income statement, cash flow statement and balance sheet; file `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`. Operating income $208.4 million; depreciation and amortization $74.4 million, of which $52.9 million is amortization of intangible assets from acquisitions; capital spending $20.5 million; working capital rose by $38.8 million; net income $111.9 million; book equity $1,400.7 million; goodwill $2,363.2 million; invested capital $2,348.0 million (book equity plus debt less cash). Goodwill is what the buyers paid above the value of SolarWinds' identifiable assets in the 2016 buyout. Results from the model, section 8 (`model-output.md`): a return on invested capital of 6.66% and economic value added of −$84.0 million.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* ROIC is 6.66%, against a cost of capital of 10.23%, a gap of 3.58 points. Economic value added, the gap times the capital, is −$84.0 million: the capital earned that much less than its charge. Return on equity is 7.99% against a cost of equity of 12.65%, a gap of 4.66 points. SolarWinds does not clear its hurdle. Goodwill is 100.65% of invested capital. Without it, invested capital is −$15.2 million, and a return on negative capital means nothing. Operating assets are mostly paid for by customers in advance, deferred revenue of $389.3 million. So the business earns a great deal on its working assets and a poor return on the price paid for the company. Both statements are true: the 6.66% judges the purchase, the other describes the operations. Over time, invested capital hardly moved: $2,422.9 million in 2022, $2,356.3 million in 2023, $2,348.0 million in 2024. The return rose because profit did. Operating income was −$819.6 million in 2022 as reported, or $86.8 million once the $906.4 million of non-cash write-downs is taken out, then $150.4 million in 2023 and $208.4 million in 2024. The same method gives 2.69% for 2022 and 4.79% for 2023. Cash: $156.3 million + $74.4 million − $20.5 million − $38.8 million = $171.4 million of free cash flow in 2024. Of the $74.4 million gap between EBITDA and operating income, $52.9 million is old acquisition cost wearing off, with no cash and no reinvestment, and the other $21.5 million is the depreciation of equipment and software, about the same as the $20.5 million spent on new capital. Stock compensation of $76.5 million is treated as a real expense and not added back, a judgment call. Research and development of $108.6 million is expensed; capitalizing it would change profit and capital, not cash, so we left it. A typical SolarWinds project is a repeated, small product-development spend paid back over years of renewals, plus occasional large acquisitions that built the goodwill. Management's forecast is organic: revenue from $832.0 million in 2025 to $1,273.0 million in 2030. Its 2030 free cash flow of $419.0 million exceeds after-tax operating income of $383.3 million. The reason is a forecast $46 million rise in deferred revenue (customers paying ahead) and cash taxes of about 25% of operating income. It is not depreciation running ahead of capital spending: in 2030 depreciation and amortization is $25 million and capital spending $27 million. Growing that cash flow forever would assume no reinvestment, and the valuation corrects that later.

*How sure we are.* The inputs are disclosed. The trend years use the same method on ledger figures. The meaning of the ROIC depends on goodwill, which reflects a 2016 price, so read 6.66% as a verdict on that purchase and not on current operations.

## Step 15: What does a typical SolarWinds project look like in cash, and what growth rate can it assume forever?

*What we wanted to know.* How cash moves through a typical SolarWinds project, which money already spent should be ignored, and how fast the company can be assumed to grow after the years we forecast. A project here means one customer contract or one bought product.

*Why it matters.* Forgetting what is already spent makes a project look worse than it is. A growth rate that is too high makes the whole company look worth more than it is, because the growth is assumed to last for ever.

*What we did.* First, the sunk-cost test. A sunk cost is money already spent that no decision can get back. Ask "what happens if I take the project, and what happens if I don't?" Only costs that differ between the two answers count. Second, the shape of the cash. SolarWinds sells software that customers pay for in advance and then renew. Third, the growth limit. A company cannot grow faster than the economy for ever, and the interest rate on safe government bonds (the risk-free rate, 4.43%) stands in for the economy's growth. The value of everything after the forecast years is the terminal value: next year's cash flow divided by the discount rate minus the growth rate. With the model's inputs, growth is 2.5%, and the discount rate is the company's 10.23% cost of capital.

*The numbers and where they came from.* The company's 2024 annual report (Form 10-K, filed with the US regulator, the SEC, on 2025-02-19) gives goodwill of $2,363.2 million, the amount paid for the company above the value of its assets in the 2016 buyout. It also gives research spending of $108.6 million, capital spending of $20.5 million, revenue of $796.9 million and deferred revenue of $389.3 million, which is cash customers paid before they got the service. File: `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`. The 10-K's segment note says the company reports one segment, so overhead is not split by product. The deal document (the information statement SolarWinds filed about its sale to Turn/River Capital) gives the two banks' growth rates: Goldman Sachs 2% to 3%, Jefferies 2.5% to 3.5%. File: `sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md`. Management's forecast takes revenue from $796.9 million in 2024 to $1,273.0 million in 2030, 8.12% a year. Terminal values from the model: $5,552.5 million if the 2030 cash flow is simply grown, $3,808.9 million if the company must reinvest to grow (model-output.md §6).

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* The $2,363.2 million of goodwill is sunk and ignored. Overhead is mostly not tied to any one project, so it is not charged to one. A typical project brings cash in first, through advance payment, and costs little afterward. Value rests on how many customers renew. We chose growth of 2.5% after 2030. That is a judgment, set from the middle of the banks' ranges. It sits well under the 4.43% risk-free rate, and neither bank went above it. The 8.12% growth in the forecast years is above the risk-free rate but lasts six years only. The bigger judgment is the cash flow that gets grown. Management's 2030 cash flow is $419.0 million, and that is more than its after-tax operating profit, mainly because deferred revenue is forecast to rise. Growing it for ever assumes the company never reinvests, so the model uses the $3,808.9 million version.

*How sure we are.* The cost figures and banks' ranges are disclosed. The 2.5% growth rate and the choice of terminal method are our judgment. A growth rate of 3% or a different reinvestment assumption would move the value per share noticeably.

## Step 16: In what currency does SolarWinds operate, and does a foreign dollar deserve a different discount rate?

*What we wanted to know.* Which currency to value the foreign business in, which discount rate to use for it, and whether SolarWinds protects itself against currency, interest-rate and cyber risks.

*Why it matters.* Mixing currencies, with euro cash flows discounted at a dollar rate, gives a wrong value. And paying to hedge a risk that shareholders could remove themselves for free wastes money.

*What we did.* The rule is that cash flows and discount rate must be in the same currency. Done consistently, a project is worth the same in euros as in dollars. The euro discount rate equals (1 + dollar rate) × (1 + euro inflation) ÷ (1 + dollar inflation) − 1. We did not convert anything. We kept everything in dollars. The foreign risk goes into the equity risk premium, the extra return investors demand for owning shares instead of safe bonds. The premium is the average of one rate for US revenue and a higher one for international revenue. For hedging, the test is whether the benefit exceeds the cost, and whether investors could do it more cheaply.

*The numbers and where they came from.* All revenue is reported in dollars. International revenue is 35.18% of the total and US revenue 64.82% (2024 annual report, note 16). File: `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`. The premium is 4.27% on US revenue and 6.11% on international revenue, which gives 4.92% (Damodaran's January and February 2025 data; the 6.11% adds a 1.84% Europe, Middle East and Africa premium, because most international offices are in Europe). Files: `sources/market-data/`. The cost of capital is 10.23% (model-output.md §1). The annual report's market-risk section (Item 7A) says the company sells in euros, pounds and Australian dollars, has no interest-rate swaps, hedges currency only to a limited extent and uses forward contracts for some balance-sheet positions. A one-point rise in rates would add about $12.4 million of interest a year, against interest expense of $112.4 million. Cyber insurance covers $15 million and directors-and-officers insurance $50 million. Net cyber-incident costs after insurance were $26.2 million (2022), a $2.1 million recovery (2023) and $10.3 million (2024). The maintenance renewal rate, the share of support contracts renewed, fell from 94% to 91% after the 2020 attack and was 97% in 2024.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* One dollar cost of capital, 10.23%, for the whole company. The business is the same product in every country, so the risk of the business, measured by its beta (1.22 before debt effects), does not change by country. Euro-based discounting would add an assumption we have no data for. On hedging, we judge the open positions small against earnings. The floating-rate loan and currency translation are not hedged, and neither is large against operating profit of $208.4 million. The $26 million securities class-action settlement was repaid in full by the directors-and-officers insurance, which is the clearest case of insurance paying for itself. The cyber policy was small against the losses. Customers stayed, which is evidence security spending worked, but the company did not disclose what it spent.

*How sure we are.* The exposure and insurance facts are disclosed. The 6.11% international premium is a judgment. A Western Europe premium alone would be lower and the global average also lower, so the 4.92% blend could be a little high. The security spend is unknown.

## Step 17: How has SolarWinds paid for itself, and is debt helping or hurting?

*What we wanted to know.* The kinds of money SolarWinds has raised, how its debt has changed since the buyout, and whether the benefits of borrowing outweigh the costs.

*Why it matters.* The benefit of debt is a lower tax bill. The cost is the risk of being unable to pay. The balance decides whether the company should borrow more or less, which sets the next three steps.

*What we did.* We listed the funding from most debt-like to most equity-like, then weighed the trade-off. Debt's main benefit is that interest is deducted before tax. The tax saved equals interest times the tax rate. Its main costs are the chance of failing to pay, the freedom lenders take away through promises (covenants) in the loan contract, and a fixed bill that must be paid even in a bad year.

*The numbers and where they came from.* From the 2024 annual report (Form 10-K): lease obligations $49.4 million, a term loan of $1,235.7 million at its face value, a credit line (revolver) of $130.0 million that was unused, deferred revenue of $389.3 million, share awards to employees worth $187.0 million, and interest expense of $112.4 million. The 2018 annual report (`sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2018.md`) gives debt of $2,358.1 million at the end of 2017, including a $680.0 million second loan that ranked behind the first, and $1,970.1 million at the end of 2018, after the October 2018 share sale that raised $375.0 million at $15.00 a share. Debt was $1,319.2 million in 2022, $1,300.4 million in 2023 and $1,285.0 million in 2024. The tax rate is 25%. Cash taxes paid in 2024 were $61.5 million, cash interest paid $104.5 million and cash from operations $188.3 million. At closing of the sale, Turn/River's new debt was a $2.225 billion first loan and a $525.0 million second loan.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* The debt is a leftover from the 2016 buyout and has been paid down as the company matured. Today it is 33.03% of the company's total value (model-output.md §1), against a median of about 12% for eight comparable companies. The tax saving is interest of $112.4 million times 25%, which is arithmetic and gives $28.1 million a year, and the company pays enough tax to use it. The costs are mostly lost flexibility: more than half of cash from operations goes to interest, and interest is covered only 1.85 times by operating profit, a "B-" level (model-output.md §2). Judged on the trade-off alone, the company carries somewhat too much debt.

*How sure we are.* The funding amounts are disclosed. "Somewhat too much" is a judgment from a qualitative weighing. The next step tests it with numbers.

## Step 18: How much debt would make SolarWinds' cost of capital lowest?

*What we wanted to know.* The mix of debt and shares at which the company's overall cost of funding, its cost of capital, is lowest, and where SolarWinds stands against it.

*Why it matters.* Cost of capital is the rate used to discount every future cash flow. A lower one raises the value of the company. Too much debt raises it again as the risk of failure grows.

*What we did.* The model tries debt ratios from 0% to 70%. At each one it does three things. It raises the shares' risk (their beta) because more debt makes shares riskier. It recomputes interest coverage, which is operating profit divided by interest. It then picks the credit rating that coverage earns, from Damodaran's January 2025 table for small companies. The rating sets the interest rate. The new interest changes coverage, so the loop repeats until the rating stops changing. The cost of capital is the shares' share of funding times their cost, plus debt's share times its after-tax cost.

*The numbers and where they came from.* Debt of $1,285.0 million and shares worth $2,604.9 million make the company worth $3,889.9 million, so debt is 33.03% (2024 annual report; share price of $15.18 on 2025-02-05 from the deal document). The shares' cost is 12.65% and debt's after-tax cost 5.33%. The cost of capital is 10.23%. Over the schedule, the cost is 10.43% at no debt, 10.22% at 10%, 10.05% at 20% and 10.03% at 21%, which is the lowest. It is 10.31% at 33%, 10.72% at 40% and 16.44% at 50% (model-output.md §1 and §3). Operating profit is $208.4 million and interest expense $112.4 million, so coverage is 1.85 times, a synthetic "B-" rating. Adding back the $52.9 million amortization of the buyout's intangible assets gives 2.32 times and an optimum of 26%. The standardized EBITDA of $282.8 million gives 2.52 times and 28%.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* The optimum is 21% (model-output.md §3), using operating profit as the coverage measure. That is a judgment. We rejected EBITDA because depreciation of $74.4 million, less the $52.9 million of buyout amortization, is about what the company spends on capital items ($20.5 million), so it is not free cash. We rejected the company's adjusted EBITDA of $384.7 million because it adds back $78.3 million of stock compensation and its payroll taxes ($76.5 million of stock compensation alone), a real cost. Adding back buyout amortization is the strongest alternative and would give 26%. The curve is shallow on the low side and falls off a cliff past 40%, so the course's advice, to be under-levered instead of over-levered, favors the low end.

*How sure we are.* Interest rates and cost figures are disclosed or measured. The ratings come from a table. The 10.23% differs from the 10.31% in the debt table because the first uses the observed 7.11% rate, while the schedule's 33% row reprices all the debt at its synthetic rate of 7.43% (coverage of 2.19 times, a B). Today's rating on the actual interest is B-. The table, the choice of operating profit and the beta of 1.22 are the main sources of doubt.

## Step 19: What is moving to the best debt level worth, and how far should the company really go?

*What we wanted to know.* How much value SolarWinds would gain by moving from 33% debt to the best level, and what ratio to recommend once real-world limits are applied.

*Why it matters.* The answer says whether capital structure is a big lever for this company or a small one. Taking on too much debt for a small gain risks the company in a downturn.

*What we did.* The gain is the yearly saving in funding cost, capitalized as a growing perpetuity, a payment that grows for ever at a steady rate. The formula is saving ÷ (cost of capital − growth). We also reran the discounted cash flow valuation at the lower cost of capital. Then we cut operating profit by 10%, 20% and 30% and watched the optimum move. A covenant is a loan promise that limits the borrower, so we also checked how close the company is to the one it has.

*The numbers and where they came from.* Moving to 21% debt lowers the cost of capital from 10.31% to 10.03%, a saving of $10.9 million a year. Capitalized at growth of 2.5%, that is worth $144.4 million. Debt would fall by $468.1 million (model-output.md §3). The discounted cash flow valuation gives enterprise value (the value of the operating business from our forecast, before debt and cash; it differs from the $3,889.9 million market firm value, which is debt plus the market value of the shares) of $3,444.3 million at today's cost of capital and $3,533.4 million at the optimum, and $13.00 against $13.52 a share (model-output.md §6). Stress test (model-output.md §4): optimum 21% unchanged, 19% with operating profit down 10% ($187.6 million), 16% down 20% ($166.7 million) and 14% down 30% ($145.9 million). The loan's only financial covenant, a limit of 7.40 times on net first-lien debt to earnings, applies only if more than 35% of the $130.0 million credit line is drawn, and none was drawn (2024 annual report, note 9). Operating profit in 2023 was $150.4 million, against $208.4 million in 2024.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* The gain is small: between about $0.52 and $1.28 a share, against a discounted-cash-flow value of $13.00. The $0.52 (discounted cash flow at the optimal cost of capital, $89.1 million), the $0.84 (annual savings capitalized, $144.4 million) and the $1.28 (adjusted present value, $220.3 million) are three measures on different bases: the first prices today's debt at its observed 7.11%, the second prices both ends at the schedule's synthetic rates, and the third values tax benefits less bankruptcy cost (model-output.md §6 and §9). Debt would be repaid at face, so lenders gain nothing, and the benefit goes to shareholders. We did not treat it as a buyback gain, because SolarWinds would be cutting debt, not buying back shares. We recommend about 15% debt, a judgment. That is a cushion below the 21% optimum, justified by the stress test (§4): if operating profit fell 30% the optimum would be 14%, so 15% is not a rival optimum. Operating profit in 2023 was 28% below 2024's, a fall between the 20% and 30% stress rows (16% and 14%). The covenant is not the limit, since it does not apply while the credit line is unused. Giving up some of the saving costs little because the cost-of-capital curve is flat near the bottom: 10.22% at 10% debt and 10.05% at 20%.

*How sure we are.* The saving is computed from the model and depends on the ratings table and the 2.5% growth rate. The three measures ($89.1 million, $144.4 million and $220.3 million) differ in basis and bracket the answer. The covenant's own definition of earnings is not in our files, so how far the company is from breaching it is estimated.

## Step 20: When does the debt-level calculation break down, and what does that mean for SolarWinds?

*What we wanted to know.* Whether the calculation of the best debt level misses something for SolarWinds: lost customers when the credit rating falls, owners who are not ordinary investors, or the fact the company has since been taken private.

*Why it matters.* If a lower rating makes customers leave, operating profit falls with debt and the best level is lower than the calculation says.

*What we did.* We looked for a past shock to test customer reaction, checked the four things that set the right debt level (tax rate, operating profit relative to company value, business risk, and the price of debt against the price of shares), and asked how the owners and the 2025 sale change the picture. The test shock was the December 2020 SUNBURST attack, in which hackers inserted malicious code into SolarWinds' Orion software.

*The numbers and where they came from.* The company told about 33,000 Orion customers and believed fewer than 18,000 had the compromised software, out of more than 300,000 customers (the company's 8-K filing of 2020-12-14, `sources/filings/swi-8k-2020-12-14-sunburst-followup.md`). The maintenance renewal rate went 94% (2019), 91% (2020), 93% (2022), 96% (2023) and 97% (2024) (annual reports, `sources/filings/`). Revenue was $669.1 million in 2019, $716.8 million in 2020, $718.6 million in 2021, $719.4 million in 2022, $758.7 million in 2023 and $796.9 million in 2024. Operating profit was $208.4 million in 2024, $150.4 million in 2023 and a loss of $819.6 million in 2022, which includes an $891.1 million write-down of goodwill. Tax rate 25%. Enterprise value on market prices is $3,630.6 million (the market value of the shares plus debt minus cash: what the market says the operations are worth), so operating profit is 5.74% of it. Eight comparable companies' ratios range from below zero to 6.78%, with a median of 4.29% and debt from 0.88% to 45.28% of company value, so the ratio alone does not set the debt level. Unlevered beta is 1.22. Equity costs 12.65% and debt 7.43% at a 33% debt ratio, against 11.63% and 5.38% at 21% (model-output.md §1 and §3). Silver Lake owned 36.56% and Thoma Bravo 29.79% at the 2024 proxy (the 2024 proxy statement, `sources/filings/swi-def14a-2024.md`), and each had to consent to a sale.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* The indirect cost of a lower rating (lost customers and profit) is small, a judgment. After an attack that hurt its reputation more than a downgrade would, customers stayed, revenue stalled for two years and then grew, and renewals recovered. Even a 30% fall in operating profit moves the best level from 21% to 14% (model-output.md §4). SolarWinds is not cyclical (its sales do not swing with the economy), and it is not part of a family group, a set of linked companies that would rescue one another. Its two sponsors are separate owners whose interests need not match those of public shareholders, and they are not diversified in this stock. Since the April 2025 sale the company is private, so market values would have to be replaced by estimates. We did not change the model, but all of this argues for a debt level at or below 21%.

*How sure we are.* The shock evidence is disclosed. The size of the indirect cost is a judgment with no company-specific estimate behind it. The ratios for the eight comparable companies are computed from their filings, with one negative value.

## Step 21: If we count the tax savings and the cost of failure separately, what debt level is best?

*What we wanted to know.* Step 18 found the best amount of debt by looking for the lowest cost of capital (the blended rate the company pays its lenders and shareholders). Here we ask the same question a second way. We value the company as if it had no debt, add the tax savings that debt brings, and subtract the expected cost of going bankrupt. The method is called adjusted present value (APV). The debt level where the total is highest is the best one.

*Why it matters.* If two methods agree, we can trust the answer more. If they disagree, one of them is hiding a weak assumption, and we need to know which.

*What we did.* The formula is: value of the company = value with no debt + tax savings from debt − expected bankruptcy cost. The tax savings are the tax rate times the debt, assuming the debt stays forever. The expected bankruptcy cost is the chance of default times the loss if it happens. For the chance of default we used a table that links a credit rating to the share of companies at that rating that defaulted within ten years (a table Professor Ed Altman at NYU maintains, shown in the lesson 21 slides). SolarWinds' rating at each debt level comes from step 18. The loss if it happens is a judgment call: we used 25% of the company's value, the middle of the lecture's 10% to 40% range. With SolarWinds' numbers: tax savings today are 25% × $1,285.0 million = $321.2 million. The schedule's 33% row, which reprices the debt at the schedule's synthetic rate, rates the company B, which defaulted 36.80% of the time, so the expected cost is 36.80% × 25% of the no-debt value, or $361.6 million. The no-debt value is therefore $3,930.2 million: $3,889.9 million − $321.2 million + $361.6 million. We then repeated the calculation at debt levels from 0% to 70% of company value. Once interest is larger than operating income, we stopped counting tax savings on the excess, because a company cannot deduct more interest than it earns.

*The numbers and where they came from.* Debt of $1,285.0 million and company value of $3,889.9 million (debt plus the $2,604.9 million market value of the shares): the 2024 annual report (Form 10-K) filed with the SEC on 2025-02-19, note 9, and the share price of $15.18 on 2025-02-05. File: `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md` and the ledger (`inputs.yaml`). The 25% tax rate is Damodaran's January 2025 table of marginal tax rates by country. The ratings at each debt level are from the model output (step 18). The default table is on the lesson 21 slides.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* At a 25% bankruptcy cost the company's value peaks at a debt level of 21% of company value, at $4,109.9 million, compared with $3,889.6 million at the 33% SolarWinds carries now (the APV table's own value for the current row; the market firm value used elsewhere is $3,889.9 million). That is a gain of $220.3 million. That is the same 21% as step 18. Choosing 25% is a judgment call. We tested 40%: the peak stays at 21%. We tested 10%: the peak jumps to 40%, because a cheap bankruptcy makes debt look safe. We rejected 10% because SolarWinds is mostly goodwill ($2,363.2 million against $2,348.0 million of invested capital; goodwill is larger because invested capital is book equity plus debt minus cash) and would fetch little in a sale. We rejected going higher than 25% for the reason the SUNBURST attack showed: customers stayed (in 2024, 97% of maintenance contracts were renewed and subscription net retention was 99%). That attack tested trust, not bankruptcy, so it only sets a floor.

*How sure we are.* The tax savings are arithmetic. The bankruptcy cost is an opinion, and the default table is ten-year data applied loosely. At 25% or 40% the answer is 21%; at 10% it is not. We only rated the debt levels already in step 18, so 21% is the best of those, not a fine search.

## Step 22: Is SolarWinds borrowing too much or too little, and how should it get to the right level?

*What we wanted to know.* Steps 18 and 21 say the best debt level is 21% of company value. SolarWinds carries 33.03%. We wanted to know if that gap is dangerous, and what the company should do about it.

*Why it matters.* Too much debt makes a company fragile. Too little invites a buyer to borrow against the company to buy it. The right fix depends on which problem the company has and how fast it must act.

*What we did.* We followed the lecture's decision path. First, is the company over or under its best level? Second, if it is over, could it go bankrupt soon? Third, does it have projects worth investing in, meaning projects that earn more than the company's cost of capital (10.23%)? If the company has time and no good projects, the advice is to pay down debt gradually from its own cash flow. We measured a project's quality by return on invested capital, which is operating profit after tax divided by the money invested in the business. For SolarWinds: $208.4 million of operating profit × (1 − 25% tax) = $156.3 million, divided by $2,348.0 million of invested capital, is 6.66%.

*The numbers and where they came from.* Debt ratio of 33.03% against 21%, debt $468.1 million above the best level, coverage of operating profit over interest of 1.85 times, cash of $259.3 million and an unused $130.0 million bank credit line: the model output and the 2024 annual report (Form 10-K), file `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`. The two large owners, Silver Lake with 36.56% and Thoma Bravo with 29.79%, come from the 2024 proxy statement (the document sent to shareholders before the annual meeting), file `sources/filings/swi-def14a-2024.md`. Management's cash flow forecasts of $212.0 million for 2025 and $258.0 million for 2026 come from the 2025 merger information statement, file `sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md`.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* SolarWinds is over-borrowed but not in danger. Coverage of 1.85 times is weak (a B- grade on our synthetic rating), but the company has cash, an unused credit line, and no loan due until 2030-02-05. It could not have been taken over against its owners' wishes: the owners' agreement required both Silver Lake and Thoma Bravo to consent to any change in control. In 2023 the board contacted 13 potential buyers, and none would bid the price that was later paid. Its projects do not clear the bar: 6.66% against 10.23%, though most of its invested capital is goodwill from the 2016 buyout. So the path is gradual: use cash flow, after about $84.3 million a year of after-tax interest, to repay debt. That frees about $127.7 million in 2025 and $173.7 million in 2026, which closes the $468.1 million gap, the move down to the 21% optimum, in about three years. We rejected borrowing to buy back shares, which moves the wrong way. The company in fact paid a $168.2 million special dividend in April 2024, which also moved the wrong way. This is the opinion we hold; the arithmetic is only the gap and the cash flows.

*How sure we are.* The debt figures are disclosed. The return of 6.66% mixes goodwill into the base, so it understates what the software itself earns. Management's forecasts are a plan. If operating profit fell 30%, the best level would fall to 14% and the gap would widen.

## Step 23: Is SolarWinds borrowing the right kind of debt?

*What we wanted to know.* Beyond how much debt, what kind: how long it lasts, whether its interest rate floats or is fixed, and which currency it is in.

*Why it matters.* Debt whose payments move with the business is safer. A company paid in euros that borrows in dollars can be hurt when exchange rates move, even if the business is fine.

*What we did.* We asked how the lecture's questions apply to SolarWinds. How long do the cash flows last? In what currencies do they arrive? Can the company raise prices when costs rise? A company that can raise prices suits floating-rate debt, because when inflation pushes interest up, its income rises too. Is the company growing or mature? Growing companies suit convertible debt (debt that can turn into shares). Then we compared the answer with the loan SolarWinds has.

*The numbers and where they came from.* All from the 2024 annual report (Form 10-K), filed 2025-02-19, file `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`. The loan is $1,235.7 million at an interest rate of SOFR (a benchmark short-term interest rate) plus 2.75%, floating, in US dollars, due in 5.0 years, with an effective rate of 7.11%. The company says most contracts run one year and it applies annual price increases. In 2024, 97% of maintenance contracts were renewed and subscription net retention was 99%. A rise of one percentage point in rates would add about $12.4 million of interest a year. Of revenue, 64.82% is from the United States and 35.18% is international. Of its 2,107 employees, 1,379 work outside the United States. The company reports no interest rate swaps (contracts that exchange floating payments for fixed ones). Of its $130.0 million credit line, $112.5 million can be drawn in other currencies.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* The right design is medium-term, floating-rate, straight debt, partly in euros. SolarWinds' loan matches on rate type: renewal rates and annual price increases show pricing power, a judgment call because maintenance revenue still fell 4.6% as customers moved to subscriptions. Its 5.0-year life is in range, but almost all of the principal falls due on one day in 2030. The gap is currency: 0% of the debt is in foreign currency against at most 35.18% of revenue, though the company's foreign costs offset part of that, and the 10-K does not let us size the net. We did not recommend a swap on the term loan. SolarWinds has no new projects to finance, so we would adjust at the 2030 refinancing, or use the credit line's euro tranche, which is 8.8% of total debt of $1,285.0 million. We did not compute what a swap would cost: no swap rates are in the filings.

*How sure we are.* The loan terms are disclosed. The currency exposure is only bounded, not measured. The pricing-power view is an interpretation of two disclosed rates and a policy statement.

## Step 24: How much has SolarWinds paid its shareholders over five years?

*What we wanted to know.* How much SolarWinds paid out in dividends (cash a company pays to its shareholders) in each of the last five years, and what that means relative to its profit and its share price.

*Why it matters.* The next steps decide whether SolarWinds should pay more or less. We can't judge that without the record. Companies seldom change their dividends, so a company's history is usually the best guide to what shareholders expect.

*What we did.* Two measures. The payout ratio is dividends divided by net income, the share of profit paid out. It can't be computed when net income is negative. The dividend yield is the dividend per share divided by the share price, the cash return before any change in price. For 2024: $168.2 million of dividends divided by $111.9 million of net income is 150.3%. The yield is $1.00 divided by a share price, which is the problem: we have no price for the dividend date.

*The numbers and where they came from.* 2020, no dividend: the 2020 annual report (Form 10-K), file `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2020.md`. 2021, $237.2 million, or $1.50 a share, paid 2021-08-24: the 2023 annual report, file `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2023.md`. 2022 and 2023, none. 2024, $168.2 million, or $1.00 a share, paid 2024-04-15: the 2024 annual report, file `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`. The payouts and ratios are reading and division. The one judgment is showing the 2024 yield as a range instead of picking a price.

*How sure we are.* The dividends are disclosed. The 2024 yield is bracketed, not exact. The five-year pattern, irregular and tied to events, does not look like what the lecture describes for most companies: steady, and tracking earnings.

## Step 25: Should SolarWinds pay dividends, and why did it?

*What we wanted to know.* Who owns SolarWinds, what that implies for dividends, and whether the company's reasons for paying them were good ones.

*Why it matters.* Dividends are cash that leaves the company. If the cash could earn more inside, or repay debt that costs 7.11% a year, paying it out destroys value. A dividend can also tell the market something the company did not mean to say.

*What we did.* The lecture offers three views. Dividends don't matter (the shareholder gets the same total return either way). Dividends are bad (they are taxed more heavily than gains). Dividends are good (some investors want them, or they signal confidence). Dividends can also move value from lenders to shareholders, since the cash could have repaid the loan. We looked at who the shareholders are, tried to test their tax preferences, read the loan terms, and compared each reason the company might have with the facts. The test of tax preferences compares a share price just before and after the first day a buyer no longer receives the dividend (the ex-dividend date).

*The numbers and where they came from.* Silver Lake owns 36.56% and Thoma Bravo owns 29.79%, from the 2024 proxy statement (the document sent to shareholders before the annual meeting), file `sources/filings/swi-def14a-2024.md`. The public holds the rest. The 2024 annual report (Form 10-K, filed 2025-02-19, file `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`) lists 33 holders of record. The loan limits dividends "subject to certain exceptions", and its one financial test applies only if more than 35% of the $130.0 million credit line is drawn. Debt is $468.1 million above the best level. The 2024 payout of $168.2 million plus $26.5 million of withheld shares was $194.7 million against free cash flow to equity (cash left for shareholders after debt payments and reinvestment) of $127.0 million. The return on invested capital is 6.66% against a cost of capital of 10.23%.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* We could not run the tax test. SolarWinds' stock no longer trades, and its price history is gone. The files also don't say how the two large owners are taxed. The company gave no reason for its payouts, only the usual statement that the board decides. Against the lecture's list: no group of dividend-seeking investors (there was no regular dividend), no signal intended (both payments were labelled special), loan limits did not stop the payment, and habit is ruled out (two payments in five years). The best remaining reading, which is an inference, is that the owners, who hold about two-thirds, wanted cash out: they would have received about $111.6 million of the $168.2 million, if their stakes were unchanged. Our judgment is that the company should pay no regular dividend and no special dividend until debt is near 21% of company value. Repaying debt is worth more, because the projects earn less than the cost of capital but debt costs 7.11%. A regular dividend would also say the move from selling licenses to selling subscriptions is finished, and in 2023 bidders walked away because of the early stage of that move.

*How sure we are.* Ownership and loan terms are disclosed. The owners' motive and tax position are not, and the ex-dividend test was not possible. The recommendation rests on two figures that a different return measure could move: the 6.66% return, which includes goodwill, and the 21% best level.

## Step 26: Did SolarWinds hand back more cash than it could afford?

*What we wanted to know.* How much cash SolarWinds gave to its shareholders over recent years, and how much it could have given. The first number is dividends (cash payments per share) plus buybacks (the company purchasing its own shares). The second is free cash flow to equity (FCFE): the cash left for shareholders after the company has paid its running costs, replaced its equipment, funded its day-to-day working needs and handled its borrowing.

*Why it matters.* A company that pays out more than it can afford runs down its cash or borrows. A company that pays out less piles up cash that shareholders may not trust it to use well. Either one is a warning sign about how the business is run.

*What we did.* The formula is: FCFE = net income − (capital spending − depreciation) − increase in working capital + new borrowing − debt repaid. Depreciation is the accounting charge for wear on equipment and acquired assets. Working capital is the money tied up in the short-term running of the business, such as bills owed by customers. For 2024 the formula reads: net income $111.9 million, minus (capital spending $20.5 million minus depreciation and amortization $74.4 million, which is −$53.9 million, so we add $53.9 million), minus $38.8 million of working capital, plus $0.0 million of net borrowing. That is $127.0 million. We then set it against cash returned. We built three years, 2022 to 2024. Earlier years include N-able, a business SolarWinds spun off (separated as its own company) in July 2021, so they are not comparable.

*The numbers and where they came from.* Everything comes from SolarWinds' 2024 annual report (Form 10-K), filed with the SEC on 2025-02-19: income statement, cash flow statement and balance sheet. The 2021 dividend and cash balance come from the 2023 annual report. Files: `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`, `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2023.md`. The model computes the table. FCFE was −$606.2 million in 2022, $59.4 million in 2023 and $127.0 million in 2024. Cash returned was $11.1 million, $18.8 million and $194.7 million. The 2024 figure is a one-time $1.00-a-share special dividend of $168.2 million plus $26.5 million of buybacks. Those buybacks are shares the company withheld from employees to cover their taxes when their stock vested, not purchases on the open market.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* In 2024 SolarWinds returned $67.7 million more than FCFE, which is 153% of it. It did not borrow to do so. Net new borrowing was $0.0 million. Cash fell from $289.2 million to $259.3 million. The 2022 figure of −$606.2 million looks alarming but is distorted. Net income that year, −$929.4 million, includes $906.4 million of write-downs, mostly of goodwill (the premium the owners paid for the company in 2016 over its book assets). Those are accounting charges, not cash, and the model adds them back; what is left is mostly $664.4 million of debt repaid. Over the three years FCFE was −$419.8 million and the company returned $224.6 million. We did not find any shareholder who campaigned to force the cash out. The two private equity firms that owned the company, Silver Lake (36.56%) and Thoma Bravo (29.79%), sat on or appointed the board and received about two-thirds of every dividend.

*How sure we are.* High on the returned cash, which is disclosed. Moderate on FCFE: the formula follows the course, but the 2022 figure depends on the impairment treatment above, and 2020 and 2021 are not built.

## Step 27: Should shareholders trust SolarWinds with its cash?

*What we wanted to know.* Whether SolarWinds had earned the right to keep cash rather than hand it back, and whether it ever paid dividends with borrowed money.

*Why it matters.* If a company earns less on its projects than investors could earn elsewhere, every dollar it keeps is worth less than a dollar to shareholders. The answer decides whether paying cash out is good policy or a symptom.

*What we did.* Professor Damodaran's test has two parts. First, does the company earn more on the money invested in it than that money costs? The earnings measure is return on invested capital (ROIC): operating profit after tax divided by the money shareholders and lenders have put in. The cost is the cost of capital: the average return those investors require. Second, has the stock done better than the market? Then place the company on a grid, the course's two-by-two table: good or bad projects on one side, and returning more or less cash than free cash flow to equity (the cash left for shareholders, step 26) on the other.

*The numbers and where they came from.* The model computes the base year. After-tax operating income is $156.3 million (operating income of $208.4 million less 25% tax). Invested capital is $2,348.0 million (equity, plus debt, minus cash, all from the balance sheet in the 2024 annual report, Form 10-K, `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`). ROIC is 6.66% against a cost of capital of 10.23%, so the company destroys about $84.0 million of value a year (economic value added). Return on equity is 7.99% against a cost of equity of 12.65%. Goodwill is 100.65% of invested capital. Goodwill is what the private equity owners paid in 2016 above the book value of the assets, so part of the low return measures the price they paid. The stock comes from the pay-versus-performance table in the 2024 proxy statement (the document sent to shareholders before the annual meeting, `sources/filings/swi-def14a-2024.md`): $100 invested at the end of 2019 was worth $51 at the end of 2023, while an index of peers rose to $211.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* No, shareholders should not trust SolarWinds with extra cash, and the company sits in the "bad projects" row of the grid. ROIC has risen from 2.69% in 2022 to 4.79% in 2023 to 6.66% in 2024 (the 2022 figure has the write-downs added back), but it has never reached the cost of capital. We could not compute Jensen's alpha, the stock's return beyond what its risk predicts, because the delisted stock has no free price history. The proxy's index is the stand-in and stops at 2023. On borrowing: no dividend was paid with new debt. The 2024 dividend of $168.2 million came with $0.0 million of net borrowing. The 2021 dividend of $237.2 million came from $505.6 million that N-able sent SolarWinds when it was separated. Silver Lake and Thoma Bravo received about 66% of the 2024 dividend, $111.6 million. Nothing we found says they needed the cash. The decision being put off was the sale of the company, which came in February 2025.

*How sure we are.* High that returns are below the cost of capital, because the gap is wide and the inputs are disclosed. Moderate on the stock comparison, because the index ends in 2023 and its peer group is the company's own choice.

## Step 28: Does SolarWinds pay out like its competitors?

*What we wanted to know.* How SolarWinds' dividends and buybacks compare with similar companies, and whether it is copying them or going its own way.

*Why it matters.* Many companies copy their sector's payout policy without asking whether it fits. If SolarWinds' situation differs, copying would be wrong, and a very different payout needs an explanation.

*What we did.* We took the payout ratio (dividends divided by net income) for the eight comparison companies, the same eight peers a bank used in its fairness opinion. We compared it with Professor Damodaran's January 2025 table for 351 software firms (a different Damodaran dataset from the 333-firm beta file in steps 8 to 10: this one covers dividend payouts, that one betas). Then we ran the course's market regression. A regression is a fitted formula that predicts one number from others: here, payout from beta (how much a stock moves with the market), expected growth and the debt share of the company's value. Its form is: payout = 0.649 − 0.296 × beta − 0.800 × growth + 0.300 × debt share. With SolarWinds' numbers: 0.649 − 0.296 × 1.6714 − 0.800 × 8.12% + 0.300 × 33.03% = 18.8%.

*The numbers and where they came from.* Peer payout ratios are from the peers' annual reports (`sources/peer-filings/peer-facts-2025-02-05.md`). Five pay no dividend. Progress Software pays 46.05%. NetApp and Open Text pay dividends that the SEC data did not label, so they are blank. The software sector, in Damodaran's table (lesson 28 folder, `spreadsheet-divfcfe24.md`), paid 27.22% of net income as dividends and returned 80.36% including buybacks. The regression coefficients are from the lesson 28 slides and come from US companies in January 2014. Beta 1.6714 and a debt share of 33.03% come from the model. Growth of 8.12% is management's own forecast, because analyst estimates could not be obtained. SolarWinds' own 2024 payout comes from the 2024 annual report (`sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`): dividends of $168.2 million on net income of $111.9 million, which is 150%.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* SolarWinds diverged in 2024. Its payout of 150% was about eight times the regression's 18.8%, and including buybacks it returned 174% of net income against the sector's 80.36%. In 2022 and 2023 it paid no dividend, and then it looked like its peers. So the divergence was one special dividend, driven by two owners who held 66.35% together, not a policy. Our judgment is that this was not "me-too" (copying what peers do) at all. The regression is old and weak, since the lesson reports an R-squared of only 19.6%, meaning it explains about a fifth of the differences between companies. We treat 18.8% as a rough marker, not a target.

*How sure we are.* Low to moderate. The peer payout data is thin, the coefficients are from 2014, and the sector table is an average over many firms. The direction, that 2024 was far above the norm, is clear.

## Step 29: What is SolarWinds worth on the stock market, and do we value the whole company or only the shares?

*What we wanted to know.* The market value of SolarWinds' shares, how it compares with the company's earnings and cash, and which of two valuation routes to take: value the whole company, or only the shares.

*Why it matters.* The wrong route forces us to forecast something nobody can forecast. SolarWinds' borrowing was about to change sharply, and the cost of its equity moves with it.

*What we did.* Market capitalization (market cap) is the share price times the number of shares: $15.18 × 171.6 million shares = $2,604.9 million. We used the price on 2025-02-05, the last trading day before the sale of the company leaked. To value only the shares you discount cash flow to equity (what is left after debt payments). To value the whole company you discount cash flow to the firm (before debt payments) and subtract debt at the end. Professor Damodaran's rule is to value the whole firm when the debt ratio is unstable.

*The numbers and where they came from.* The price comes from the Goldman Sachs fairness opinion in the merger information statement (`sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md`). The share count is the record-date count in the same document. Net income of $111.9 million and debt are from the 2024 annual report (Form 10-K, `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`). The model gives the price-to-earnings ratio, the share price divided by earnings per share, as 23.28x. Debt was $1,285.0 million, which is 33.03% of the company's value at market prices and 47.28% at book (accounting) values. The buyer, Turn/River Capital, committed $2,225.0 million of first lien debt and $525.0 million of second lien debt (the lenders paid first and second if the company fails) for the purchase.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* Value the whole firm, because the debt was about to more than double and an equity model would need a forecast of it. The DCF (discounted cash flow valuation) needs four things: the cash flow the company makes now, how fast it grows, how risky it is (the discount rate, 10.23%) and when it matures (growth 2.5% after 2030). The growth is the hardest, because management's 2030 cash flow is higher than its after-tax operating profit, and growing it forever would assume the company reinvests nothing. That choice alone moves the terminal value (the value of everything after 2030, before discounting) from $3,808.9 million to $5,552.5 million. Over time, the market value is hard to follow. A $100 investment at the end of 2019 was worth $51 at the end of 2023, while an index of peers rose to $211. A 1-for-2 reverse split (two old shares become one) in July 2021 and the spin-off of N-able break the series. The deal price of $18.50 is 21.9% above the unaffected price. Earnings went from a loss of $929.4 million in 2022 to a profit of $111.9 million in 2024, helped by margins, since revenue grew only about 5% a year.

*How sure we are.* High on the market cap and the multiples, which are arithmetic on disclosed numbers. The choice of route is a judgment, but a firm one. The long-run history of market cap is partial, because the ledger holds a single market cap, at the analysis date.

## Step 30: What cash does SolarWinds produce, and what rate do we discount it at?

*What we wanted to know.* The cash the business produces for all its investors in the base year, 2024, and the discount rate that matches it.

*Why it matters.* This is the starting point of the whole valuation. An error here carries into every later year and into the terminal value.

*What we did.* Free cash flow to the firm (FCFF) is cash from operations before any payment to lenders. The formula is: FCFF = operating income × (1 − tax rate) − (capital spending − depreciation) − increase in working capital. For 2024: $208.4 million × (1 − 25%) = $156.3 million; capital spending of $20.5 million less depreciation and amortization of $74.4 million is −$53.9 million, which adds $53.9 million; working capital took $38.8 million. So FCFF = 156.3 + 53.9 − 38.8 = $171.4 million. The reinvestment rate is the share of after-tax operating profit put back into the business: (−53.9 + 38.8) ÷ 156.3 = −9.66%. A negative rate means depreciation and amortization exceeded new spending.

*The numbers and where they came from.* All from the 2024 annual report (Form 10-K, filed 2025-02-19; `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`): the income statement and cash flow statement. We used the marginal tax rate of 25%, the rate on the next dollar of profit, from Professor Damodaran's January 2025 tax table, because the company booked a tax benefit of $8.1 million on $103.8 million of pre-tax income and the reported rate is unusable. Stock-based compensation of $76.5 million is treated as a real cost, so it is not added back. The model computes the cost of equity (12.65%) and cost of capital (10.23%).

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* FCFF for the base year is $171.4 million. The model's table gives $146.3 million for 2022 (adding back the write-downs) and $190.6 million for 2023. A judgment call is the reinvestment rate. Of the $74.4 million of amortization, $52.9 million is for assets acquired in the 2016 buyout, which the company will never replace. Leaving that out gives reinvestment of $37.8 million and a rate of 24.18%. We use the reported figures, which are what the ledger carries. A cash flow before debt payments pairs with the cost of capital, 10.23%, not the cost of equity. The cost of capital is the blended rate required by lenders and owners: 12.65% on equity, 5.33% on debt after tax, weighted 66.97% and 33.03%. It may change: it would be 10.03% at the best debt ratio, 21%, and it would rise sharply if the buyer's $2,750.0 million of new debt pushed the company toward half debt, where the model's schedule shows a cost of capital of 10.72% at 40% but 16.44% at 50%. The jump happens because at 50% debt coverage falls to 0.54 times, the rating drops to C, and the cost of debt leaps to 19.93%. That schedule is a rough estimate from a synthetic credit rating, not the sponsors' actual loan terms.

*How sure we are.* High on the inputs, moderate on the result. Every figure is disclosed, but the working capital swing is mostly one tax item, a $23.8 million fall in income taxes payable, and the reinvestment rate depends on the amortization judgment. The 2020 and 2021 years are not built because they include N-able.

## Step 31: How fast can SolarWinds grow, and has it earned that growth?

*What we wanted to know.* How fast SolarWinds has grown, how fast management says it will grow, and how fast its own spending and returns say it can grow.

*Why it matters.* Growth is most of the value in the forecast. If it is not backed by investment, the valuation is paying for growth the company has not paid for.

*What we did.* Professor Damodaran's rule is that growth is earned: expected growth = reinvestment rate × return on invested capital (ROIC). ROIC is after-tax operating profit divided by the money invested in the business. For SolarWinds, ROIC is 6.66% and the reinvestment rate is −9.66% (step 30), so growth = −9.66% × 6.66% = −0.64%. If the 2016 acquisition amortization is excluded from the reinvestment calculation, the rate is 24.18% and growth is 1.61%. For net income the same idea uses return on equity of 7.99% and an equity reinvestment rate of −13.5%, giving −1.1%.

*The numbers and where they came from.* Revenue, operating income and net income are from the 2024 annual report (Form 10-K, `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`) and the 2023 report (`swi-10k-fy2023.md`). Revenue grew from $719.4 million in 2022 to $796.9 million in 2024, a rate of 5.25% a year, or 3.5% a year from the $718.6 million of 2021. Operating income went from −$819.6 million (including $906.4 million of non-cash write-downs, so $86.8 million without them) to $150.4 million to $208.4 million. Management's forecast is in the merger information statement, filed 2025-03-27 (`sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md`): revenue of $832.0 million in 2025 rising to $1,273.0 million in 2030, which is 8.12% a year, with operating margin from 32.7% to 40.1%. Analyst forecasts could not be obtained.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* The fundamentals justify about zero growth. Actual growth is 5.25% and management's plan is 8.12%. The growth is not coming from capital: invested capital fell from $2,422.9 million to $2,348.0 million while revenue rose. In management's plan, free cash flow is higher than after-tax operating profit in every year, a total of $163.1 million of negative reinvestment while revenue rises $476.1 million. The 2024 annual report attributes the revenue rise to subscription revenue (recurring fees for software access), partly offset by lower one-time license sales. Another source is research spending of $108.6 million that the accounts treat as an expense, not an investment. Margins are not stable (12.1% in 2022 without the write-down, that is $86.8 million on $719.4 million of revenue, then 19.8%, then 26.2%), so the simple formula is a weak guide. A revenue-based forecast fits better, but SolarWinds' history gives no usable sales-to-capital ratio, which is revenue per dollar of invested capital. Book revenue per dollar is 0.34, but goodwill is 100.65% of invested capital, so it says nothing about what new growth costs. The model uses management's path through 2030, and after that forces reinvestment of 25.00% (growth of 2.5% divided by a stable ROIC of 10%).

*How sure we are.* High on the historical numbers. Moderate on fundamental growth, because it rests on one year's reinvestment and on the amortization judgment. Low on management's 8.12%, because no analyst numbers exist to check it, and it assumes no net reinvestment.

## Step 32: When does SolarWinds stop growing fast, and what is it worth after that?

*What we wanted to know.* A forecast cannot run forever, so we forecast six years and then assume the company grows at one steady rate for the rest of time. We needed to pick when that steady phase starts, how fast the company grows in it, and how much it must keep investing to grow.

*Why it matters.* The value of everything after the last forecast year is called the terminal value. Its present value, after discounting to today, is $2,122.7 million of the $3,444.3 million enterprise value (the value of the business before subtracting what it owes); the terminal value itself, before discounting, is $3,808.9 million. A small change in how we treat it moves the share price by several dollars.

*What we did.* First, the period. SolarWinds' customers renew their subscriptions at a rate called net retention: this year's spending from last year's customers divided by last year's. For SolarWinds, subscription net retention was 99% in 2024 and 97% in 2023, so customers stay but spend about the same. The count of customers paying over $100,000 a year rose from 979 to 1,148. Revenue grew from $758.7 million to $796.9 million, about 5.0%, close to the middle of its eight peers. That is a business that defends its position and does not take share. We kept management's six-year window, 2025 through 2030, and treated its plan as optimistic: it has revenue growing 8.12% a year.

Second, the growth rate after 2030. We chose 2.5%, a judgment call. It sits below the 4.43% interest rate on 10-year US government bonds, the ceiling the course sets, because no company can outgrow the economy forever. It is within the ranges both of the sale's advisers used.

Third, reinvestment. A company that grows must spend to grow. The formula is reinvestment rate = growth rate ÷ return on capital, where return on capital is the profit earned on each dollar invested. With the numbers: 2.5% ÷ 10% = 25%. So after 2030 SolarWinds keeps 25% of its after-tax operating profit in the business. We chose the 10% return as a judgment: about equal to the 10.23% cost of capital, so growth after 2030 adds no value beyond what it costs.

Management's own 2030 numbers do not do this. Their free cash flow, the cash left after paying for operations and investment, is $419.0 million, which is more than their profit after tax of $383.3 million. The gap comes mainly from a forecast $46 million rise in deferred revenue and cash taxes near 25% of operating income. It does not come from depreciation running ahead of capital spending: in 2030 depreciation and amortization is $25 million and capital spending $27 million. Growing that cash flow forever would mean growing with no net reinvestment. The base year shows a similar gap on its face: in 2024 it spent $20.5 million on equipment and software while its depreciation and amortization charge was $74.4 million. Most of that charge, $52.9 million, is the write-down of intangible assets bought in the 2016 takeover, so it is not a sign of a shrinking business. But it makes the base year look like a business that needs little investment.

*The numbers and where they came from.* Net retention, customer counts and revenue: SolarWinds' 2024 annual report (Form 10-K), filed with the SEC on 2025-02-19, management's discussion section. File: `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`. Management's forecast (revenue, operating margin, free cash flow, 2025 to 2030): the merger information statement, in the section "Certain Company Financial Forecasts". File: `sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md`. The 4.43% bond rate: the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis's FRED database, 2025-02-05. File: `sources/market-data/`. The terminal value comparison is from the model. The growth rate, the 10% return and the six-year window are judgments. The 25% reinvestment rate is arithmetic once those are chosen.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* Six years, 2.5% growth, 10% return on capital, 25% reinvestment in the terminal year. The terminal value is $3,808.9 million. We did not take the other common shortcut: growing management's final-year free cash flow at 2.5%, which gives $5,552.5 million. That version is $1,743.6 million higher before discounting, which is $5.67 a share after discounting and dividing by shares ($18.67 against $13.00, model-output.md §6). We rejected it because it assumes the company grows with no reinvestment. The $18.67 is therefore the value a share would have if the final-year cash flow were grown without reinvestment. The 10% return is not what separates $13.00 from $18.67. The model's sensitivity (model-output.md §6) gives value per share of $13.00 at a 10% return, $13.10 at 10.23%, $13.69 at 12%, $14.38 at 15%, $15.07 at 20% and $15.75 at 30%. Even a 30% return on new capital stays well short of $18.67. The final-year version implies terminal reinvestment of −9.33%, so no positive return reaches it; only negative reinvestment forever does. We also kept the 10.23% cost of capital for the terminal period rather than lowering it for maturity.

*How sure we are.* The retention, customer and revenue figures are disclosed. The forecast is management's, unaudited. The growth rate, return and reinvestment choices are judgments, and the terminal value is the most judgment-dependent figure in the valuation. A shorter growth period would trim the margin gains the plan assumes.

## Step 33: How do we get from the value of the business to the value of one share?

*What we wanted to know.* The discounted cash flows give the value of the whole business. A share is a claim on what is left after everyone else is paid. We needed to know what to add (cash, stakes in other companies) and what to subtract (debt, and the shares promised to employees).

*Why it matters.* Each item moves the answer. Using the diluted-share shortcut instead of subtracting the employees' claim overstates the price by $0.86 a share ($13.86 against $13.00). Leaving the claim out entirely would overstate it by $1.09 ($187.0 million ÷ 171.6 million shares).

*What we did.* We started from the enterprise value of $3,444.3 million, the discounted cash flow value of the operating business before debt and cash. We subtracted debt of $1,285.0 million, and added cash of $259.3 million. That leaves $2,418.6 million before employee claims.

Cash first. A dollar of cash is normally worth a dollar. The course says to mark it down if investors expect the company to waste it, because the company earns less than its cost of capital. SolarWinds earned about 6.7% on the money invested in it in 2024, against a cost of capital of 10.23%, so the case for a discount exists. But the company did not sit on its cash. In 2022 it repaid $664.4 million of debt, and in 2024 it paid a $168.2 million special dividend, a one-off payment to shareholders. It also has an unused $130.0 million bank credit line. We valued the cash at face value, a judgment call. Even a 31% discount, the course's example, would only remove about $0.47 a share.

Other companies. A company that owns part of another business needs that stake added, and one that owns most of a business with outside owners must subtract their share. SolarWinds has neither. Its balance sheet shows no investment in another company and no outside owners in any subsidiary. It spun off its former business N-able in July 2021 by handing N-able shares to its own shareholders, so it holds none.

Employee claims. SolarWinds pays staff partly in restricted stock units, promises of shares that arrive once the employee stays long enough. There are 12.214 million of them, plus 113,430 vested options to buy shares at $0.98. They are a second claim on the equity. We valued them at $187.0 million: each unit at the $15.18 unaffected share price, because the employee pays nothing for it, and the options at their built-in gain. We subtracted that from the $2,418.6 million of equity and divided by the 171.6 million shares outstanding today. The result is $13.00.

The shortcut is to divide $2,418.6 million by 174.5 million "diluted" shares, an average over the year that adds only 2.9 million shares. That gives $13.86. It undercounts the claim.

The $76.5 million of stock compensation the company paid in 2024 is already treated as a cost in operating profit, and management's forecast is after that cost. So future grants are paid for in the cash flows, and the $187.0 million covers only what was already granted. Nothing is counted twice.

*The numbers and where they came from.* Cash of $251.9 million plus short-term investments of $7.5 million, the balance sheet in the 2024 annual report (Form 10-K), filed 2025-02-19. Debt: note 9 (face value of the term loan) and note 7 (leases). Units and options: note 10. Shares outstanding, 171.6 million: the merger information statement, record date 2025-02-07. Share price of $15.18 on 2025-02-05: the Goldman Sachs fairness opinion in the same document. Files: `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`, `sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md`. The bridge is from the model. The treatment of cash and the share price used for the units are judgments; the rest is arithmetic.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* Cash at face value. No cross holdings or outside owners, so nothing to add or subtract. The employee claim subtracted, shares counted as basic. We did not use the diluted-share shortcut, and we did not value the units at our own $13.00 estimate, which would shrink the claim slightly and avoid going in circles.

*How sure we are.* Cash, debt and share counts are disclosed. The $187.0 million rests on the share price chosen for the units; at a lower price the claim falls and value per share rises a little.

## Step 34: What is SolarWinds worth as it is run today, and what would a better owner add?

*What we wanted to know.* The value per share on the story management tells, and then how much more the company would be worth if someone changed how it is run. The gap between the two is called the value of control.

*Why it matters.* If a buyer pays more than the status quo value, the question is whether the extra is real value a new owner can create or just a price paid for hope.

*What we did.* The status quo story is management's own plan. SolarWinds is a mature IT monitoring business, nearly finished moving customers from one-time licences to subscriptions. Revenue grows from $832.0 million in 2025 to $1,273.0 million in 2030. Operating profit as a share of revenue rises from 32.69% to 40.14%, against 26.2% in 2024. Free cash flow, after paying staff partly in stock, goes from $212.0 million to $419.0 million. We discounted those cash flows at the cost of capital of 10.23%. The cost of capital is the return investors demand for funding the company; it blends the cost of equity, 12.65%, and the after-tax cost of debt, 5.33%, weighted by the 33.03% of capital that is debt. Present value is what a future cash flow is worth today after discounting.

The cash flows for 2025 to 2030 are worth $1,321.6 million today. Everything after 2030, the terminal value, is worth $2,122.7 million today. Together that is an enterprise value of $3,444.3 million. Subtracting debt of $1,285.0 million, adding cash of $259.3 million and subtracting the $187.0 million employee claim, and dividing by 171.6 million shares, gives $13.00 a share. That is 0.86 times the $15.18 unaffected price and 0.70 times the $18.50 deal price.

Now the better owner. The course gives four levers: run existing operations better, change how much is reinvested and what it earns, change the mix of debt and equity, and push out the date the company stops growing. We could price only one. The cost of capital approach says SolarWinds' best debt ratio is 21%, against 33.03% today. At the 10.03% cost of capital that goes with it, enterprise value is $3,533.4 million and the share is worth $13.52. The value of control from this lever is $0.52 a share on the discounted cash flow measure; capitalizing the annual savings gives $0.84 and adjusted present value $1.28, three measures on different bases (model-output.md §6 and §9). Reaching 21% means repaying $468.1 million of debt, more than the company's cash.

The other three levers are judgments we did not price. Management's plan already has margins rising to 40.14%, so a new owner has little extra to claim. The company earns about 6.7% on money invested, below its cost of capital, so cutting low-return spending would help, but we cannot value that without a different forecast. And the evidence on customer retention does not support a longer growth period.

Who could make these changes? Silver Lake owned 36.56% and Thoma Bravo 29.79% at the 2024 shareholder proxy, and each had a right to consent to any sale of the company. Three of the nine directors came from Silver Lake. With control this concentrated, the course says the market price reflects the status quo, because outside shareholders cannot force a change. In 2024 the sponsors chose a $168.2 million special dividend while debt was already above its best level. Turn/River's financing runs the same way: it committed to a $2,225 million first-lien loan and a $525 million second-lien loan, which are loans secured by claims ranked first and second on the company's assets, plus a $200 million credit line. Buyers like this borrow to raise the return on their own equity, not to lower the company's cost of capital.

*The numbers and where they came from.* Management's forecast: the merger information statement, "Certain Company Financial Forecasts". File: `sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md`. Ownership: the 2024 proxy statement, beneficial ownership table. File: `sources/filings/swi-def14a-2024.md`. Financing commitments: the merger information statement. The 21% optimum, 10.23% and $13.52 come from the model. The Goldman Sachs and Jefferies value ranges ($14.30 to $23.70 and $14.70 to $19.70) are from the same document.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* Status quo value $13.00. Value with the financing change $13.52. We did not credit extra value from operations, reinvestment or growth period. That is a judgment, not a calculation.

*How sure we are.* The plan is management's, unaudited, and optimistic against a 5.0% growth history. The financing lever is a model output; the others are opinion. A different terminal reinvestment rate would move $13.00 far more than any lever we priced.

## Step 35: How does the market price similar companies, and where does SolarWinds fall?

*What we wanted to know.* A second way to value a company is to see what investors pay for similar ones. We wanted SolarWinds' price relative to eight comparable companies, and which yardstick to trust.

*Why it matters.* This is the market's opinion, set against our own cash-flow estimate. If the two disagree, we need to know why before trusting either.

*What we did.* A multiple divides a price by something the company earns. If a company's value is $1,000 and it earns $100, it trades at 10 times earnings. To compare fairly, the top and bottom must match. If the top is the price of the whole business (enterprise value, which is the share value plus debt minus cash), the bottom must be a profit measured before interest, such as revenue or earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA). If the top is the share value alone, the bottom must belong to shareholders, such as net income or book equity. We defined every figure the same way for SolarWinds and for each peer, and took the median, the middle value, so one extreme company does not drag the result.

SolarWinds' enterprise value is $3,630.6 million: equity of $2,604.9 million plus debt of $1,285.0 million minus cash of $259.3 million. Against the middle of the eight peers:

- Enterprise value to revenue: 4.56 times, against 3.32. A premium.
- Enterprise value to EBITDA: 12.84 times, against 16.39 (six peers). A discount.
- Price to earnings: 23.28 times, against 26.57 (seven peers). A discount.
- Price to book value, meaning the share value divided by the accounting value of the owners' stake: 1.86 times, against 4.58. A discount.

The pattern makes sense. SolarWinds keeps more profit from each dollar of revenue than most peers, so it looks expensive on revenue and cheap on profit. For a business like this the measure that compares best is enterprise value to EBITDA. Revenue multiples ignore that peer operating margins run from −18% to 31%. Price to book is not usable because SolarWinds' book equity of $1,400.7 million sits on $2,363.2 million of goodwill, an accounting entry from the 2016 takeover. Price to earnings is distorted by debt and one-off charges. EBITDA is measured before interest, so debt does not distort it.

Applied to SolarWinds, the peer median of 16.39 times EBITDA implies $19.95 a share. The peer median revenue multiple of 3.32 implies $8.34. Both subtract the $187.0 million employee claim, as the discounted cash flow does (model-output.md §7). We trust the first for the reasons above, but the two differ by a wide margin, and that tells you how much the choice of peers and yardstick matters.

Why is SolarWinds cheaper than peers on EBITDA? Its growth, about 5.0%, is close to the peer middle. Its debt is 33.03% of capital against a peer middle of 11.72%. Because enterprise value and EBITDA both sit before debt, the multiple should not depend much on how a company is financed, so debt does not explain the gap by itself. Heavy debt can raise the risk a buyer sees, but that is a risk effect we cannot measure from eight companies. Cheap companies mostly deserve to be cheap, as the course says. After allowing for margin, a rough check across the eight peers leaves SolarWinds slightly cheaper than expected, but with eight companies and one outlier that is within the noise.

*The numbers and where they came from.* SolarWinds' figures: the 2024 annual report (Form 10-K), filed 2025-02-19, and the share count in the merger information statement. File: `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`. The peers are Akamai, NetApp, NetScout, OneSpan, Open Text, Progress, Qualys and Teradata, from their own SEC filings, with share prices at the close on 2025-02-05. File: `sources/peer-filings/peer-facts-2025-02-05.md`. The peer figures are not all from the same fiscal period: NetApp's year ended in April 2024, NetScout's in March 2024 and Open Text's in June 2024, while the others are calendar 2024 or close to it. Price to book was set aside partly because NetApp and Teradata trade at more than twenty times book value on equity shrunk by share buybacks. They are the US-listed companies from the list one of SolarWinds' advisers, Jefferies, used. Three non-US companies on that list were left out because they report on different accounting rules, and Informatica because it has since been acquired. Datadog, Dynatrace and PagerDuty were rejected because they grow far faster. The multiples and implied prices come from the model.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* Enterprise value to EBITDA as the main yardstick, giving $19.95. We did not use revenue or book value to set the price.

*How sure we are.* The data are disclosed. The weak point is the sample: none of the peers is a direct competitor, only six have usable EBITDA, and one high-priced peer, Qualys, lifts the median. The price is therefore a range, not a point.

## Step 36: Was $18.50 a good price, and what is the story of SolarWinds?

*What we wanted to know.* To tie every step together: did SolarWinds earn enough on what it invested, was its borrowing sensible, did its payouts follow from those answers, and how does our value compare to the $15.18 market price and the $18.50 Turn/River offered?

*Why it matters.* This is the answer the project exists to give: whether a shareholder should have taken the deal.

*What we did.* The course rests on three principles. Invest only where returns beat the cost of capital. Borrow in the mix that lowers that cost. Pay out cash you cannot invest well.

Investment. SolarWinds earned about 6.7% on the money invested in it in 2024, against a cost of capital of 10.23%. Part of that is accounting: $2,363.2 million of its capital is goodwill from the 2016 buyout. Even so, our own assumption for the mature company, 10%, only matches the cost of capital. It does not beat it.

Financing. The company owes $1,285.0 million, 33.03% of its capital. The best mix by our calculation is 21% debt. Moving there is worth between $0.52 and $1.28 a share depending on the measure (three measures on different bases, model-output.md §6 and §9).

Payout. With returns below the cost of capital, the course says to hand cash back, and the owners did: the $168.2 million special dividend in 2024. But the same cash could have repaid debt that was above its best level. In 2024 the company returned $194.7 million against $127.0 million of cash that belonged to shareholders after debt payments. Payouts followed the first answer and ignored the second.

The company in a paragraph. SolarWinds sells IT monitoring software that customers rarely leave: $796.9 million of revenue, an operating margin of 26.2%, and growth near 5%. Silver Lake and Thoma Bravo bought it in 2016, listed it in 2018, and still held 36.56% and 29.79% in 2024. They spun off the N-able business, repaid debt, paid special dividends, and in February 2025 agreed to sell to Turn/River Capital for $18.50 a share in cash. The sale needed no shareholder vote, because the two held enough shares to approve it by written consent. Management's plan more than doubles operating profit by 2030 and treats its cash flow as growing forever without reinvestment. Our valuation does not accept that, and arrives at $13.00 a share.

Now the deal. Our discounted cash flow value is $13.00: 0.86 times the $15.18 unaffected price, the last price before the deal news, and 0.70 times the $18.50 offer. The terminal value is the reason for the gap. Using management's cash flow forever gives a terminal value (before discounting) of $5,552.5 million; reinvesting what growth requires gives $3,808.9 million. That $1,743.6 million difference is $5.67 a share after discounting and dividing by shares ($18.67 against $13.00, model-output.md §6), about as much as the whole gap between $13.00 and $18.50. In other words, $18.50 is about what the plan is worth if SolarWinds can grow with no reinvestment: $18.67 a share if the final-year cash flow is grown without reinvestment.

The market comparison is more generous. The peer middle for enterprise value to EBITDA implies $19.95 a share, the revenue middle $8.34, a span of $8.34 to $19.95. Goldman Sachs and Jefferies, SolarWinds' two advisers, valued the company at $14.30 to $23.70 and $14.70 to $19.70, and $18.50 sits inside both.

*The numbers and where they came from.* Returns and payouts: the 2024 annual report (Form 10-K) and the model's cost of capital (10.23%), optimal debt (21%) and cash flow tables (free cash flow of $419.0 million in 2030). Ownership: the 2024 proxy statement. The offer, the advisers' ranges and the written consent: the merger information statement, filed 2025. Files: `sources/filings/swi-10k-fy2024.md`, `sources/filings/swi-def14a-2024.md`, `sources/deal-docs/swi-defm14c-2025-merger.md`. Values: the model. The 6.7% return and the gap between the two terminal values are our arithmetic from those figures.

*What we decided, and what we didn't.* For a shareholder holding at $15.18, $18.50 was a good price: 22% above the unaffected price, which was also the highest close in the prior year, above our $13.00, and above the highest earlier indication, $13.00 a share, from the 2023 sale process (one bidder first said about $14.00 and revised it down to $13.00). It sits inside both advisers' ranges. The case for holding out would be a belief in management's 2030 cash flows, and we do not share it. We did not conclude the price was the best available: the owners who approved it were also the sellers, and both advisers had business with them.

*How sure we are.* The facts are disclosed; the verdict rests on two judgments. First, that growth after 2030 needs reinvestment, which moves the value more than anything else. Second, the 10% return in the terminal period, though the choice matters less than it seems: even a 30% return gives only $15.75 a share (model-output.md §6), and only negative reinvestment forever reaches $18.67. If the first is wrong in the plan's favor, the value rises toward the deal price. We found no rival offer after signing, but that does not prove none was possible.

## Glossary

- **10-year Treasury.** A US government bond that pays back in ten years, used as the benchmark risk-free rate.
- **Adjusted EBITDA.** The company's own version of EBITDA, adding back stock pay and one-off items.
- **Adjusted present value (APV).** A way to value a company as its value with no debt, plus the tax savings from debt, minus the expected cost of bankruptcy.
- **Amortization.** The spreading of an acquired asset's cost over its life.
- **Annual recurring revenue (ARR).** The yearly value of contracts that renew.
- **Appraisal rights.** The right of shareholders who disagree with a sale price to ask a court to set a fair price.
- **Arithmetic average.** The simple average of yearly returns.
- **Basic shares.** The shares actually outstanding today.
- **Beta.** A measure of how much a stock moves when the overall market moves.
- **Book value.** The accounting figure for an item on the balance sheet, here the loan after fees and discounts are subtracted.
- **Bottom-up beta.** A beta built from the average of similar companies, then adjusted for the company's debt.
- **Buyback.** The company purchasing its own shares, which returns cash to the shareholders who sell.
- **Capital spending.** Money spent on equipment, software and other long-lived assets.
- **Cash-corrected beta.** A beta adjusted so cash held by the companies does not dilute the risk of the business.
- **Class action.** A lawsuit brought on behalf of a large group of shareholders.
- **Clientele.** A group of investors drawn to a company because of its dividend policy.
- **Convertible debt.** A loan that can be exchanged for shares.
- **Corporate governance.** The system of who decides what in a company, and whom they answer to.
- **Corporate life cycle.** The path from start-up to growth to maturity to decline.
- **Correlation.** A number from 0 to 1 showing how closely a stock moves with the overall market.
- **Cost of capital.** The average rate a company pays to fund itself with debt and shares, used to discount future cash flows.
- **Cost of equity.** The return shareholders demand for owning the stock.
- **Country risk premium.** The extra equity premium for operating in a riskier country.
- **Covenant.** A promise in a loan contract that limits what the borrower can do.
- **Credit rating agency.** A company, such as Moody's, that grades the chance a borrower will default.
- **Cross holdings.** Shares a company owns in other companies.
- **Customer shipping address.** The location the annual report uses to assign revenue to a country.
- **Cyclical company.** A company whose profits swing with the economy.
- **Debt ratio.** Debt as a share of the company's total value.
- **Default probability.** The share of companies at a given credit rating that failed to pay their debts within ten years.
- **Default spread.** The extra yield investors demand because a borrower might not repay.
- **Deferred revenue.** Cash received from customers for service not yet delivered.
- **Depreciation.** The yearly accounting charge for the wearing out of equipment bought earlier.
- **Diluted shares.** The share count including shares that employee awards could add, here an average over the year.
- **Discount rate.** The yearly return used to convert future cash into today's value.
- **Discounted cash flow (DCF).** A valuation that adds up future cash flows, each reduced for the time and risk before it arrives.
- **Diversified investor.** An investor who owns many stocks, so firm-specific risks average out.
- **Dividend.** Cash a company pays to its shareholders from its profits or cash.
- **Dividend yield.** The dividend per share divided by the share price.
- **EBIT.** Earnings before interest and taxes, the company's operating profit.
- **EBITDA.** Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of cash profit.
- **Economic value added (EVA).** The gap between ROIC and the cost of capital, times the capital.
- **EMEA.** Europe, the Middle East and Africa, a regional grouping.
- **Enterprise value.** The price of the whole business, equity plus debt minus cash.
- **Equity reinvestment rate.** Net capital spending plus working capital, less net new borrowing, as a share of net income.
- **Equity risk premium.** The extra yearly return investors expect from stocks as a whole above the risk-free rate.
- **Ex-dividend date.** The first day a share trades without the right to the next dividend.
- **Expected bankruptcy cost.** The chance of default times the loss in value if default happens.
- **Expected growth.** Reinvestment rate times return on invested capital.
- **Face value.** The amount a lender is actually owed on a loan.
- **Family group.** A set of linked companies whose members would rescue one another in trouble.
- **Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (FRED).** The public database that published the daily Treasury rate.
- **Firm value.** Debt plus equity at market value.
- **First lien.** A loan with the first claim on the company's assets if it fails, repaid before the second lien.
- **Fixed costs.** Costs that do not fall when sales fall, such as salaries.
- **Float.** The shares held by the public rather than by large insiders or controlling owners.
- **Floating-rate debt.** Debt whose interest rate resets as a benchmark rate moves.
- **Form 10-K.** The yearly report a public US company files with the Securities and Exchange Commission.
- **Forward contract.** An agreement to exchange currency at a fixed rate on a future date.
- **Free cash flow.** The cash left after paying for operations and investment.
- **Free cash flow to equity (FCFE).** The cash left for shareholders after operating costs, equipment replacement, working capital and net borrowing.
- **Free cash flow to the firm (FCFF).** After-tax operating income plus depreciation, minus capital spending and the rise in working capital.
- **Functional currency.** The main currency a subsidiary operates in.
- **Geometric average.** The compounded yearly average return.
- **Golden parachute.** Payments to executives triggered by a change of control.
- **Goodwill.** The accounting value of the premium paid for a company over the fair value of its assets.
- **Goodwill impairment.** An accounting write-down of the premium paid for an acquired company, which uses no cash.
- **Hedge.** A contract or position that offsets a risk.
- **Historical premium.** The average past return of stocks above bonds.
- **Holder of record.** A shareholder registered directly with the company rather than through a broker.
- **Hostile takeover.** A purchase of a company that its owners or managers oppose.
- **Hurdle rate.** The minimum return an investment must earn to be worth making, here the cost of capital.
- **Impairment.** An accounting write-down when an asset is judged to be worth less than its recorded value.
- **Implied premium.** The premium that makes the market's expected cash flows worth today's index level.
- **Incremental cash flow.** The cash flow that changes if a project goes ahead.
- **Indirect cost of distress.** Profit lost because customers, suppliers or employees lose confidence in a weak company.
- **Information statement.** The document a company sends shareholders about a deal approved by written consent.
- **Interest coverage.** Operating income divided by interest expense.
- **Interest rate swap.** A contract that exchanges floating-rate interest payments for fixed ones.
- **Invested capital.** Book equity plus debt, less cash: the money tied up in the business.
- **IPO.** Initial public offering, the first sale of shares to the public.
- **Jensen's alpha.** The part of a stock's past return not explained by its beta, a measure of past performance.
- **Lease.** A contract to rent property for years.
- **Levered beta.** A beta including the effect of the company's debt.
- **Marginal investor.** The buyer or seller whose trades set the share price at the margin.
- **Marginal tax rate.** The tax rate on the next dollar of income.
- **Market capitalization.** Share price times number of shares.
- **Market value.** What investors would pay for something today.
- **Me-too policy.** Copying the dividend or financing policy of competitors without testing whether it fits the company.
- **Median.** The middle value in a list.
- **Multiple.** A price divided by a measure of earnings, such as enterprise value divided by expected EBITDA.
- **Net retention.** The share of last year's subscription revenue from existing customers still there this year, after cancellations and including upgrades and extra purchases.
- **Observability.** Software that monitors the health of computer systems.
- **Operating income.** Profit before interest and taxes.
- **Operating margin.** Operating income divided by revenue.
- **Optimal debt ratio.** The debt ratio at which cost of capital is lowest.
- **Optimal value.** The value if the company were run better, here with its best debt ratio.
- **Options.** The right to buy a share at a fixed price.
- **Over-levered.** Carrying more debt than the best level for the company.
- **Pay versus performance.** A table in the proxy statement that compares executives' pay with the company's results and its shareholders' return over several years.
- **Payout ratio.** Dividends divided by net income.
- **Percentile.** A rank among many results. The 25th percentile is the value that a quarter of the results fall at or below.
- **Perpetuity.** A payment that continues for ever.
- **Preferred stock.** A kind of share that pays a fixed dividend and ranks ahead of common stock.
- **Premium.** The amount a buyer pays above the market price.
- **Present value.** What a future amount of money is worth today after discounting for time and risk.
- **Price to book value.** The share value divided by the accounting value of the owners' stake.
- **Price-to-earnings ratio.** The share price divided by earnings per share.
- **Pricing power.** The ability to raise prices when costs rise without losing customers.
- **Private equity.** Firms that buy companies with pooled money from outside investors and hold them for a few years; also called a private equity firm.
- **Proxy statement.** The document sent to shareholders before the annual meeting, listing directors, pay and ownership.
- **R-squared.** The share of a stock's price movement explained by the market.
- **Recapitalization.** Changing the mix of debt and equity without changing the business, for example by borrowing to buy back shares.
- **Recurring revenue.** Revenue that renews on its own, here subscriptions plus maintenance contracts.
- **Refinancing.** Replacing a loan with a new one when it comes due.
- **Regression.** A fitted formula that predicts one number from others, based on many companies.
- **Regression beta.** A beta estimated by comparing a stock's past returns with the market's.
- **Reinvestment rate.** The share of after-tax operating profit put back into the business through net capital spending and working capital.
- **Relevered beta.** An unlevered beta adjusted upward for the risk that a particular company's debt adds.
- **Renewal rate.** The share of existing customer contracts that are renewed.
- **Reportable segment.** A part of a company that it reports results for separately.
- **Restricted stock unit.** A promise to give an employee a share after they stay for a set time.
- **Return on capital.** The profit earned on each dollar invested in the business.
- **Return on equity (ROE).** Net income divided by the book value of shareholders' equity.
- **Return on invested capital (ROIC).** After-tax operating income divided by the capital invested in the business.
- **Reverse split.** A change that combines shares. In a 1-for-2 reverse split, two old shares become one new share.
- **Revolver.** A credit line the company can draw on and repay.
- **Risk-free rate.** The return on an investment with no chance of default.
- **Sales-to-capital ratio.** Revenue per dollar of invested capital, used to estimate how much investment new growth needs.
- **Scaled default spread.** A country's default spread multiplied by how much riskier stocks are than bonds.
- **Second lien.** A loan whose claim ranks behind the first lien.
- **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC).** The US regulator that collects companies' annual reports.
- **Segment.** A part of a company that reports its results separately.
- **Share class.** A category of shares with its own voting rights.
- **Signaling.** A company's action that tells the market something about its future, such as raising a dividend.
- **Small-firm group.** The set of companies with a market value under $5 billion that Damodaran's small-firm ratings table covers.
- **SOFR.** Secured overnight financing rate, a benchmark short-term interest rate.
- **Special dividend.** A one-off cash payment to shareholders outside the regular dividend.
- **Spin-off.** Separating part of a company into a new, separately traded company.
- **Sponsor.** A private equity firm that owns a large share of a company.
- **Standard error.** The typical size of the error in an estimate, a measure of how much it could move by chance.
- **Status quo value.** The value of the company run as it is now.
- **Stock-based compensation.** Pay in shares or share units instead of cash.
- **Stockholders' agreement.** A contract between a company and its large owners setting their rights.
- **Stress test.** Rerunning a calculation with worse inputs to see how the answer changes.
- **Subscription ARR.** ARR from subscription contracts only.
- **Subscription revenue.** Revenue from recurring fees for software access, as against one-time license sales.
- **SUNBURST.** The 2020 attack in which malicious code was inserted into updates of SolarWinds' Orion software.
- **Sunk cost.** Money already spent that cannot be recovered and so should not affect a decision.
- **Synthetic rating.** A credit rating estimated from the company's own numbers when no agency has graded it.
- **Tax shield.** The tax saved because interest is deductible.
- **Terminal value.** The value, at the end of the forecast period, of all cash flows after it, before discounting. Its present value is that figure discounted to today.
- **Total ARR.** Subscription ARR plus yearly maintenance on older perpetual licenses.
- **Total beta.** Market beta divided by the stock's correlation with the market, used when the owner holds all their money in one company and so bears all of its risk.
- **Unaffected price.** The share price before news of the deal reached the market.
- **Unlevered beta.** A beta with the effect of debt removed.
- **Unlevered value.** What a company would be worth if it had no debt.
- **Value of control.** The optimal value minus the status quo value.
- **Weighted average cost of capital (WACC).** The blended cost of all funding, each source weighted by its share of the company's total value.
- **Weighted equity risk premium.** The premium averaged across regions by revenue share.
- **Working capital.** The cash tied up in everyday operations such as bills owed and bills collected.
- **Written consent.** Shareholders approving an action by signing, without a meeting.
