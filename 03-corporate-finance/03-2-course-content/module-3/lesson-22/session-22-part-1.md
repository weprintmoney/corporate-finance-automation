# Session 22 Part 1 — Moving to the Optimal Debt Ratio

So at this point, you should have an optimal debt ratio for your company, right, using the cost of capital approach or the APV approach, or even looking at sector averages. Let's close the deal. Let's assume that your optimal is different from your actual. In this session, we're going to talk about what to do next.

## The Two Follow-Up Questions

All of the different approaches we've talked about have a singular objective. They will give you a right mix of debt and equity, an optimum mix of debt and equity for your company. So let's say you've arrived at an optimum mix of debt and equity. That optimal debt ratio you end up with can be higher or lower than your actual debt ratio, and you face a follow-up question, "What do I do next?"

Let's look at the questions you have to address. If your actual is equal to your optimal, you're done. There's nothing much to do. Don't mess up a good thing. If your actual is less than your optimal, then you're under-levered. If your actual is greater than your optimal, then you're over-levered. And here are the two basic questions you have to answer.

The first is, "How quickly do I need to move? Is there some urgency involved?" You might be able to adjust your optimal over three years or five years if you have the luxury of time, or you might have to do it in the next two weeks. "How quickly do I need to move to the optimal?"

The second question you need to answer is, "How am I going to move to the optimal?" Let me explain the two broad ways you can adjust your debt ratio. One is through what's called a recapitalization. What is that? You borrow money and reduce equity, buy back stock, or you raise equity and retire debt. Those can change a debt ratio by changing just the capital mix. The other way you can change your debt ratio is by taking new investments disproportionately funded with either debt or equity depending on which one you're trying to increase. So how quickly do I need to move, and what's the best way of moving to the optimal?

## Framework for Under-Levered Companies

To answer these two questions, I'm going to set up a framework that I find useful for deciding how to get from the actual to the optimal. So let's assume you've completed the actual debt ratio for your company and your optimal, and you've compared the two numbers. One of two things can be true. Actually one of three, but the third where the actual is equal to the optimal, as I said, we can let up on. There's nothing more to do. But let's look at the two scenarios which require action.

The first is if your actual is less than the optimal, you're underlevered. The question you got to ask is, "Does this make me the target of a potential acquisition?" Here is why. Having too little debt might make you the target of a hostile acquisition. You made an acquirer's job easier by having too little debt. The acquirer can, in a sense, borrow money using your assets and buy you out.

If you're a small company with very little insider holdings, and perhaps a bad history in terms of stock returns. We talked about Jensen's Alphas earlier in this class. A Jensen's Alpha measures how well or badly your stock has done. If you're a company that's a small company with a very negative Jensen's Alpha, you under-performed the market, I think you're potentially the target of acquisition, and you need to move quickly.

If you need to move quickly, you don't have the luxury of time. You need to increase your debt ratio essentially overnight. And to do that, you have to borrow money and buy back stock, or borrow money and pay a special dividend. Or, if you're a really small company, you might be able to do what's called a debt for equity swap, where you go to equity investors in your company and you offer them an equivalent market value debt.

So that's if you have to move quickly. But let's assume that you're a larger company or you have a pretty good stock market history, or you're insider-controlled. In other words, you have the luxury of time, then I'm going to have a follow-up question I'm going to ask you. Do you have good projects? Before you automatically say yes, look at the numbers. You have a history. If you have a history of delivering more than your cost of capital, positive net present value projects, I'm going to argue that you have a gift. You could use that excess debt capacity to take those new projects. Because if you do that, you get a double whammy. The first is by borrowing more money, you push yourself towards your optimal debt ratio, that lowers your cost of capital, that's the first whammy. Second, you use that money to take on projects. These are good projects. They bring positive net present value. And remember what we said about positive net present value projects. They increase your value as a company. So if you have the luxury of time, and you have good projects, the best thing you can do is borrow money and take those good projects.

What if you have the luxury of time, but you don't have good projects? Then you have to increase your debt ratio gradually over time, and you have to do it the same way you did it when you moved your debt ratio quickly, by borrowing money and buying back stock, or borrowing money and paying more dividends. Over time, your debt ratio will increase, but you will do it gradually over time rather than right away.

## Framework for Over-Levered Companies

So that's if you're under-levered. If you're over-levered, you have too much debt, you have a different problem. The question I'm going to ask you is, "Are you under bankruptcy threat?" Because that's what you worry about when you have too much debt. If the answer is yes, you have a triple C rating, you have trouble making your interest payments, then you need to bring your debt ratio down quickly.

And this is one of the most difficult tasks in business. How does an overlevered company bring its debt ratio down quickly? You're saying, "Why can't I sell my assets?" Because everybody knows you're in trouble, and your bargaining position is shot, you're not going to get fair value for the assets. You're saying, "Why can't I issue equity?" Well, who's going to buy your shares? You're a basket case. You're desperate, and you have to use your desperation as your biggest weapon. In other words, you go to your lenders, and you negotiate from the point that if they don't agree to your terms, you're going to go bankrupt. And remember what happens if you go bankrupt. Effectively, you're using your bargaining position to get the best terms you can to reduce your debt. Perhaps some of your lenders will accept equity in return for debt. Perhaps they will give you easier terms, but you're trying to negotiate the terms of your surrender.

So if you have too much debt, and you don't have the luxury of time, I know that the pathways you're looking at don't look attractive. But you have to look at the least unattractive pathway to get where you want to go, which is to bring your debt ratio down as quickly as you can.

But let's say you have too much debt but you're not under the threat of takeover. You can make your interest payments. You can make it through the next few years. You now have the luxury of time. I'm going to ask you the same question I asked you when you were under-levered. Do you have good projects? If your answer is yes, here's my suggestion. Over the next few years, first start paying dividends, take all your retained earnings and put them in these good projects. Your equity over time will grow, and as it grows, your debt problem will go away. It's the pleasantest way to get rid of a problem of having too much debt. If you don't have good projects, your life just got a little more difficult. Now, you have to use those retained earnings to pay down debt, and over time you will shrink as a company, but that's okay. You will effectively have a lower debt ratio when all is said and done.

## Applying the Framework to Disney

So let's try applying this framework to Disney as a company. Well, based on my analysis, I concluded that Disney was under-levered. Its actual debt ratio is 11.6%. Its optimal debt ratio, using the approaches we talked about, is about 40%. Is it under threat of takeover? I don't think so, for a couple of reasons. One is its size. It's a $130 billion company, which is a large company. It's not impossible to take over but difficult to do so. Second, its Jensen's Alpha that we computed. Its measure of stock price performance was +9% a year for the five years leading into 2013. That means that investors are probably willing to cut it some slack. So I'm going to argue that it's not under threat of a takeover and it has the luxury of time.

Does it have good projects? Well, it does earn a return in capital, 12.61%, well above its cost of capital of 7.81%. I think it has good projects. So November of 2013, if you'd asked me, "What should Disney do?" My advice for them might have been, "Go out and borrow money. You have debt capacity. And over time, build more theme parks, make more movies, expand ESPN. And as you go through time, your debt ratio will increase, but it will also get the supplemental benefit of having taken great projects with positive net present values."

## Applying the Framework to Your Company

So the two basic questions you need to address after you come up with the optimal, first, "How quickly do I need to move?" Now, that could be immediately, it could be gradually, but perhaps in some companies, you might decide not to move at all. And assuming you decide to move, what's the right way to get to the optimal? Should I be recapitalizing or taking new projects? And you can see already that the answer will be different for different companies.

So now let's talk about applying this to your company. When you look at your company, and you have your optimal debt ratio and your actual debt ratio follow-up, first question you're going to have to ask is, "How much time do I have? How urgent is my problem?" If you're under-levered, the question you're looking at is, are you potentially the target of a takeover? Could somebody take you over? If the answer is yes, don't dawdle. Do something quickly. If you're overlevered, the question you're answering is, "Am I under threat of bankruptcy?" Look at your rating. If it's triple-C rated, if you're triple-C rated, you don't have the luxury of time. Do something quickly. Assuming you have time as your ally, then ask the follow-up question, "Do I have good projects?" And be honest with yourself. If you have good projects, the pathway to increasing your debt ratio or reducing your debt ratio is to fund those projects disproportionately with debt if you have to lower debt ratio, or disproportionate to equity if you have too high a debt ratio. Over time, you will get rid of your debt ratio problem.
