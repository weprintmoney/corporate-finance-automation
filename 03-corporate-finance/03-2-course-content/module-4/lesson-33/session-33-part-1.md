# Session 33 Part 1 — From Firm Value to Equity Value: Cash and Cross-Holdings

## From Present Value to Value of Equity

So let's review where we are in this process. In coming up with the discounted cash flow valuation of a company, we've talked about estimating cash flows and discount rates, we then moved on to growth, and then we closed off by looking at terminal value. We're almost there, right? You discount the cash flows back, add to your cost of capital, you should come up with a value. But what is that value?

So let's talk about what you get as your present value when you discount cash flows back at a discount rate. It depends on what the cash flows were and what discount rate you used. If you discounted dividends at the cost of equity, what you get as a present value is the value of equity. So in the dividend discount model, you're done. If you'd estimated free cash flow as equity, potential dividends, and discounted them back at the cost of equity, what you get is the value of equity, but to the extent that you have equity options outstanding that managers might have been granted options, to get to a value per share, now there's some work to do.

But if you discounted free cash flows to the firm at the cost of capital, your work, unfortunately, is just beginning, because here's what you've estimated. By discounting the free cash flow to the firm at the cost of capital, you valued the operating assets of the company. Let me explain why. The free cash flow to the firm is based on operating income, right? So you've estimated the value of any assets whose earnings are part of operating income. Put differently, you haven't valued any of those assets whose earnings are not part of operating income.

I don't mean to sound mysterious, but if you value a company like Apple, you know what you haven't valued yet? You haven't valued the cash that the company has. If you have companies that have cross-holdings in other companies, you haven't valued those cross-holdings yet. So you've got to add the cash, you have to add the cross-holdings. In some cases, there might be other assets you might add on to this value, assets that haven't been utilized, that are vacant assets. That gives you the value of the firm.

To get to the value of equity, you need to subtract out the value of debt. But that's not all. To the extent that you granted equity options or restricted stock to managers in your company, that reduces your value of equity in common stock. You got to subtract out at least the value of the equity options, and what you're left with is the value of the equity in the common stock. So let's talk about each of the items that you have to deal with after you value the cash flows to get to the value of equity per share.

## Valuing Cash: Neutral, Discounted, or Premium

Let's start with cash. The normal rule with cash is, don't mess with it. If you have a company with $100 million in cash, just add the $100 million and move on, and that's what we tend to do in discounted cash flow valuations. But let me ask you a provocative question. Are there conditions where you see a company with $100 million in cash where you might actually treat the $100 million as worth less than $100 million, if so, why? Or more than $100 million, if so, why?

To illustrate why this might happen, let me give you three companies that look exactly the same in terms of enterprise value and cash. In fact, all three companies have $100 million in cash on their balance sheet. But here's how they're different. Company A has a history of earning a return on capital roughly equal to its cost of capital, and is a developed market company. Company B is a company with a history of taking bad projects, earning a return in capital of 5% when its cost to capital is 10%, also developed market company. Company C has a history of taking good projects, earning well above its cost to capital, about 10% more than its cost to capital, and it's an emerging market company.

I'm going to argue that in one of these three companies cash is going to be a neutral asset. What do I mean by a neutral asset? $100 million in cash is going to be valued at roughly $100 million. In one of these three companies, cash might be discounted in the hands of the company, and I'd like you to start thinking about which one. And one of these three companies, cash might actually have a premium attached to it. Think about it.

I would argue that in Company A, cash is going to be a neutral asset. Why? Because this company has a history of being a blah company. What's a blah company? A blah company is one that earns roughly its cost of capital. It does no good, it does no damage.

In Company B, I'm going to argue that cash might have a discount attached to it. Why? Not because it's cash and earns a low rate of return, but because you're afraid of what the company might do with the cash. This company has a history of taking bad projects. You're afraid they will use the cash to take more bad projects. There will be a stupidity discount you attach to the cash.

For the third company, you might actually attach a premium to the cash, and here is why. Not only does it have a history of taking good projects, but it's an emerging market, in this case, Argentina. A market where, in the last 15 years, there have been at least four scenarios or four time periods where capital markets have shut down, both equity and debt markets. Having cash in a market like that one could be a strategic weapon that adds value to the company. Adds value why? Because this cash can be used not only to allow the company to survive a period where capital markets shut down, but be used to buy assets from other companies that are desperate for the cash.

Put differently, cash might sometimes have a premium or discount attached to it. In fact, there is some evidence that markets actually do this. This is actually from a study that looked at how much a dollar in cash is valued in the hands of a U.S. company. And here's what it found. On average, a dollar in cash is valued at about a dollar, which is what we tend to do in discounted cash flow valuations. But if you look at mature companies, slow growth companies with negative excess returns, companies like Company B, here's what you find. A dollar in cash is valued at about 69 cents. That's a 31% discount. And if you focus on young growth companies, especially smaller companies that face capital constraints, companies like Company C, a dollar in cash is valued at $1.22.

So if you're adding on the cash balance to get your value for your firm, keep doing what you're doing. But in some cases, when you find your company to be undervalued, step back and think about why that might be. It just might be that investors don't trust the company with the cash that it has on its hands.

## Cross-Holdings and the Three Accounting Treatments

Let's talk about cross-holdings. Cross-holdings are difficult to value because the accounting for cross-holdings is all over the place. In fact, generically, there are three ways a cross-holding can be accounted for.

It can be treated as a minority passive investment, in which case, here's what you're going to see. In the income statement, you're going to see the dividends you receive from that cross-holdings. In the balance sheet, you're going to see what you originally invested to get that cross-holding. Already, you can see a disconnect opening up between what you see on the income statement and the balance sheet and the value of a cross-holding, especially if you invest in a company that's become a very valuable successful company.

The second scenario for accounting is, if you own 15%, 20% of a company and you're treated as having some control of that company. In that case, the accounting is treated as a minority active investment. What happens then? In the income statement, you have to show 15% or 20%, depending on the percentage you own of the net income or net loss of that subsidiary. So not dividends, but net income or net loss, that's a little better. And on the balance sheet, you have to show what you originally invested plus some minus retained earning sets. So it's an updated book value, a little better than minority passive investments.

But if you own 40%, 45%, 50%, or 55% of a company, you might be viewed as effectively controlling the company. When you do that, accountants consolidate. What does that mean? In your income statement, you record 100% of the revenues in the operating income of the subsidiary. In the balance sheet, you have to record 100% of the assets and the debt of the subsidiary. But you own only 60%, right? So here's what accountants do to bring your financial statements back into balance. They take the 40% that does not belong to you and show it as a liability. It's called a noncontrolling interest on a minority interest.

Why am I going on and on about the accounting for cross-holdings? Because if you don't understand how cross-holdings are accounted for, you really cannot value them.

## Valuing Cross-Holdings and Next Steps

So let me at least set up how you would value cross-holdings in a perfect world. If you gave me a company with cross-holdings in a perfect world, here's what I would do. I would value the parent company standing alone, and then I take each cross-holding, minority or majority, and value that subsidiary standing alone as well. Then to value of the parent company with the cross-holdings, I take the value of the parent company plus the percentage of each subsidiary that belongs to that company. That is the best way to value a company with cross-holdings, but already you can see the barriers you're going to face in doing this. To do this, you'll need full financial statements for the parent company as well as full financial statements for each and every subsidiary. And if you can get that, you can do it right. You can value it as the sum of the parts of the company.

I'm not sure whether your company has cross-holdings, but it definitely will have cash. So here's the first thing I'd like you to do. If you've done the discounted cash flow valuation for your company, and you've used a free cash flow to the firm and a cost to capital to get there, add on the cash for the moment. Don't worry about discounting it or premiuming it, just add on the cash. We'll come back and talk about what to do if you don't trust the company later. Then check for cross-holdings. You will find the vestiges of cross-holdings on your balance sheet. Why vestiges? Because the numbers you see on the balance sheet will not be the numbers that reflect the values of those cross-holdings. Depending on how the cross-holdings are accounted for, you have a lot more work to do.
