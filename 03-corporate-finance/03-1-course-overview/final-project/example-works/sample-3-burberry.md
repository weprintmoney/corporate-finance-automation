Burberry’s Short History
Burberry is a luxury British fashion house established in 1856 by Thomas Burberry and headquartered in
London. Since the invention of gabardine by Thomas Burberry more than 150 years ago, outerwear has
been at the core of the business, and remains so today – best expressed through the iconic Burberry
trench coat. The brand manufactures clothing, fragrances, and fashion accessories. Burberry’s
distinctive check pattern is one of the most widely known trademarks in the world. Burberry was an
independent company until 1955, when it was taken over by Great Universal Stores (GUS). In the 70s
the brand was strongly associated with football firms, hooliganism and later the ‘chav’ stereotype,
gaining negative connotation. Since then, the brand as tried to re-invent itself by massive advertising,
celebrity endorsements and much more. In 2006, when Rose Marie Bravo retired and Angela Ahrendts
took over the company, the check was placed back on the inside of the garments and British celebrity
endorsements helped gain a positive view of the brand once again.

The business is structured by channel, region and product division supported by core corporate
functions.

533 institutional investors owning ~$495 million worth of shares, with more than 98% of the

The largest investor in the company is the world’s largest investment management

The company’s board consist of currently consists of 10 members – the chairman, the Chief

Risk – Marginal Investor and Hurdle Rates
Since the marginal investor is well diversified, I have looked at only market risk while estimating
the cost of equity. The market risk is measured with the market beta. Since the regression beta
had a large standard error (.137 on a beta estimate of .987), I have used a bottom-up beta
estimate for Burberry using the “Single Business – Global“ approach because Burberry is single
business brand – selling luxury merchandize. The company has three revenue streams – Retail
(including digital), Wholesale and Licensing.

Current Market Value of Equity - £7,144,200,000
Current Market Value of Debt - £144,113,289
Current Market Debt to Equity Ratio – 2.02%
Regression Beta – 0.987
Equity Risk Premium (weighed based upon region) = 7.65%
Using the above values I have estimated the bottom-up levered beta of 0.74 for Burberry.
Using a T-bill rate of 1.33% for UK and a risk premium of 6.35%
       Cost of Equity = Risk Free Rate + Risk Premium * Beta (bottom-up levered)
                        = 1.33 + 7.65*0.74
                        = 7.01%
Since the regression beta has large standard error, I will go with the Cost of equity based on the
bottom-up levered beta.

Burberry is not rated at Bloomberg but because it has an annual turnover of more than £5
billion, it is a type 1 company. Based on the type of the company and its interest coverage ratio
of 146.25, the company gets a synthetic rating of ‘AAA’.

In conjunction with Burberry’s after-tax cost of borrowing of 1.68%, which is estimated based
on its rating above, I estimate a cost of capital for the firm of 6.90%

Cost of Capital = Cost of Equity * (1 – Market D/Capital) + After-tax cost of debt * (Market D/Capital)
             = 7.65% * (1 – 1.97%) + 1.68% *(1.97%)
             = 6.90%
I used a 21% marginal tax rate for UK to compute the after-tax cost of debt.

Investment Analysis and Stock Price Performance

Burberry is a fashion house and is typically involved in design projects designing and then
producing collections for every year for spring, autumn and winter season. Burberry is also
heavily involved in participating and organizing international fashion shows. While I do not have
data breakdown for each such project, I can measure Burberry’s return on capital as one firm.
In 2014, Burberry earned a pre-tax operating income of £468,000,756 on a book value of capital
invested of £756,200,715. The after-tax return on capital based upon these numbers is 46.27%

Return on Capital = EBIT (1-t)/Book Value of Capital from 2013-2014 where t = effective tax rate
                    = £468,000,756 (1- 25.23%)/£756,200,715
                     = 46.27%
Relative to the cost of capital of 6.90%, Burberry is enjoying a big earnings in excess returns of
39.37% and a positive EVA
EVA = (.4627 - .0690) £756,200,715 = £297,716,204
This helps in explaining that Burberry’s stock has been a good investment in the last four years
when it earned 13% higher than expected each year. (Intercept of the regression/1- regression beta =
.174/ (1-.987) =13%).
What might account for Burberry’s excess returns? The firm clearly has competitive advantage
in the luxury market with its international recognition and broad appeal. Additionally, Burberry
has a unique history and positioning as the authentic British lifestyle brand. It has a highly
successful merchandising and marketing strategy across both apparel and accessories,
diversified distribution channels and geographic profile, proven international management
team and most important of all Burberry did not limit itself to embracing modern technology
only in its stores - it aggressively exploited the opportunities offered by the Internet, digital
marketing and social media by making its online experience just as distinctive and 'branded' as
its in-store experience. By doing so, it also widened its reach to a wide global audience.

To maintain its excess returns, Burberry should focus on the following:
    Continue to ride on the British heritage. It’s appealing to the world.
    Focus on the younger generation, target the millennial market.
    Keep the brand globally unified.
    Continue to expand internationally, especially Asia which brings the largest share of the
      revenues, has the largest youngest population than anywhere else and a burgeoning
      middle class with deeper pockets than before.
    Re-launch new designs for products that are historically associated with the brand like
      the trench coats, cashmere scarves and ponchos.
