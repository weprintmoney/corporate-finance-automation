# Session 35 Part 2 — Deconstructing and Applying Pricing Multiples

So let's say, based on the first half of this session, you have the pricing for your company, different multiples for your company, and a distribution of each of those multiples across the sector for your company. Let's talk about the next step. How do you decide whether your company is cheap or expensive? So let's turn to the third step in deconstructing multiples.

## Embedded DCF Assumptions in Every Multiple

Earlier, when we talked about discounted cash flow valuation, we said the value of a company is a function of its cash flows, growth, and risk. A lot of people prefer not to do discounted cash flow valuation because they don't like to make assumptions about cash flow, growth, and risk. So what do they do instead? They use a multiple. They do pricing. And in doing so, they assume that they're running away. They're escaping that responsibility of actually estimating what cash flows, growth, and risk have to be.

Well, unfortunately, that doesn't work because embedded in every multiple are all of the assumptions you make in discounted cash flow valuation. In fact, when you decide to use a multiple, whether it be PE ratios or EV to EBITDA or EV to sales, I'm going to argue there are two questions you need to answer before you use that multiple sensibly. The first is you need to identify the variables, the fundamentals that drive that multiple. And I'm going to show you a very simple process for backing out those variables. And once you back them out, here's what you're going to discover. Embedded in every multiple are assumptions about cash flows, growth, and risks, the same assumptions you were making in discounted cash flow valuation.

Second, as those variables change, you need to tell me how your multiple will change. So let me give you an example. One of the variables that drives PE ratios is expected growth. As growth increases, PE should increase, but you need to be much more explicit than that. You need to be able to tell me for every 1% increase in growth rate in your sector, how much will the PE for a company go up? And the reason is without being able to answer the second question, you cannot compare PE ratios across companies within a sector.

## Backing Out the Drivers: The PE Ratio Example

So let's try the trick that's going to allow us to back out the variables that drive a multiple. I'm going to take an example. Let's assume that the multiple I'm looking at is the PE ratio, and I want to find out what drives the PE ratio. Here's what I'm going to do. Because the multiple is an equity multiple, I'm going to go back to the simplest equity valuation model I can think of, a stable growth dividend discount model. Value of equity per share in that model will be the expected dividends per share next period divided by the difference between the cost of equity and the growth rate. It's called the Gordon Growth Model.

Let's say I divide both sides of that equation by the earnings per share. On the left-hand side now, I have the PE ratio. On the right-hand side, dividing dividends per share by earnings per share gives me the payout ratio. I end up with an equation for the PE ratio for a company in a stable growth dividend discount model. And guess what, the PE ratio for a stable growth dividend paying stock is a function of three variables: its payout ratio, its cost of equity, and its expected growth rate.

In fact, I can give you the dynamics. Holding all else constant, as my growth rate increases, my PE ratio will go up. Holding all else constant, increasing my cost of equity, the risk of the company, will lower my PE ratio. And holding all else constant, increasing my payout ratio will increase my PE ratio. If you're puzzled by the last variable, remember, the only way I can increase the payout ratio while holding the growth rate fixed is by earning a higher return equity. So put differently, here's what I'd expect to see in a low PE ratio stock. I'd expect to see a low growth rate, perhaps high risk, or a low return equity. Most companies that look cheap deserve to be cheap. So in pricing, you're trying to make sure that when you see a stock have a low PE ratio or a low EV to EBITDA, there isn't a good fundamental reason that explains it away.

## Extending the Approach to Other Multiples

You can extend this approach to almost every multiple, and here's what you should do. If you have an equity multiple, price earnings, price-to-book, any equity multiple, go back to dividend discount model. Do the algebra. At the end of the algebra, you should have the variables that drive that multiple. With PE as you saw, those variables are cost of equity, growth, and payout. When you do the analysis for price-to-book, you're going to see a new variable pop up, its return equity. The price-to-book ratio for a company is a function of its cost of equity, its growth rate, its payout ratio, and its return equity. So with every equity multiple, you're going to be able to identify the variables that drive that multiple.

If you have an enterprise value multiple, EV to EBITDA, EV to sales, go back to the simplest enterprise value model that you can think of. To me, that's a stable growth free cash flow to the firm model. Value of a firm is the expected free cash flow to the firm next year divided by the cost to capital minus the growth rate. Do the algebra, and you'll have the variables that drive each of these multiples. Every multiple has driver variables, and to use that multiple well, you need to know what those driver variables are.

## Applying a Multiple: Comparables and Controlling for Differences

So now we're ready. We've defined, we've described, we've analyzed. Let's talk about applying a multiple. To apply a multiple, there are two decisions you have to make. The first is, what are those comparable firms? What are you going to define as your comparable firms? Now, if you ask most analysts that question, the answer is if it's a software company, I need to look at other software companies. If it's a utility, I need to look at other utilities. My pushback is, why? If you're looking at valuation, there's nothing in valuation that says that a utility has to be compared to another utility, a software company has to be compared to another software company. In fact, in valuation, a comparable company is one with similar cash flows, similar growth, and similar risk no matter what sector you're in. So perhaps, we should start defining comparable much more broadly.

And second, no matter how careful you are, after you've got those comparable firms, your company would still have differences from those comparable firms. You got to figure out how to control for those differences. So you ask me, is Disney cheap or expensive relative to the entertainment sector? I'm going to do a lot of hand-waving, but the bottom line is I can't tell you. Why? Because its PE ratio's close to that of the sector, its growth rate is lower, but it's probably a less risky firm than the typical entertainment company. So you can see very quickly why storytelling can lead you into the wrong places with pricing.

## Deutsche Bank vs. European Banks: A Regression Approach

So you think, "What do I do?" To illustrate one possible solution, let me look at European banks in November of 2013, and Deutsche relative to those European banks. I know this is an extended table with lots of companies on it, but here's what I'd like you to focus on. Focus on Deutsche's price-to-book ratio which is 0.67, and focus on the median for the sector which is 0.89. Based on the price-to-book ratio, it looks like Deutsche is cheap, right? But at the same time, Deutsche's return on equity of 0.93% is much lower than the typical return on equity in the sector. Deutsche looks cheap, but there's a good reason why it looks cheap.

So here's one way to explain the difference. I took the price-to-book ratios for the European banks and I ran a regression against two numbers. One was the return equity, the second was the tier 1 capital ratio. Why do I include these two variables? The return equity tells me how good the projects are that the banks have taken. The higher the return equity, the higher the price-to-book ratio. And the tier 1 capital ratio tells me how under or over-capitalized these banks are. I would expect banks that are in a high return equity and have a higher tier 1 capital ratio to trade at a higher price-to-book ratio, and the regression backs it up. The T statistics is significant, the r-squared is 24%. I'd like it to be higher but it's as good as it's going to get.

I plug the numbers for Deutsche into that regression: its return in equity which is minus 0.93% and its tier 1 capital ratio which is 15.13%. I come up with a predicted price-to-book ratio for Deutsche of 1.00. At its actual price-to-book ratio, 0.67, Deutsche looks cheap even after I've controlled for its low return equity and its tier 1 capital ratio. I'm not suggesting that statistics is going to give you the answer every time you got to do a pricing, but it's a tool that should be in your toolkit to control for differences across companies.

## A Practical Playbook

So you want to try pricing your company? Here's what I suggest you do. Find your comparable companies and define comparable broadly. It's good to have a bigger sample rather than a smaller sample. Then once you got that bigger sample of comparable companies, pick a multiple, pick the variables that you think should drive that multiple, and try some statistics. You'll be surprised at how much power you'll get once you can start to put a number on the questions that you have to answer. You're well on your way to pricing your company.
