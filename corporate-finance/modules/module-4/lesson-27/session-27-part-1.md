# Session 27 Part 1 — Assessing Dividend Policy: Trust, Projects, and the Cash Matrix

## From Potential Dividends to Assessment

Last session we talked about estimating potential dividends, free cash flow to equity, and comparing them to the cash actually returned in the form of dividends and buybacks as a starting point for assessing dividends. In this session, we're going to talk about adding some tools to the process that'll let you decide which companies you're going to push to pay out more in dividends, which companies you will push to pay less in dividends, and which you're going to leave alone.

So let's extend our discussion of dividends. In the last session, I talked about how much a company can afford to pay out in dividends as the free cash flow to equity, the cash left over after every conceivable need. Then I asked you to compare that cash that is available to be paid out in dividends to what the company actually does pay out. That becomes the starting point for assessing dividend policy, because when you make that comparison of what your company actually returns to what it could have returned, one of three things is going to be true.

Your company might be that unusual company that actually pays out what it can afford to in dividends. There are a few companies that do that. Those companies follow what's called a residual dividend policy. The other two cases are more interesting. One or two things can be true when you look at those other two cases. Either your company can be one of those companies that returns less cash than it has available and builds up a cash balance, or your company can be one of those companies that returns more cash than it can and burns through cash balances.

## Do You Trust the Managers with Your Cash?

So let's take each of those branches. Let's start with the branch where your company is accumulating cash. It's holding back cash, either because it has a positive free cash flow to equity and does not pay a dividend, or it has a positive free cash flow to equity and pays out less than that amount in dividends. So assuming your company is a company that does build up cash. It's holding your cash. The question you're going to ask is, do I trust the managers of this company with my cash?

Now, of course, you could ask them, "Can I trust you?" But that's a dumb question because you know what the answer is going to be, "Of course." So rather than ask managers, I'm going to look at their track record, track record in terms of taking projects. Remember how we measure the quality of projects by looking at the difference between return in invested capital and cost of capital? You could also look at what kind of returns it will deliver to you as a stockholder by looking at the Jensen's alpha, a measure of how well or badly the stock has done in the past.

If your company has a history of taking great projects and delivering great returns, guess what? You're going to trust them with your cash. If they don't, you're going to push to get your cash back. Not because you're greedy, but because you're prudent.

So that's if your company holds back cash. You're saying, "What if my company is paying out more than it's free cash flow to equity in dividends?" Again, I'm going to ask the same question. Do you have good projects? Because if you have good projects, you're doing double damage to yourself by returning too much cash. That cash could've been invested in your good projects, and perhaps you should have held them back. And by returning that cash, you're creating a deficit, a deficit that you can make up only by burning through past cash balances.

Of course, if you don't have good projects, then I'm not sure what to do next. Because if I ask you to cut your dividends because you're paying too much, I'm afraid that that cash might find its way into the bad projects. So this is a case where I'm going to argue that your dividend problem has its roots in you taking bad projects. Unless you fix the root of that problem, your inclination to take bad projects, this problem will continue and come back.

## The Dividend Assessment Matrix

So ultimately what you do for a company is going to be different depending on the characteristics of the company. And I like to use a matrix to capture the differences. And in this matrix, in the two axes, here is what you see. On one axis, you see whether your company has good projects or bad projects, and I'll let you determine how you measure good or bad. I look at the difference between return in capital, and cost of capital is my simple proxy for good or bad projects. If you earn more than your cost of capital, you have good projects. If you earn less, you have bad projects. On the other axis, I look at how much you return in the form of dividends and buybacks relative to your free cash flow to equity. Here, if you return less, you have a cash surplus. You're building up cash balances. If you return more, you have a cash deficit.

Here's why I find the matrix useful. The companies in the right-corner box, this is the box of companies that have good projects and are accumulating cash, get the maximum flexibility when it comes to building up cash balances. It is no surprise that companies like Google can accumulate huge amounts of cash and not feel any pressure. Investors trust them with the cash. If you're a company that has a history of taking bad projects, and you accumulate cash, don't be surprised to see pressure build up on you from investors to give back more of the cash. If you're a company that has a cash deficit in good projects, you're hurting yourself. You need to cut your dividends or buy back less stock. And if finally you're a company with bad projects, and you're giving back too much cash, as I argued, your problem is really an investment problem that's manifesting itself as a dividend problem. You've got to deal with the investment problem first if you want to deal with the dividend problem.

So when you get a chance, you might want to put your company on this matrix to see where it sits, because that's going to tell you a great deal about what you want to do next with the company.

## Disney Through Time: 2003, 2009, and 2013

So let me take a company and take it through the process. It's the company we should be familiar with by now, a company we've come back to over and over again. I took a look at Disney in 2003, way back in time, but there's a reason I want to go back in time. If you remember, in 2003, Disney was still a company run by Michael Eisner in his waning days. In these waning days, investors did not trust Michael Eisner, but here's what the company looked like.

In the 10 years leading into 2003, Disney had $969 million in free cash flow to equity. They could have paid out $969 million every year. They actually paid out about $639 million a year. So each year, they are holding back about $330 million in cash. And guess where this shows up. They had a cash balance in excess of $3.3 billion. You're saying, "So what?" Remember the question about cash balances? Do you trust the managers of this company with your cash?

So if you're a stockholder in Disney in 2003, let me give you some ammunition you could have used to argue with Disney. Between 1994 and 2003, Disney earned a return in equity on its projects about 2% lower than its cost of equity. It didn't take very good projects. Its stock price didn't do very well either. Over that same 10-year period, its Jensen's alpha was about minus 3%. It did 3% less than it should have, given its risk and given how the market did. So you have 10 years of bad history. And here's what Disney's management is asking you, "Do you trust us with this cash?" Think about it. Would you trust a company with your cash if it has a history of taking bad projects and delivering bad stock returns? My guess is no. In fact, what would have made this even more emphatic is the fact that the CEO asking you for the trust was Michael Eisner. A CEO, as we've noted, who was an imperial CEO, who did not really care about his stockholders in the good days, essentially asking you for some slack in the bad days. In 2003, my guess is it would be very unlikely for Disney to be able to get away with accumulating cash.

You're saying, "So what?" Let's move forward to 2009. By 2009, the numbers had shifted. A new CEO had come in in the form of Bob Iger. He was actually returning more cash than he had coming in. In other words, the dividends and buybacks at Disney had started to exceed the free cash flow to equity. He was burning through cash, but here's the other factor that you have to keep in mind. Between 2003 and 2009, with a new CEO in place, Disney had turned things around on both dimensions. What both dimensions? The first is the project choice. Remember they were earning less than the cost of equity prior to 2003? They started making more than the cost of equity. They were generating excess returns. They were taking good projects. And their Jensen's alpha, which was a negative number in 2003, had become a positive number.

So let's say it's 2009, and Bob Iger were in front of you, and you were a shareholder in Disney. And he said, "Look, we'd like to start to accumulate cash again. We've been paying out too much in cash." Would you cut him some slack? I think you'd be more inclined to give him the benefit of the doubt because he's now had a five or six-year history of delivering returns that are greater than your required returns, both in terms of projects and stock returns. You could actually see why for the same company you can go from shareholders cutting the company a lot of slack to pulling back on the company or vice versa.

Let's do one final look at Disney. In 2013, Disney had returned to holding cash. It had gone back to paying out less than its free cash flow to equity in the form of dividends and buybacks. And, in fact, in 2013, it was still earning a return on capital higher than the cost of capital. It continued to take good projects and continued to deliver positive returns. If I came to you as Disney's CEO in 2013 and asked you, "Hey, can we keep accumulating cash?" My guess is I have a much stronger hand than Michael Eisner had in 2003.

## Companies Earn the Right to Accumulate Cash

The bottom line is, companies earn the right to accumulate cash. They're not endowed with that right. So if you have a good history of taking projects and delivering returns for your stockholders, you will be able to accumulate cash without any blow-back. If you have a bad history, get ready for a reaction from shareholders when you want to hold back their cash.

So let's start to make that assessment for your company. If you've already estimated the free cash flows to equity and the dividends and buybacks for your company, I'd like you to also look at the Jensen's alpha which you might have already computed for your company and what kind of returns your company makes on its invested capital relative to the cost of capital. Bring them all together in your assessment. If your company has good projects, delivers good returns, and it has a big cash balance, just let it go. It's acquired the right to hold on to your cash. If your company has a bad history, you're within your rights. You're well within your rights to demand that that cash be returned back to you, and even if you don't, there will be others who put that pressure on the company.
