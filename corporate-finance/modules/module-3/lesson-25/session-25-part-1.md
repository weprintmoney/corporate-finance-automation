# Session 25 Part 1 — Three Schools of Thought on Dividends

In the last session, we introduced basic facts about dividend policy and how to measure how much a company pays in dividends. In this session, let's talk about different schools of thought when it comes to how much a company should return to its shareholders. And as you will see, these schools of thought range a spectrum.

## The Three Schools of Thought

So are dividends good, bad, or neutral? Should companies pay more in dividends, less in dividends, or no dividends at all? There are three schools of thought when it comes to the trade-off on dividends.

Here's what the first school of thought says. It says if you live in a world with no taxes, where there are no tax disadvantages associated with dividends as opposed to capital gains, and companies can issue stock whenever they want to raise equity, and there are no floatation or transactions cost involved, it doesn't matter how much you pay in dividends. That's the Miller-Modigliani school on dividends.

The second school of thought says that dividends are bad because they create a tax disadvantage to investors. And this school of thought had its roots in the U.S. in the last century, when, in fact, there was a tax disadvantage associated with the receipt of dividends by investors. Dividends were taxed at a higher rate than capital gains.

There is a third school of thought. The third school of thought says there are some investors out there who like dividends. Why? It doesn't really matter. They just like dividends. If those investors happen to be your shareholders, the school of thought says your job is to pay more in dividends. And if you do, your stock price will go up.

So dividends can be good. Dividends can be bad. Dividends can be neutral. It's extremely convenient to have three schools of thought that give you three very divergent conclusions. Because no matter what you do as a company, you have an entire school of thought backing you up. But the reality is all three schools of thought can't be right. So let's take a look at each of these schools of thought and try to explain why they come to the conclusions that they do.

## The Miller-Modigliani School: Dividends Don't Matter

So, let's start with the dividends-don't-matter-school, the Miller-Modigliani school. There are two big assumptions that drive the school of thought. The first is, if you're an investor, you get taxed at exactly the same rate on dividends as you do on capital gains. The second is, if you as a company have to issue equity, you can do it without any transactions cost and floatation cost.

And here is why you have to make those assumptions. In this school of thought, when you pay too much in dividends, more than you should, here's what happens. Your investors get a larger portion of their expected return in dividends and less in capital gains. So if your expected return is 9%, you might get 6% of that from dividends and 3% from capital gains, because the company chose to pay more in dividends. So your total return does not change, the composition does. And because you get taxed at the same rate, you don't care.

You're saying, "But the company paid too much in dividends. Doesn't it mean that it'll take fewer projects?" Not in this case, because here's what the company does. Because there are no transactions cost, so floatation cost and issuing new equity, the company will go out and issue new shares, use the equity from those shares to take the projects they were planning to do anyway.

So, in this school of thought what happens is companies take exactly the same investments no matter what their dividend policy is. Their value is not determined by how much they pay in dividends. Investors might get more in dividends with one policy than another, but they don't care either. So in this school of thought, dividends are neutral.

## The Dividends-Are-Bad School: Tax Disadvantages

Let's take the second school of thought, and it's fairly easy to see where this school of thought was born. As I mentioned in the last session, dividends for much of the last century in the U.S. have been taxed at a much higher tax rate than capital gains. And for some periods, the difference was stark. I gave the example of 1981. At the start of 1981, if you received dividends as an investor, they would treat it as ordinary income, and if you're in the highest tax bracket, taxed at 70%. If in 1981 you got capital gains, you were taxed at 28%.

Now you can already see, if you're an investor, and you pay a 70% tax rate on your dividends and a 28% tax rate on your capital gains, it seems silly that companies would continue to pay dividends. So the dividends of that school is based on tax differences between dividends and capital gains and how your investors get treated.

## Measuring Investor Tax Preferences: The Ex-Dividend Day

So let's say you're looking at your company, and you're trying to answer a very simple question. What do investors in my company think about dividends as opposed to capital gains when it comes to taxes? Now, you could ask them what their tax rates are, but that's a dangerous question, because investors often will refuse to answer that question. But there is a simple way in which you can bank out what investors in your company typically think about dividends as opposed to capital gains. And that's by looking at what's called the ex-dividend day.

The ex-dividend day is the day by which you have to buy shares in a company to be able to collect its dividends. So let's assume that you have a company where every investor in this company has bought shares in the company, let's say, four or five years ago. The price at which you bought these shares was P. So all of you bought shares on the same day at the same price. Tomorrow is going to be an ex-dividend day, and here are your choices you face as an investor.

You can sell before the ex-dividend day and get a price, let's call it PB, or you could sell after the ex-day and get a different price, PA. If you sell before the ex-dividend day, you don't get dividends. If you sell after the ex-dividend day, you collect the dividends.

So here is what your cash flows will look like as an investor. If you sell before the ex-dividend day, you're going to get whatever the price is, PB. You're going to pay capital gains on the difference between that price and what you originally paid, which is P. But you don't get the dividend. If you sell after the ex-dividend day, you get a different price. You pay capital gains on that different price, plus you get dividends on which you pay the tax rate on dividends.

For this market to be stable, in other words, for this market to be one where every investor doesn't try to sell the day before the ex-dividend day or the day after, you have to be indifferent between selling before and after. So, I'm gonna set those cash flows equal for the typical investor, and here's what I end up with. I end up with the price change in the ex-dividend day as a percentage of the dividend being a function of the tax rate I pay on my dividends and the tax rate I do on my capital gains.

This might sound like an abstraction, but here's what should you expect to see. If the typical investor in your company pays exactly the same tax rate on dividends and on capital gains, you should expect to see the price drop on the ex-dividend day be roughly equal to the dividend. If your investors get taxed at a higher tax rate on dividends than they do on capital gains, as was the case in the U.S. for much of the last century, you should expect to see prices drop on the ex-dividend day by less than the amount of the dividend. There's some parts of the world where dividends get taxed a lower rate than capital gains. In those parts of the world, you should actually expect to see the price drop on the ex-dividend day be greater than the dividend.

So here is what we're doing. On the ex-dividend day, we're observing what the price change is relative to the dividend. And from that, we're hypothesizing what type of taxes investors face in your company. So typically on ex-dividend days, you see the price drop be roughly equal to the dividend. I think it's reasonable to conclude that investors in your stock are pretty indifferent between receiving dividends and receiving capital gains, at least when it comes to taxes.

## Empirical Evidence from U.S. Stocks

Now, this statistic on what happens on the ex-dividend day has been measured, or in the U.S., repeatedly. The very first studies looked at U.S. stocks in the 1960s when there was a stark difference between the tax rate on dividends, was 70%, and the tax rate on capital gains. Those studies found that on the ex-dividend day in the late '60s, for a typical U.S. stock, the price change was only 78% of the dividend.

You move forward to 1981 through '85, the tax difference has narrowed. Now you're paying a 50% tax rate on dividends and a 20% rate on capital gains. The price drop is now 85% of the dividend.

Then you get to '86 through 1990, where, at least in theory, the tax rate on dividends and capital gains was the same. It was 28%. The price drop is now 90% of the dividend.

You're saying, "But shouldn't it be 100% of the tax rates are the same?" I'm going to argue that even if the tax rates are the same on dividends and capital gains, there is a benefit you get when you get capital gains. And here's the benefit. When you receive dividends, you have no choice about when you pay taxes. You have to pay tax in the year in which you get dividends. When you have capital gains, you get to determine when you pay taxes because you don't have to pay those taxes until you sell the shares. There's a timing option, and that timing option, in my view, will mean that capital gains will still be a little more advantageous to you, even if they're taxed at the same rate as dividends.

## When Dividends Matter More

Now, I know those sound like abstractions. You're saying, "Who cares about those schools of thought? We're going to bring those trade-offs together when we look at your company. But at least at the moment, you can see the rationale for why dividends might matter more in some companies than others. The closer you get to the Miller-Modigliani world, where there are big and open capital markets with low cost, and tax rates are the same across dividends and capital gains, the less dividends should matter. The more the tax rates diverge, the more dividends will matter.
