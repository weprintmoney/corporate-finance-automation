# Session 26 Part 2 — Free Cash Flow to Equity, Cash Accumulation, and Dividend Policy

So let's continue with this assessment of how much companies can pay out in dividends. I gave you the measure. Free cash flow to equity, a potential dividend, is the cash left over after you've met every conceivable need. So let's take that measure, try it on a company, and then talk about what happens when companies don't pay out what they can afford to in dividends, and also talk about what happens when companies pay out more than they can afford to in dividends.

## Microsoft 1996: Illustrating the Shortcut

The company I'm going to use to illustrate the shortcut is Microsoft in 1996. In 1996, here's what the numbers looked like for Microsoft. It had net income of $2,176 million, so it made a lot of money. Its capex was $494 million, and its depreciation was $480 million. Let me pause right there. Four-ninety-four minus $480 million gives me a $14 million net capex. If you're surprised that a company growing as fast as Microsoft in the mid '90s could get away with reinvesting very little, remember, accountants don't count R&D as capex. So that explains that small number.

The working capital increased by $35 million during the course of the year. So collectively, Microsoft reinvested about $49 million of the $2,176 million back into the company. In 1996, Microsoft used no debt. The free cash flow to equity for Microsoft is therefore the net income minus the net capex of $14 million minus the change in working capital of $35 million. And because the company uses no debt, all of that money, the reinvestment, has to come from equity. My free cash flow to equity for Microsoft, after I've done those computations and calculated them, is $2,127 million.

You're saying, "What does that measure?" It tells me that in 1996, Microsoft could have paid $2,127 million dividends or bought back stock with that cash. Guess what Microsoft actually did? It paid no dividends. It bought back no stock. So, let me ask you a question. If you have $2,127 million in potential dividends, and you pay no dividends, and you buy back no stock, where does that go? Where would you find that on your balance sheet? Here's where you're going to find it. You're not going to find it in fixed assets or current assets. Remember, this is after capital expenditures and working capital needs. You're going to find it as an increase in your cash balance.

In 1997, Microsoft had $3 billion in free cash flow to equity. Paid nothing out of dividends, bought back no stock. Its cash balance increases by another $3 billion. Do you see what's going to happen if a company does this year after year after year? You have huge free cash flows to equity. You pay out little or no dividends, and you buy back no stock, your cash balance is going to increase over time. If you ever wondered how companies end up with $50 billion, or $100 billion, or $150 billion cash balances, it's not manna from heaven that magically shows up in your balance sheet. It's a direct consequence of year after year holding back cash that you could have paid out as dividends.

## Free Cash Flow to Equity for Banks

So let's build on that concept of dividends and free cash flow to equity and think about whether we can do this for a bank. If you think about the way I computed free cash flow to equity for a manufacturing company or a non-financial service company, I started with net income. I subtracted out net capex. I subtract out change in working capital. Can you imagine trying to do that for a bank? The net income you can get, but what is net capex for a bank? Working capital, at some level, is everything in a bank's balance sheet or nothing. Computing free cash flow to equity using the approach that I described for non-financial service companies is not going to work for a bank.

So here's how I'm going to estimate the free cash flow to equity for a bank. It draws on a concept I introduced earlier. With a bank, when you think about reinvestment, you don't think about land building and equipment. You think about what the company has to put back or what the bank has to put back into its regulatory capital to be able to grow and survive. I'm going to treat whatever the bank has to reinvest in its regulatory capital as the equivalent of reinvestment. And that's going to be determined by two things, how quickly the bank is growing, and how undercapitalized or overcapitalized the bank is. Drawing on a concept we've already introduced, fast-growing undercapitalized banks will need to reinvest a lot more in regulatory capital. What does that mean? You subtract that from your net income, these banks might be unable to pay dividends or might even have to raise fresh equity to keep going.

I've tried this for Deutsche Bank in 2013. I've projected out the net income for the bank. I've subtracted out the investment I expect them to make in regulatory capital, and that requires some assumptions about how quickly they're growing, not that fast, and how much their regulatory capital ratio is changing, which is not that much. I end up with an investment in regulatory capital. You subtract that from the net income, you end up with a free cash flow to equity. And take a look at what I'm estimating as my free cash flow to equity in year one. I have a big negative number. You're saying, "What does that tell me about Deutsche Bank?" If Deutsche Bank is doing the right thing when it comes to dividends, it should not be paying dividends next year. Instead, it should be focusing on raising fresh equity, but here's where the problem comes in. Remember we said dividends are sticky? Deutsche has had a history of paying dividends, and they will continue to pay dividends even if their free cash flow to equity is negative. You're saying, "Well, how do they cover it?" Well, they issue even more equity. It's strange, but it's the result of that inertia that drives how companies look at dividends.

## Global Comparison: Potential vs. Actual Cash Returns

So let's use this concept of free cash flow to equity as potential dividends and compare it to the actual cash return looking across companies all over the world. I've taken all publicly-traded companies globally at the start of 2017 and broken them down into groups. So let me go group by group, and as I go with each group, I'd like you to think about whether these companies are doing what you'd expect them to do.

The first group are companies with negative free cash flows to equity. In other words, they are either losing money, or even if they're making money, reinvesting so much that they have negative free cash flow to equity. This first group, they have negative free cash flow to equity, and they have no dividends and no buybacks. Kind of makes sense, right? Negative free cash flow to equity don't return cash.

The second group of companies, the companies with positive free cash flows to equity. These could pay dividends or buy back stock, but they still have no dividends and no buybacks. What's happening at these companies? These are companies that are going to be accumulating cash like Microsoft was in 1996.

The third group of companies are companies with free cash flows to equity that are positive but which pay out dividends that are less than that free cash flow to equity. In other words, the free cash flow to equity could be $100 million, but they pay out only $50 million, or $30 million, or $10 million. These companies are also accumulating cash.

In the final grouping of companies here, I've looked at positive free cash flow to equity companies that pay out more than they can afford to in the form of dividends and buybacks. What are these companies doing? They're actually drawing down existing cash balances or trying to make up a deficit.

So when you look across companies, it's perhaps good to think about which of these groups you'd put your company into. Whether it's a company that's accumulating cash like the first three groups are, or paying out too much in cash, which is reducing cash, as the last two groups are. So if you look at companies, these are numbers you might want to start your analysis with.

## Chrysler 1994 and the Cost of Accumulating Cash

Now, let me go back to what I talked about earlier. I said if a company has positive free cash flows to equity, and it does not pay out dividends, and does not have buybacks, it's building up cash. I went further. I said if a company has positive free cash flow to equity and is paying out less than that free cash flow to equity, it's building up cash as well. You're saying, "So what?"

To see what happens when companies accumulate cash, I'm going to take you back in time to 1994. And this was actually the cash balance that Chrysler at that point in time had accumulated. Chrysler in 1994 had a cash balance of $8.5 billion that was the result of 10 years of building up cash balances. What I mean by that is I computed the free cash flows to equity every year for 10 years, and I computed the dividends and buybacks each year, and I computed what that change would do to the cash balance. So over that 10-year period, Chrysler built its cash balance up from almost nothing to $8.5 billion dollars.

In 1994, Chrysler was targeted by an activist investor, Kirk Kerkorian, and the reason he targeted Chrysler was because of its large cash balance. And his argument was Chrysler has too much cash. At $8.5 billion, that was almost 30% of Chrysler's value. Chrysler's initial pushback was "Hey, we need the cash. We're a cyclical company," and you could see very quickly why that argument didn't hold muster. If you look at this 10-year period that I've graphed here, in the middle of that time period between 1989 and 1991, there was a pretty severe recession in the US. Chrysler did burn through some cash. In fact, they burned through about a half a billion in cash. You can see why already $8.5 billion is way too much cash, even if you're a cyclical company. The second argument Chrysler used was "Hey, maybe we need this cash to do a big acquisition." That didn't go down very well with stock holders. Ultimately, Chrysler had to give in and return about half this cash back to the stockholders.

The point I'm trying to make is when you hold back cash, your cash balance balloons out. If you're a company that investors don't trust, you're very quickly going to see those investors put pressure on you.
