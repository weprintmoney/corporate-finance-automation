# Session 21 Part 1 — Adjusted Present Value Approach

In these last few sessions, we've used the cost of capital approach to come up with the optimal debt ratio for a company. In this session, I'd like to talk about other ways in which you can find the right mix of debt and equity for your company, and talk about the pluses and minuses of these other approaches.

## From Cost of Capital to Adjusted Present Value

In the cost of capital approach, the right mix of debt and equity is the one that minimizes the cost of capital, or more expensively, maximizes your value as a company. There is another way you can approach the effect of debt on value, and it's called the adjusted present value approach.

To understand the adjusted present value approach, let me break down the value of a company into two components. The first is the value that the company would have had if it had not borrowed any money at all. That's called the unlevered firm value. So in effect, you're saying, "If my company had been run as an all-equity funded company, how much would it be worth?" And then you can look at the effect of debt as a second factor.

Remember, the effective debt is twofold. There's a plus and a minus. The biggest plus of debt is a tax benefit. The biggest minus of debt is an expected bankruptcy cost. So in the adjusted present value approach, rather than try to incorporate the effect of debt into your discount rate of the cost of capital, you try to bring it in into the value by first calculating the value of the firm with no debt and then looking at the effect of debt on the value.

## The Three Steps of the APV Approach

So if you try to implement the approach, there are three steps you have to go through.

The first step is, you have to compute what the value of your company would be with no debt, the unlevered firm value. And there are two sub-approaches you can use to estimate that firm unlevered value. The first is to do a full-fledged discounted cash flow valuation, where you take the cash flows of the business and discount back at an unlevered cost of equity. Effectively, you're saying, "If I had no debt, that would've been my cost of capital." So that requires a full valuation, and that's a lot of work. The second approach is cheating, but it's much easier to get to. Here's what you do. You start with the market value of the company that you can observe given its existing debt, and then you back out the effect of the debt from that value. In other words, you ask, "What if I did not have the debt? What would my value be?" That will give you an unlevered firm value from which you can begin. So that's the first step is getting the unlevered value of the business.

Here's the second stop. You have to tell me what the tax benefits of debt are, and this is surprisingly easy number to estimate. Remember, the biggest tax benefit of debt is interest is tax deductible. So you have $100 million in interest expenses next year, and a 40% tax rate, you're going to save about $40 million in taxes. However, since debt is a permanent part of your capital, you're going to save this money not just next year, but in perpetuity. If you take the present value of your interest tax savings over time, it actually simplifies to the tax rate times a dollar debt that you take on. In other words, you borrow $1 billion at a 10% rate with a 40% tax rate, your total tax benefit will be 40% of your total debt, and that 40% will give you $400 million in tax benefits.

So at this point, we have the unlevered value of the firm. We have the tax benefits from debt. So third and final component, which is expected bankruptcy costs. As you borrow more money, you're increasing the probability that you will go bankrupt, right? And that's going to be true no matter who you are as a company. You could be the most stable company in the world, but you will still increase your probability of bankruptcy by borrowing more money. To get the expected bankruptcy cost, I need two numbers. I need to estimate that probability that you will go bankrupt, and I have to estimate the cost of going bankrupt, both the direct and the indirect costs.

## Estimating Probability of Default and Cost of Bankruptcy

So let's look at how we might try to estimate this number because this to me is the weakest link in the adjusted present value approach. To get the probability of default, perhaps, perhaps, I can use the synthetic ratings approach. Remember how we estimated what the rating for Disney would be at different debt ratios using the interest coverage ratio? If you can tell me what the expected rating for your company is, I might be able to tell you what the likelihood of default for your company is. So maybe we can estimate a probability of default.

The cost of bankruptcy is messier because there are two components to it. The direct cost is, of course, the legal cost, the dead weight cost of actually going bankrupt. And that's going to be pretty similar across companies, perhaps 5%, maybe 10% of the overall value. It's the indirect cost we're going to get variations across companies. Remember what we said about indirect bankruptcy costs. If you're a company that makes long-term tangible assets that requires servicing, your indirect bankruptcy costs will be much higher than if you're a company that's a service company. So your indirect bankruptcy costs could range anywhere from 5% or 10% to maybe 30% or 40%. And it's a number that's really difficult to estimate, but if you could estimate it, you could put the adjusted present value approach into practice.

So let's see if we can try this on Disney. I'm going to start by estimating an unlevered value for Disney. And to estimate the unlevered value for Disney, I'm going to need a probability of default at different levels of debt, or at different ratings. I started with some raw data. This is actually a table that is estimated every few years by my colleague, Ed Altman, at NYU. And basically, what he does, is he takes bonds in different ratings classes, and these are 10-year bonds, corporate bonds, and he looks at the default rates of each of these ratings classes over the following 10 years. So this is an ex-post analysis, and here's how you'd read it. If you're a AAA-rated company, there's a 0.07% chance of defaulting in the next 10 years, close to 0, and that's not surprising. If you're a BBB rated company, there's a 7.54% chance of defaulting in the following 10 years. And if you're a CCC rated company, that number rises to 59%. This is going to give me my probability to default, and I will need this when I analyze Disney.

## Applying APV to Disney

So let's try this on Disney. The first step to applying this approach for Disney is estimating an unlevered firm value for Disney, a value that Disney would have if they'd no debt. So here's how I'm going to do it. I'm going to start with the existing market value for Disney. The existing market value for Disney is $137.8 billion. That value, however, reflects a fact that Disney has $15.96 billion in debt. So the question I'm asking is, "What if the debt were not there?" Well, the first thing I'm going to lose is the tax benefits on that debt, right? So I take 36.1%, that's Disney's marginal tax rate, times $15.96 billion. That's a shortcut we use to compute the tax benefits. And I take it out of the value. Why take it out? Because if I did not have the debt, I would not have those tax benefits.

But I will gain something, right? Because if I don't have that debt, I would not have the expected bankruptcy cost that come from that debt. To estimate the expected bankruptcy costs, here's what I started with. I started with Disney's rating of A. And if you go back to the table that we just looked at, A rating gives you a 0.66% chance of bankruptcy. Then I estimated the cost of bankruptcy at Disney, which I estimate to be 25%. Why 25%? I've put Disney in the middle of the distribution in terms of expected costs of bankruptcy. In my view, the cost of bankruptcy can range anywhere from 10% to 40%, and I put Disney right in the middle, at 25%. Now, here's what my expected bankruptcy costs will be. It's going to be the 0.66%, based on my existing rating, times 25% of $137.8 billion. That gives me $227 million, but because I don't have the debt anymore, I'm going to add it back. So in effect, I'm subtracting out the tax benefit because I don't have the debt, and I'm adding back the expected bankruptcy costs. I get an unlevered firm value for Disney of $132.3 billion.

I'm almost home because here's what I do next. I compute the tax benefits and the expected bankruptcy costs for Disney at every debt ratio from 0% to 90%. So here's how it works. At a 10% debt ratio, I compute the dollar debt, $13.784 billion. I compute the tax benefits as 36.1%, the tax rate times that dollar debt. And that tax benefit, as you can see, increases as I increase my debt. For the expected bankruptcy costs, here's what I do. I start with that same dollar debt, compute an interest expense, an interest coverage ratio, and a rating, just like I did for the cost of capital, and I use that rating in conjunction with the table you just saw to estimate a probability of default. Then I estimate an expected bankruptcy cost with a 25% cost of bankruptcy driving it. And I get an expected bankruptcy cost that also rises as my debt ratio goes up. My adjusted present value is my unlevered firm value that I computed in the prior section plus the tax benefit from the debt level minus expected bankruptcy costs. Remember, my objective is to maximize the value of the firm, and that's going to happen at about 40% debt. That's very convenient. That's exactly the optimal debt ratio I used with the cost of capital approach, but don't expect it to happen with every company. The reason is, the big driver of the optimal debt ratio in the adjusted present value approach will be the cost of bankruptcy. And as I admitted for Disney, there is no solid reason I can give you for why it's 25%.

## Closing Encouragement

So do you want to try the adjusted present value approach for your company? Well, give it a shot. It's not that different from what you did in the cost of capital approach. But instead of incorporating the effect of debt into your cost of capital, you're going to try to bring it in explicitly as a tax benefit and an expected bankruptcy cost. Good luck to you.
