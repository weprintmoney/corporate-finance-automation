# Session 21 Part 2 — Relative Approach to Optimal Debt Ratios

## Intrinsic vs. Relative Approaches

So at this stage we have two different ways of computing the optimal debt ratio, the cost of capital approach and the adjusted present value approach. I call these approaches intrinsic optimal debt ratio approaches. Intrinsic because you're trying to figure out what the right mix of debt and equity for your company is based on its cash flows and its characteristics.

You say, "What are the ways then?" In this last part of this session, I'm going to look at the way in which most companies set debt ratios. You know what they do? They look at what everybody else in the sector is doing. I'm going to call this the relative approach to coming up with an optimal debt ratio for your company.

In the relative approach of coming up with the debt ratio for your company, you look at other companies in your sector. You look at the mix of debt and equity that they use, and you try to stay as close as you can to what the rest of the sector does. So everybody else borrows a lot of money, you do, too. If nobody else borrows money, well, you don't either.

You say, "Why would I want to do this?" Well, by staying close to the sector, you're, in effect, making sure that if you make a mistake, you'll always have lots of company. So understand the intuition, the rationale for why companies try to stay close to the sector. But I would suggest that we can refine this approach, even if it's the approach you want to use, and here's how.

Let's assume that I decide to compare Disney to the entertainment sector as we're going to do in a few minutes. As I look at Disney versus other entertainment companies, I might find that Disney has more debt, a higher debt ratio than other entertainment companies. But here's what I also have to remember. Disney is a much bigger company than the rest of the companies in the sector. I would expect it to be safer, and therefore borrow more money. Disney might have a higher tax rate than the other companies in the sector, in which case, I would expect it to borrow more money. Disney might have less risk that the other companies in the sector, in which case, I'd expect it to borrow more money.

Do you see where I'm going? When you look at a company relative to the sector, the fact that this company borrows more or less in the sector should not be the end of your analysis. It should be the beginning of your analysis.

## Comparing Companies to Their Sectors

So let's try this for the companies that we're looking at. Before I show you the numbers, I'll also have to tell you that the results I found were incredibly convenient, and I'll explain in a minute why they were convenient.

Let's take Disney. Here's what I did. I took Disney's actual debt ratio, both in market value terms, and book value terms, and gross terms, and net terms, and I compared them to industry averages. Well, what I call my industry is going to make a difference. So in this case, I looked at U.S. entertainment companies as my sector. And if you look at Disney's debt ratios relative to the sector using every measure of debt, whether it's book debt or market debt, gross debt ratios or net debt ratios, Disney has a lower debt ratio than the sector. I told you this is convenient, and here's why. Remember when I did the cost of capital approach and the APV approach, I came up with an optimal debt ratio for Disney that was much higher than the actual debt ratio. The case I was making to Disney is that they have too little debt. And the pushback I might have got might have been that the Disney CFO might have looked at me and said, "Well, if we borrow money, are we going to look strange? Is this typical of the entertainment sector?" At this stage, my argument is buffered by the fact that if I look at Disney relative to the sector, it actually has a lower debt ratio, making my case that Disney is under-levered stronger.

Let's move to Vale. When I look at Vale's actual debt ratios on every single measure, book or market, gross or net, Vale has more debt than the typical mining company. And here I'm looking at global mining companies as my sector. Here, again, it's strengthening my case that Vale is overlevered.

When I look at Tata Motors relative to the other automotive companies, here's what I find. I find that Tata Motors, on almost every debt ratio measure, book or market, gross or net, has a higher debt ratio than the sector. Again, strengthening my case that Tata Motors is overlevered.

Let's look at Baidu. Baidu, if you look at its debt ratio, has a low debt ratio, but so does the rest of the sector. And I concluded that Baidu was pretty close to where it should be in terms of its optimal, and that's what this analysis seems to back up as well.

So my job became easier after comparing the sector, because every one of my recommendations got strengthened by looking at the sector. That's not always the case. Here's what could happen. You could look at the optimal debt ratio for a company and conclude that your company is significantly under-levered, that it can go out and borrow more money. But when you compare it to the sector, it might turn that your company is doing exactly what other companies in the sector are doing, which is not borrowing money. Your case got a lot harder to make. Because you might be able to convince a company that it has excess debt capacity on a cost of capital basis, but it's unlikely that they will borrow money if it makes them look very different from the rest of the sector.

## Regression-Based Refinement: Sector

There's one final tweak you can apply on this relative analysis that perhaps might make it stronger. Remember what I did in the previous [inaudible] compare what Disney did, what Tata Motors did, what Vale did, what Baidu did to the sector averages. The average is just a single number. What if I could look at all of the data?

Let me explain. When you look at Disney against the entertainment sector, there are about 50 entertainment companies in that sector. And their debt ratios are all different. Perhaps I might be able to [inaudible] from that data other factors that drive debt ratios. I actually opened up my statistics book to the multiple regression chapter. In a multiple regression, you have a dependent variable that you're trying to explain and independent variables that you think should matter. That's what I'm going to bring to the debt ratio analysis. I'm trying to explain difference in debt ratios, and I think tax rates should matter. I think how variable your earnings are should matter, and your cash flows as captured by the EBITDA as a percent of your value should matter.

Let's say I could run a regression across my entertainment companies. I'm going to end up with a regression equation that tells me how entertainment companies vary in terms of debt ratios and why. Then I could take Disney's numbers, plug them into that regression, and I could get a predicted debt ratio based on how the rest of the sector is behaving, that's much richer than just comparing to the average.

Let's see how this works using the auto sector as an example instead of the entertainment sector. I took 56 global auto companies, and I ran a regression of debt to capital, against the tax rate, the EBITDA as a percent of value, and how much these companies were investing in CapEx as a percent of value. That regression has an R squared of 21%, nothing to get excited about, but it is what it is. When I plug the numbers for Tata Motors into this regression, here's what I get. I get a predicted debt ratio for Tata Motors of 18.5%. You're saying, "What does that tell me?" Given Tata Motors' tax rate, its EBITDA, and its CapEx, I would expect it to have a debt ratio of 18.5%. Its actual debt ratio is 29%. Based on this regression, I would conclude that Tata Motors has too much debt relative to how the rest of the auto sector is behaving. So, it's an extension of the relative analysis, and it effectively allows you to compare your company to the rest of the sector with much richer data.

## Regression-Based Refinement: Market

Let's do one final tweak. Why, if I can do this, essentially compare companies with very different characteristics, why stay sector-focused? Why not look at how the rest of the market is borrowing? In other words, I could run the regression you just saw for auto companies for the entire market. I do this once every year. I end up with a regression, for instance, here, of the 2014 data for U.S. companies, a regression that describes how debt ratios vary across U.S. companies as a function of the effective tax rate, the percent of shares held by institutions, the standard deviation and operating income over the previous 10 years, and how much each company had its EBITDA.

You're saying, "Why just these four variables?" Well, these were the variables that seem to have some explanatory power. That regression has an R squared of 8%. Again, not great, but I'm trying to explain debt ratio differences across the entire market.

You're saying, "What are you going to do with this regression?" So if you plug the numbers for Disney into the regression, you come up with a predicted debt ratio of 19%. Remember that Disney's actual debt ratio was 11%. That would tell you, given how the rest of the market is setting debt ratios, Disney has too little debt.

## Wrap-Up: Consensus Across Approaches

So let's wrap up. I've tried to compute the optimal debt ratio for each of my companies using different approaches. With Disney, for instance, I know the actual debt ratio is about 11.6%. Using the cost of capital approach, the optimal debt ratio I get is 40% without any constraints, 40% with the constaint of letting operating income change over time, and 40% with the adjusted present value approach. If I just compare to the sector average, I get 28.5% adjusted for the differences in debt ratios across the sector. And if I compare to the market, I come up with an 18% debt ratio. I have a consensus. Using every single approach for Disney, I've concluded that they're under-levered. I think it makes my case stronger because they all agree.

Now let's look at Vale. Vale's actual debt ratio is high. It's about 35%. It's higher than my optimal, if I use the cost of capital approach. Though, if I replace the operating income with a normalized number, I could come up with a higher optimal. It is higher than my enhanced cost of capital approach, and it's higher than what I get with the APV approach, and it is higher than the industry average. Here, again, I have mostly consensus. Vale looks over-levered.

When I look at Tata Motors with a 29% debt ratio, that's higher than my optimal using the cost of capital approach and the APV approach, and it's higher than the industry average. Again, the consensus view I get is that it's over-levered.

And finally, Baidu, using every approach, looks mildly under-levered or perhaps correctly levered.

That's the end game here, and here's what I'd like you to do for your company. Try as many of these approaches as you can. You're not going to lose by trying an approach. See if they give you the consensus that I got for my companies. And if they don't, try to decide which of these approaches you're going to use in deciding what the right mix of debt and equity is for your company.
