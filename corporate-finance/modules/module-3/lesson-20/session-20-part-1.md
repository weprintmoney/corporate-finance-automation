# Session 20 Part 1 — Weak Links in the Cost of Capital Approach

## Revisiting the Cost of Capital Approach

In the last few sessions, we've looked at using the cost of capital approach to come up with the right mix of debt and equity for Disney as a company. In this session, I'd like to look at the soft spots, the weakest links in that approach, and perhaps extend it to other companies.

So, let's think of what we tried to do in the cost of capital approach. We change the mix of debt and equity to see where the cost of capital is minimized, right? And we argue that that is the right mix of debt and equity for a company because if the cost of capital is minimized, we argued that the firm value is maximized. But is that true? Let's look at the weak links in the cost of capital approach.

## Weak Links: Static Operating Income and Distress Effects

Here's the first. As I change my mix of debt and equity, all that's changing in the cost of capital approach is the cost of capital. Nothing happens to my cash flows. My operating income remains as it is as I go from AAA rated, to AA rated, to BBB rated, to maybe C rated, and that's not realistic. We do know that as ratings drop, companies are hurt. They're hurt because customers might buy less of their products, employees might demand more to work for them, and in general, you could argue that the threat of distress, even if you're not in distress, the very threat of distress can have an effect on your operating income. So, the first thing we might want to consider is as the debt ratio changes it's not just the cost of capital that changes but your cash flows to the firm could also change, your operating income could change.

Now, the second problem with the cost of capital approach is it used last year's operating income, and last year's operating income is static. It's what happened last year. It could be different next year. So, perhaps if we could make this analysis more dynamic, we'll get a better measure of the right mix of debt and equity.

## Adapting the Approach for Disney

So, let's try adapting the cost of capital approach to Disney, and here's what I'm going to do. I'm going to make two changes. The first is as the rating changes…in other words, as I borrow more money and I adjust the rating to get to a cost of capital…I'm also going to allow the operating income to change, and here's how I'm going to do it. I'm going to assume that as your ratings drop, there could be an effect on operating income. In the case of Disney, the effect starts to happen once you get to investment grade. If you're AAA rated, AA rated, or A rated, I'm going to assume that Disney's operating income is what it is, but if it goes to a BBB rating, its operating income drops. By how much? Well, to the extent that Disney is a pretty large company and the drop is low, I would assume a 5% drop, but if this were a firm with a large indirect bankruptcy cost, you could make it as high as 15%. What you see in this table, actually, is the variance across firms with low indirect bankruptcy cost like Disney, all the way to companies with high indirect bankruptcy cost. That is the biggest change I'm going to incorporate into the analysis is I'm going to allow, as the debt ratio changes, not just that the rating will change but also the operating income.

Let's look at what the optimal debt ratio for Disney is if we allow the operating income to change as the debt ratio changes. Remember that the optimal without bringing in this constraint was 40%. Bringing in the constraint, it looks like the optimal doesn't change. In fact, it still stays 40%. The difference, though, is if you get past the optimal, the drop off in value gets much more dramatic. In other words, the caution I'd added earlier about it's better to be underlevered than overlevered, applies in spades now. So, my optimal debt ratio for Disney would still say at 40% but perhaps, perhaps, because of this analysis, I will stop a little earlier than 40%, perhaps at 30% or 35%.

So, it's easy to adjust the cost of capital approach to the fact that operating income can change as the debt ratio changes. If I wanted to, I could make this even more dynamic. Rather than compute the optimal just using last year's operating income, I could make operating income into a distribution. If you remember, in Capital Budgeting I talked about Monte Carlo simulations. You could do Monte Carlo simulations here and end up with a distribution of optimal debt ratios if you want to make this analysis dynamic. The bottom line is the cost of capital approach is a lot more flexible than people give it credit for, and it can be adjusted or adapted to meet whatever additional stuff you want to bring into the analysis.

## Applying the Approach to Tata Motors

Let's move on. Let's try to apply this approach to the other companies in my analysis. So, I took Tata Motors and I said, "Let's compute the optimal debt ratio for the company." You're saying, "But it's an Indian company. Can you do this?" Why not? Indian companies, Russian companies, Brazilian companies all have to minimize their cost of capital. They all want to maximize value. So, I did with Tata Motors exactly what I did with Disney. As the debt ratio changed, I adjusted the beta. I started with the bottom-up beta, the unlevered beta, and as the debt ratio went up, I estimated what the levered beta for Tata Motors would be at every debt ratio. That gives me the cost of equity at every debt ratio. Just as with Disney, I computed the interest coverage ratio and the synthetic rating for Tata Motors at every debt ratio. The one adjustment I had to make for Tata Motors that I did not have to do for Disney is I had to add the country default spread to the company default spread at each level of debt, but that just raises the cost of debt across the board.

I'll look for the optimal debt ratio and the optimal debt ratio for Tata Motors is about 20%. That is actually lower than their actual debt ratio of 29%. And if you're wondering, why? There are lots of reasons, but one could be that Tata Motors is part of a family group of companies. You're saying, "Why should that matter?" When I looked at the Tata Group, Tata Motors, Tata Steel, and Tata Chemicals, all looked overlevered by a little bit, but Tata Consulting Services looks significantly underlevered. Looking at it from the outside, it looks like Tata Motors is drawing on Tata Consulting Services' debt capacity to borrow money. So, that's the optimal debt ratio for Tata Motors. It's 20%.

## Applying the Approach to Vale

Let's try one final company. Let's take Vale, Brazilian mining company. Let's compute the optimal debt ratio. The optimal debt ratio for Vale, if I use their actual operating income, is about 30% debt. So, it's different from the Disney 40% and the Tata Motors 20%. It's 30% debt. But before I decide that Vale is overlevered because it has 39% debt, note that I'm using the operating income from the most recent year, just as I did for Disney and Tata Motors, but here, there's a consequence. Because Vale is a mining company, its earnings are very sensitive to the price of the commodity, iron ore, that it mines the most. So, iron ore prices were down in the most recent year, and that gets reflected in the optimal debt ratio.

In fact, the push-back you might get in this calculation is, "Hey, what if iron ore prices go back up? What if they normalize?" One way to normalize is to look at the average operating income over time and if, in fact, I use the average operating income over a period of time, it looks like I could afford a much higher debt ratio. In the case of Vale, using the average operating income over previous years that allows the iron ore price to be normalized, I get a 50% optimal. So, if you believe that iron ore prices will bounce back, perhaps. If you're the CFO of Vale, you're going to do nothing about the fact that you're at 39% debt ratio, or perhaps even borrow more. But if you feel iron ore prices are down for the count, they're not going to come back in the near future, then you are overlevered.

## Apply This to Your Company

So, that analysis essentially extends what I did for Disney to Tata Motors and Vale, two publicly traded companies very different from Disney. I don't know what company you've picked for your analysis, but whatever that company is, take the approach that we used for Disney, Tata Motors, and Vale, and apply it to your company. If you want, bring in that distress cost that I talked about. That's an option that allows you to change the operating income as the debt ratio changes and the rating changes. Look at what your optimal is. You're well on your way to analyzing the financing mix for your company.
