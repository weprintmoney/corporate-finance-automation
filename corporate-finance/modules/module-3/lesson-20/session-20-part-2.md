# Session 20 Part 2 — Optimal Debt Ratios Across Company Types

So now that we've talked about applying the cost-to-capital approach, which we developed with Disney, on to Tata Motors and Valley [SP], two other companies that are mature companies, you think, will this approach work if I'm looking at a young growth company, a private business, or a financial service firm? I think so. And that's what we're going to try to do in this next part of the session. So in the cost to capital approach, here's what we're doing: we're trying to minimize the cost to capital and perhaps even adjust the operating income to see that mix of debt and equity that maximizes the value of a business.

## Young Growth Company: Baidu

Will this work for a young growth company, a company like Baidu? I don't see why not. In fact, in this table I've looked at the optimal debt ratio for Baidu by doing exactly what we did for the other companies. And guess what? The optimal debt ratio for Baidu is about 10% debt. That's a pretty low debt ratio, right? You're saying how come? Is it because they're not making money? No, they are making money. But they have a very high market value because they're a young growth company, and the market reflects that in a high market capitalization. So this is a company with a low optimal debt ratio. And this goes back to what we talked about in the context of life cycles. Young growth companies can't afford to borrow much money, and the approach, in fact, backs this up. Incidentally, Baidu's actual debt ratio is about 5%, which makes it pretty close to correctly levered. So it is doing what it should be doing.

## Private Business: Bookscape

Let's now talk about the private business in our mix, Bookscape. Can we complete the optimal debt ratio for Bookscape? Yes. But we have to do some homework first, and here's why. With the other companies that we've looked at so far, we started with the existing market value of the company before computing the optimal. Well, with Bookscape, I don't have a market value for the company because it's a private business. So here's what I did. I estimated the market value by cheating. I took the net income from the most recent year, and I multiplied it by a P ratio for a book companies. What that gave me was an estimated market value. Once I could get that market value, I did exactly what I did with my other companies: look for that mix of debt and equity that minimizes my cost to capital. And in the case of Bookscape, that optimal debt ratio is about 30% debt. Bookscape is actually at about 28% debt, which tells me that they're pretty close to correctly levered. The bottom line is even if you have a private business without a market value, you can still compute an optimal debt ratio for your company using exactly the same techniques you had for a public company, as long as you're willing to estimate a market value for the equity, perhaps looking at publicly traded companies.

## Financial Service Firms: Regulatory Capital, Not Debt Ratio

Let's do one final example. What if you had to compute the optimal debt ratio for a bank? To begin with, that question actually doesn't make sense because remember what we said about banks. Debt to a bank is not a source of capital. It's raw material. So if you ask me whether a bank has to much or to little debt, I can't answer that question. But there's another question I can try to answer. If you ask me, does my bank have to much equity or to little equity? I can answer that question because banks, to operate, have to meet regulatory capital requirements.

So let me explain what I mean by this. Let's assume you have a bank with $100 million of loans outstanding, these are loans that are given to other borrowers, and that's its business, and a book value of equity of $6 million. Let's assume that it's regulatory capital is equal to its book equity. So basically, right now, it has a regulatory capital ratio of 6% and loans outstanding of $100 million. Let's make this bank a high growth bank that's under-capitalized. You say what are you talking about? Let's assume that next year the loans are going to jump from $100 million to $150 million. It's making a lot more loans. And let's assume that because it's undercapitalized it wants to increase it's regulatory capital ratio from 6% to 7%. Here's what I'm going to do next. I'm going to take the expected loans outstanding next year, $150 million, multiply it by 7%, my regulartory capital ratio, and I'm going to come up with the regulatory capital of $10.5 million next year. That's going to be my book equity next year. I'm at $6 million right now, so I have to increase my book equity from $6 million to $10.5 million over the next year.

What is the easiest way to do this? If you're actually a profitable bank, making more than $4.5 million in net income, here's all you need to do. All you need to do is retain enough earnings to get to $10.5 million. But let's say that you have only $3 millon in net income. Even if you retained 100% of your net income, that's going to get you from $6 million to $9 million. You're saying, where am I going to get the extra million and a half? But you're a bank that will have to issue new equity next year.

So when you look at banks, there are two things you should look at to decide whether these banks should be issuing more equity or less equity in the future. The first is how quickly are they growing; fast growing banks will need more regulatory capital and more new equity in the future. Second, you have to look at how under-capitalized or over-capitalized the bank is, relative regulatory capital requirements and relative to what the bank itself might feel it needs to hold as capital. If you have a under-capitalized bank, you will need to issue more equity. So when you think about financing mix for a bank, rather than think in terms of debt and equity, think in terms of book equity and regulatory capital.

## Why Optimal Debt Ratios Vary: Four Drivers

In fact, let's talk about why our optimal debt ratios vary so widely across the companies we've looked at. Let me remind you that the optimal debt ratio I got for Disney was 40%, for Tata Motors was 20%. For Baidu it was 10%. For Valley it was 30%, and you can already see that the debt ratio has raised from 10% to 40%. In fact, when you apply this approach, there are some companies, even money-making companies, the optimal debt ratio can be 0%. And there are other companies at the other end of the spectrum where your optimal debt ratio might be 80% to 90%. You're saying, how come?

Here's the first factor. Tell me what your marginal tax rate is. Remember that the biggest benefit of borrowing money is a tax benefit. The higher your marginal tax rate, the greater your optimal debt ratio is going to be. In fact, to illustrate this, here's what I did. I took my five companies, non-financial service companies, and changed my marginal tax rate from 0% to 50%. Notice that at a 0% tax rate, that the optimal debt ratio for every one of my companies become 0%. So let's make that a strong conclusion. If you pay no taxes, if you have zero tax rate, debt cannot help you. It can only hurt you. As the tax rate rises, your optimal debt ratio will go up. That is why if you're a company that is incorporated the US, and claims your income in the US, and is taxed in the US tax rate, you will borrow more money than an Irish company with a 12.5% tax rate.

The second factor that drives your optimal debt ratio is how much money you make as a company. Because ultimately, the cost-to-capital approach is based on how much you can afford to borrow, and that's driven by how much you generate as earnings. The more a company generates in earnings as a percentage of it's market value, the more it can afford to borrow. I have a simple ratio that I use to compute how much a company can afford to borrow. I look at the operating income, the EBIT, that I use to drive my optimal debt ratio as a percentage of my enterprise value. So we look at Disney, at the operating income. The operating income is about 7.5% of enterprise value. In contrast, take a look at Baidu. It's a young growth company, and because it's market value of equity is so high, it's EBIT is only 3.18% of it's operating income, of it's enterprise value. If you look at that difference, that difference is going to tell you why the optimals are different for the two companies. The higher your operating income is as a percent of your enterprise value, the higher your optimal debt ratio will be as a company.

Let's turn to the third factor. The riskier you are as a company, the less you should be able to borrow. And this shows up in your analysis in two places. The first is, as an unlevered beta, risky businesses has higher unlevered betas. The second place it would show up is in the ratings you attach to this company for any given interest coverage ratio. Risky companies, as they borrow money, will see two impacts. The first is their beta will climb much more quickly than safer companies. And second is, their rating would drop off much more quickly than for a safer company. So when you do this analysis, you should expect to see the optimal debt ratio below other riskier companies.

Which brings me to the final factor. This is the only macro factor that seems to drive optimal debt ratios. Remember the choice you're facing. You can either borrow or you could raise equity. There is a price of risk in the equity market, and that's the equity risk premium. And there's a price of risk in the barn market, and that's a default spread. If you look at this graph, I have actually tracked what the price of risk is in both markets across time. And there have been periods where one market has been cheap relative to the other. For instance, take the late '90s. The equity risk premium was low, and default spreads were high. If you're a company deciding to raise financing in '98 and '99, you'd be much better off issuing equity because the price of risk in that market was low. You go to the early part of the next decade, you see the price of risk in the equity market stays high, but the price of risk in the barn market, the default spread drops off. If you're a company raising financing in 2005 or 2006, you're much more likely to use debt.

## Summary

So in summary, your optimal debt ratios will function at three variables specific to your company: your tax rate, your capacity to generate cash flows, and your riskiness as a company, and one macro economic factor. Ultimately, they all show up in the optimal debt ratio you have for your company. So you ready? Let's assume you computed the optimal debt ratio of your company in the change in value.
