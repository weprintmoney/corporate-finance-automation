# Session 23 Part 1 — Designing the Right Type of Financing

So now that we've talked about the right financing mix for a company, let's turn to the other half of the financing principle: what's the right type of financing for your company?

## The Driving Principle: Match Debt to Assets

To address the question of what the right kind of financing is for your company, I'm going to give you a very simple example to set up the process. Let's suppose you're looking at a company whose value increases and decreases over time for lots of different reasons, mostly macroeconomic. This company is going out to borrow money, and let's assume it's looking at two different ways it can finance its business.

In the first, it borrows money whose value remains the same no matter what happens to the company. So you see that first graph where you see the debt as a fixed line? That's a company that borrows money where the value of debt remains invariant to whatever happens to the value the company. That company gets into trouble every time the value of the business drops below the debt. Technically, it's bankrupt at least a couple of times over the period that we're looking at this company.

Now, let's suppose the same company decides instead of borrowing money, where the value the debt remains the same no matter what happens to the company, the value of the debt itself changes as the value of the company changes. In what way? As the value of the company goes up, the value of the debt goes up. As the value of the company goes down, the value of the debt goes down. Take a look at this company. It actually has borrowed more money than the first company, but it never goes bankrupt.

Therein lies the driving principle for the right kind of financing for your company. The right kind of financing for your company is flexible financing. You're willing to make much more in payments when times are good and much less in payments when times are bad. That's the reason you want to match your debt up to your assets. You want the cash flows in your debt and the cash flows in your business to move as closely together as possible. Or put differently, you'd like to issue debt that behaves like equity, that gives you the flexibility of equity while giving you the tax benefits of debt.

## Five Questions to Customize Your Debt

So let's start on this process of designing the perfect debt for a company. Let's assume you have a company, and you come to me for some advice on the right kind of debt for your company. I'm going to have a series of questions for you. And as you go through these questions, you're going to see why I need the answers to these questions.

The first question I'm going to ask you is about the duration of a typical project you take on. If you're a company like Boeing, and a typical project lasts 25, 30, or 50 years, I think you should be taking long-term debt.

The second question I'm going to ask you is about what currency your cash flows are in. Give me a pie chart of the currencies that your cash flows come in. If 70% of your cash flows come in dollars and 30% in euros, my advice to you is that your debt should be 70% dollar debt and 30% euro debt.

The third question I'm going to ask you is about how inflation affects your cash flows. What I'm I talking about? If you're a company with pricing power, basically you can pass inflation through to your customers, here's what I expect to see. When inflation goes up, your cash flow should go up. When inflation goes down, your cash flow should go down. You're saying, "What do I care?" This is going to determine whether you're a good candidate for issuing floating rate debt or fixed rate debt. In floating rate debt, your interest rates change over time. They go up when a base rate goes up, the prime rate, the T-bond rate, the Libor rate, and they go down when the base rate goes down. The biggest driver of interest rates over time is inflation. So when you shift floating rate debt, here's what's going to happen. If inflation goes up, your interest payments are going to go up. That's not going to be a problem if you've pricing power and your cash flows also go up. So if you're a company who's cash flows go up and down with inflation, i.e, you have pricing power, I would expect you to be a much better candidate for floating rate debt.

The fourth question I'm going to ask you is are you a mature company or a growth company? If you're a growth company, here's what I expect to see. I expect to see low cash flows now and much higher cash flows in the future. You are a much better candidate for convertible debt if you're a growth company, and here's why. On convertible debt, you coupon payments will be low. Why? Because you're giving up a valuable option, a conversion option. And as you grow over time and become a mature company, your convertible debt will become straight debt.

So think about it. Those four questions are going to determine 90% of debt design: how long-term? In what currency? Fixed rate or floating rate? Straight or convertible?

Then I'm going to ask you a final question. Tell me what else affects your cash flows and perhaps, perhaps, I might be able to build it into your debt design. Let me give a simple example. Let's assume you're a gold mining company. The biggest variable affecting your earnings and cash flows over time is gold prices, right? When gold prices go up, your earnings are going to be high. When gold prices go down, your earnings are going to be low. You're saying, "What do I do with that?" Let's assume I could design a bond for you where your coupon rate is tied to gold prices. Your coupon rates are high when gold prices are high. Your coupon payments are low when gold prices are low. That's an advantage for you as a gold mining company. In fact, those bonds exists. They are called gold link bonds. They are part of a family of bonds called commodity bonds. So essentially, I've designed a bond that captures how your cash flows move as a company. That's why debt design has to be customized to a company.

## Tax Check: Don't Be Too Clever

So let's assume you have designed that perfect debt. Think of what you've just done. You've designed debt that looks and acts like equity, right? Because with equity, the biggest advantage you get is your cash flows are truly flexible. They reflect how you're doing as a company.

Your second stop is to make sure you haven't been too clever for your own good. What am I talking about? If you design debt that is too flexible, looks too much like equity, the tax authorities might decide that it is equity, not debt. In which case, you want to cut the main rationale for using debt, which is that you get tax savings from debt. So your second stop is to first make sure that the debt you've designed, the security you've designed is still going to meet all of the requirements of debt. And second, if there are ways you can tweak that security to increase your tax benefits, this is your last chance to do it.

## Keeping Constituent Groups Happy: Trust-Preferred Stock

So let's review where we are. You've customized debt for your company based on its characteristics, you've checked to make sure you get the tax benefits, and perhaps, maximize them by tweaking your debt, now comes the third step. This is a tricky one, so hang in there with me. You want to design debt to keep your different constituent groups happy. And there are three very different groups you got to keep happy. The first is your stockholders. The second is your bondholders, and the third is, if you're regulated, the regulatory authorities.

Let's start with your bondholders. The people who've already lent you money don't want you to lend any more money. So when you issue new financing, those bondholders want you to issue equity because it makes them feel safer. Your equity investors, on the other hand, know that when you issue equity, you're increasing the number of shares. When you increase the number of shares, the dilution bogeyman is going to come out of the closet. So stockholders would prefer that you borrow money and give them the benefits of leverage. And regulatory authorities, especially with financial service firms, clearly want you to issue equity.

So in a sense, you'd like to keep all three groups happy. And if you could, here's what you'd like to do. You'd like to issue a security that looks like equity to your bondholders, looks like debt to stockholders, and looks like equity, again, to your regulatory authorities. You're saying, "What chance do I have of pulling that off?" Well, people try. Let me give you one example. It's a security called trust-preferred stock that was concocted or created about 30 years ago. Here's how trust-preferred stock was designed, and as I describe it, think about where you would put it in the debt and equity spectrum.

Trust-preferred stock has a fixed dividend that's set upfront. That fixed dividend is tax deductible. And if you fail to make that dividend, you have to turn over voting rights to the trust-preferred stockholders. Fixed payment, tax-deductible, loss of control, looks like debt to me, right? But rather those coups that bankers often boast about, the banker who created this particular security managed to get it classified as equity by the ratings agencies. So think of what you're doing. You're borrowing money when you issue trust-preferred because you get all of the benefits of debt. And while you're borrowing money, the ratings agency is actually giving you a higher rating as you borrow more money. It sounds too good to be true, right? But for a while, companies were able to pull this off with the help of their bankers. In fact, if you were a banker in the early 1990s, you'd have been selling a lot of trust-preferred stock because you'd be making more money on trust-preferred than on traditional financing securities.

So now let's talk about what kind of borrower might be suited for trust-preferred stock. Remember, in effect, you're borrowing money, but you're getting the benefit of a higher rating as you go along. You could try to get over-levered clients to issue trust-preferred, but that would be dangerous, right? Because if they're over-levered, that tells me they're already having trouble making their interest payments. But you're an under-levered company, a company that has debt capacity that it's holding back because it has a rating constraint. In other words, it does not want its rating to drop below A. You see the advantage of issuing trust-preferred? You're in effect, using your debt capacity without violating your rating constraint.

## Sugarcoating for Bondholders and Avoiding Market Mistakes

So we've designed the perfect debt. We've got the tax okay. We've kept these different groups as happy as we can conceivably can, now you get ready to issue your debt, and you realize that nobody will buy your bonds. You're saying, "Why not?" For one or two reasons. Either you have no history in the bond market and nobody trusts you, or you have a history in the bond market, and a bad history, and nobody trusts you. You have to sugarcoat your debt. You have to give bondholders some protection.

We talked about Nabisco at the start of this class, a company that went out and borrowed money, and while borrowing money made its existing bondholders worse off. Post-Nabisco bondholders did not want to expose themselves to that kind of risk. So here's what you saw. You saw put clauses put into bonds, where bondholders could put their bonds back to the company and get their face value back. You saw a convertible debt. Think of this as sugarcoating your bonds so your bondholders feel more comfortable.

We're almost home. You've designed the perfect debt. You got the tax okay. You got your different groups at least on your side. Bondholders are being soothed by adding special clauses. You get ready to issue debt. My final note on this is make sure you haven't locked in market mistakes that work against you. What am I talking about? Let's assume you've been rated BBB, but that you should really been rated AA. In other words, the ratings agencies have underrated you. If you issue long-term bonds today, even if those are the right type of bonds for you given the projects you have, you might be locking in a market mistake that works against you and paying too high an interest rate. So if there are market mistakes that work against you, perhaps you might want to wait. Issue some bridge financing, try to convince the ratings agencies to fix a mistake, and then issue your debt.

That's the process by which you design the perfect financing. So if you look at the sequence of steps we went through to get to the perfect financing, it started with the company, looked at the tax code, looked at the different groups within the company and what they might want out of this financing, and then closed up by looking at what the market might accept in your bond offering. It's a sequential process, and if you can follow it, I think you can design financing for almost any type of company.
