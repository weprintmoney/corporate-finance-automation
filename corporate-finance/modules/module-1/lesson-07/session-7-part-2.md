# Session 7 Part 2 — Company Equity Risk Premiums and Implied ERP

## From Country Risk Premiums to Company Risk Premiums

So now that we have a way of estimating equity risk premium for individual countries, let's move from estimating risk premiums of countries to estimating risk premiums for individual companies. In this process, you're going to see why it matters so much what the risk premium is in different parts of the world.

So let me start my analysis with Disney in November of 2013. I think we'd all agree that Disney is a U.S. company. It's a quintessential U.S. company, right? That said though, it gets 82% of its revenues in the U.S. and 18% from the rest of the world, about 12% from Europe, about 6% from the Asia-Pacific region, and the rest from Latin America. You think, "So what?" Even though Disney is a U.S. company, I'm not going to give it the equity risk premium for the U.S. Remember the equity risk for the U.S. is 5.5%. What I did instead was I looked at how much of Disney's revenues come from each part of the world and took a weighted average. The weights, of course, add up to 100%. My weighted average equity risk premium by region is 5.76%.

Incidentally, you might wonder how I get the equity risk premium for an entire region. I didn't mention this when I showed you the global map of equity risk premiums, but the equity risk premium for Asia is a weighted average of the equity risk premiums of the countries in Asia. Weighted by what? Weighted by GDPs. China is weighted a lot more, as it should be, than Vietnam. So I have a weighted average equity risk premium by region. And based on Disney's revenues in the different parts of the world, my estimate of the equity risk premium for Disney is 5.76%, U.S. company with international operations.

## Applying the Method Across the Sample: Bookscape, Vale, Tata Motors, Baidu, Deutsche Bank

Let me look at the rest of my sample. We started with my easy company. Bookscape, I told you, was a New York-based bookstore. It gets revenues from only New York. The U.S., I use the U.S. risk premium.

Vale's a Brazilian company, but here's a surprise. It's a Brazilian company, but its biggest market is China. The weighted average equity risk premium for Vale reflects where it sells its iron ore, and that weighted average of 7.38% reflects where Vale does business, not where it's incorporated.

Tata Motors is an Indian company, but here again, take a look at the breakdown. Because Tata Motors acquired Jaguar Land Rover in 2009, it acquired a global presence. And that global presence shows up as revenues from different parts of the world. The weighted average risk premium for Tata Motors is 7.19% and it reflects where Tata Motors does business and not just the Indian equity risk premium.

When you look at Baidu, it gets all of its revenues in China. So that was an easy one. I used the Chinese equity risk premium.

And then I got to Deutsch Bank. When you look at banks, banks have no revenues. It's impossible to think about revenues for a bank. So what I looked at was where Deutsch Bank's assets were, and based on those assets, I took a weighted average. For every one of these companies, I'm looking past the country of incorporation to where they do business.

Incidentally, when you think about why I've used revenues as my weights, part of the reason is desperation. It's the only item that companies consistently break down geographically. I could have used EBITDA or EBIT, but I'm a little concerned about trusting accountants on those numbers. There is another choice I could have used, and I do this for natural resource companies. If I have to estimate the equity risk premium for Royal Dutch Shell, an oil company, rather than think about where it sells its oil, I'm going to look at where it produces its oil. My equity risk premium could be based on where you do production. So be flexible. Revenues are one way to do it, but there might be others.

## Why I Shifted to Implied Equity Risk Premiums

Now, let me back up again and talk a little bit about why I've shifted almost entirely to implied equity risk premiums in corporate finance. Prior to 2008, I used to compute the equity risk premium at the start of every year and use it through the entire year in markets like the U.S. And if you push me, my defense would have been, "Well, it's a developed market, how much can equity risk premiums change during the course of a year?" The answer came to me between September 12th of 2008 and January 1st of 2009 when I actually computed the implied equity risk premium for the U.S. day-by-day.

Take a look at this graph at the start of the crisis. Now the day before Lehman goes bankrupt, September 12th of 2008, the equity risk premium for the U.S. was 4.37%. There was a day in November, two months later, where the equity risk premium for the U.S. jumped to 8%. What did I learn out of this crisis? Never again would I compute the equity risk premium at the start of every year and stick with it through an entire year because risk premiums can change.

To give you a sense of how much equity risk premiums can change, let me take you forward to January 2017. In January 2017, I did exactly what I did in November of 2013. I looked at the S&P 500, I came up with a level of the index, took the cash flows in the last 12 months, and grew them. Exactly the same process. I saw for that discount rate that makes the present value of my cash flows equal to the level of the index. I came up with an expected return of 8.14%. The risk-free rate on that day was 2.45%. The implied equity risk premium on January 1st of 2017 was 5.69%. All through January, when I was doing my corporate finance, that is the equity risk premium that I used. You think, "What about February?" I recomputed the premium in February and March and April. And that is basically how I come up with my base premiums now, I update them monthly.

## Historical Context: Implied ERP Since 1960

Now you might wonder, "Is that 5.69% a high number, a low number, an average number?" Just to give you some perspective on where that number falls, I've computed and graphed the implied equity risk premiums by year for the U.S. going back to 1960.

Take a look at the 60s. Incredibly stable period for U.S. stocks, premium between 3% and 3.5%. When you get to 70s, the premium blows through the top. That's the inflation effect. Then you get one of the great bull markets of all time, starting in '78 going through the end of '99. At the end of '99, the implied premium in the U.S. was 2%. That's a peak of the dot-com boom. The bubble burst, the premium went back up to about 4%. Stayed around 4% until 2008, crisis hits, premium jumps. And since 2008, premiums have become both more volatile and higher than they've been.

The lesson I've learned is you got to be forward-looking and dynamic. As the market changes under you, you can no longer trust in mean reversion in historical risk premiums. You've got to be nimble to compute what the premium is now and build that into your analysis.

## Updated January 2017 Global Map and Applying It to Your Company

If you look at this final graph. This is my updated graph for January 2017 of those risk premiums by country. Same approach as I used for November of 2013, but notice now I've updated my base premium to reflect the premium in the U.S. at the start of 2017. The country risk premiums now reflect the ratings at the start of 2017. And I've even added a list of countries that I call "Frontier markets." Markets like Afghanistan, Syria, where you don't have a rating. I've tried to estimate a premium. I do this because companies operate all over the world. The way I describe it, we live in a world of global companies that, through the accident of history, happen to be incorporated or traded in the U.S., Brazil, or India. We better get use to dealing with operations all over the world.

So when you can look at your company, here's what I'd like you to do. Go to its annual report or its filings and see if you can see where its revenues come from. Most companies, in fact, almost every company I've looked at, gives you some kind of breakdown. Some are better than others, but take whatever you're given. Look at the revenue breakdown by country, get the equity risk premium for each of those countries, or if the revenue breakdowns by region for each of those regions, and take a weighted average. This, to me, is a much better way of thinking about equity risk premium than staying in the country of your incorporation.
