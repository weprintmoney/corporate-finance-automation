# Session 10 Part 2 — Bottom-Up Beta for Disney by Business

So now that we have a breakdown of Disney by business, let's talk about how we go from that breakdown to getting a bottom-up beta. To get a bottom-up beta, here's what I need. I need an unlevered beta for each of these five businesses. So I started the process by looking for publicly-traded companies in each business. It was easier in some businesses than others. For instance, in the movie business, I was able to find other publicly-traded movie companies and use them to come up with an unlevered beta. Let me illustrate that process and then I'll come back and talk about the other businesses that were more difficult to work with.

## Studio Business: Illustrating the Process

In the studio business, I was able to find about a dozen companies. Some are smaller than Disney. In fact, most are smaller than Disney. Many are just movie companies. Some are actually on the fringe of disaster. But essentially, those are the companies I have to use to come up with the beta for the business. These are publicly-traded companies, so I looked up their regression betas. I also looked up their market capitalization, the market value of equity for each company and the total debt. That allowed me to come up with a debt-to-equity ratio for each company. Finally, I looked up how much cash these companies had. And you're going to see why that matters in a couple of minutes.

Here's what I did next. I computed an average beta and an average debt-to-equity ratio, and I already realized I had a problem. And here was the problem. On my debt-to-equity ratio, there were a couple of outliers, companies with debt-to-equity ratios in excess of 500%, which were pushing up the average. So I did what my statistics professor told me to do when I had outliers, I use the median. The median beta that I have across these companies is going to become the beta that I start with. And that median regression beta across these companies is 1.24. The median debt-to-equity ratio across these companies, which I'm going to use to unlever the beta is about 27%. I have a median beta, a median debt-to-equity ratio, and I'm going to assume a tax rate of 40%. That's a marginal tax rate, the tax rate at which these companies get taxed on the last dollar of income.

To give you a sense of what I'm going to do, let me go through the sequence. I start with the median regression beta, 1.24. Remember, regression beta's 11 betas. I take the median debt-to-equity ratio of about 27% and I unlever that regression beta. I end up with an unlevered beta of about 1.07. Almost home, right? Not quite, and here's why. The typical movie company's about 3% cash, 97% movies, and I want a beta for just the movie business. So I do one final adjustment. I take out the effective cash. What does that mean? My unlevered beta 1.07 reflects the beta for a movie company. And if 3% of it is cash, the unlevered beta of just the movie business is going to be about 1.10. That is going to become the unlevered beta that you see me use for the movie business.

## Extending to the Other Four Businesses

I did this for the other four businesses. Now some, as I said, were more difficult than others. For the broadcasting business, to expand my sample, I brought in companies that fed into the broadcasting business. Like what? Let me give you a couple of examples. One company I brought in was King World. This is a company that syndicates TV shows, TV shows like *Jeopardy*, to the broadcasting networks. And it does well when the networks do well, and badly when they do badly. I also brought in Nielsen. Nielsen is a company, of course, that measures ratings in TV shows and essentially makes money off the broadcasting companies. In effect, here's what I'm saying. I'm looking for companies that do well when the broadcasting business does well. I'm going to treat them as comparable companies. By expanding my sample, I end up with a sample size of 26. That's pretty good. It's not as good as 100, but 26 gets me halfway there. With a sample of 26 firms, I should get an estimate abated, but five times more precise than a single regression beta. I come up with an unlevered bata for those companies exactly the way I did for the studio business. I end up with an unlevered beta of 1.03 for the broadcasting business.

For the theme park business, I ran into a problem again. There aren't that many publicly-traded theme parks in the U.S., there are only two. To expand my sample, I went global. I brought in European theme park companies, Asian theme park companies. You think, "But you can't do that." Why not? The average beta across all companies in every region is one. My sample size when I bring in global companies increases to 20, and my unlevered beta for that business is 0.70.

Consumer products and interactive gaming, I had an easier time with. I could find publicly-traded companies and pretty large samples at that. I came for the non-levered beta for those two businesses as well.

## Aggregating to a Disney Unlevered Beta

I'm almost home. I now have an unlevered beta for each of the five businesses I'm in. Now comes the next step. I need to compute an unlevered beta for Disney as a company. And to do that, I need an estimate of value of each business. Now, Disney provides revenues for each business as part of their annual report, so I started with that. Then I added a second component. Remember that sample of studio companies that I showed you? For those companies, here's what I did. I took the market value of equity and total debt that I'd already collected. I subtracted out the cash, which I had also already collected. I came up with an enterprise value for each company. What does that measure? That's what the market's estimate of the value of the operating assets of each company is. I divided that by the revenue of each company and I computed the median value of that enterprise value to revenue multiple across companies. So if you take broadcasting companies, for instance, that multiple is 3.27. I'm going to multiply the revenues that Disney gets from the broadcasting business by that multiple to come up with an estimated value for the broadcasting business. I repeat this process for the other four businesses.

I now have my estimated values for the businesses. Those become the weights that I use to come up the unlevered beta for Disney. And based on my estimates, Disney is 49% broadcasting, about 34% theme parks, and the rest of the businesses make up the remaining 17%. The unlevered beta that I get using those weights is 0.9239. That is my unlevered beta for Disney's operating assets. You think, "What do you mean operating assets?" Disney does have $3.9 billion in cash. I haven't counted that yet. And since the beta of cash is zero, cash is invested in liquid and riskless investments, the unlevered beta for Disney as a company is a little lower. It's only about 0.90 because of Disney's cash balance.

## Levering Up: Debt-to-Equity by Division

Last step in the process, is I'm going to take those unlevered betas and come up with levered betas. To go from unlevered to levered betas, I needed debt-to-equity ratio. And that debt-to-equity ratio has to be a market value. And here I run into a problem. I can tell you what the market value of Disney as a company is and the total debt that Disney has. And that debt-to-equity ratio I can use for the entire company. The debt-to-equity ratio I have for the entire company is 13.1%, and I can use that to come up with a levered beta for Disney as a company.

You think, "We're done." Well, I'd like to come up with levered betas for each of its divisions. And to make that judgment, here's what I did. I took the total debt that Disney had and I allocated it across the divisions. Why allocate it? Because Disney doesn't tell me how much each division borrows, and I came up with a total debt by division. I ended up with a debt-to-equity ratio for each division. That debt-to-equity ratio is what I used to come up with a levered beta for each business. Notice that the debt-to-equity ratios vary widely. They go from about 10% for the broadcasting business to 117% for the consumer products business, and my levered beta's reflecting.

I'm almost home. I have the levered beta for each of the five businesses, a levered beta for Disney, and I use that beta to come up with the cost of equity for the company and for each of its five businesses. My estimate of the cost of equity for Disney as a company is 8.5%, but my cost of equity for the divisions vary from 7% for the theme park business, all the way up to almost 12% for the interactive gaming business.

## Why Business-Level Hurdle Rates Matter

Now, you might wonder why I took that extra work of estimating the betas and the cost of equity for each business. Here's why I need it. Let's assume that I have a movie project where I have a return in equity of 9.5%. Should I take it? It's higher than the cost of equity for the company, but it's lower than the cost of equity for the movie business. Think about it for a moment. You might be tempted to say, "It's Disney's capital, I should be using Disney's cost of equity." But that's not quite right. Because the movie business is riskier than Disney as a company, you should demand much more of it, a higher hurdle rate. That's why I need the cost of equity for the movie business. And since that number is 9.9%, I shouldn't be taking this project. If you are a multi-business company, you have no choice but to estimate your hurdled rates by business, because whether you take projects in those businesses will depend on that hurdle rate.

That was a lot to pack in into a few minutes, but here's what I'd like you to try to do. If you have broken your company down by business, I'd like you to estimate a beta and a cost of equity for each business. That'll require doing what I just did, coming up with companies in each business, coming up with an unlevered beta, and adjusting that beta for the debt-to-equity ratio. If you can allocate debt across the businesses and come up with different debt-to-equity ratios, great. If not, just use the debt-to-equity ratio of the company. You're almost there.
