# Session 12 Part 2 — From Ratings to Cost of Debt

So let's move from having a rating to getting to a cost of debt. In this part of the session, I'd like to focus on both actual ratings and synthetic ratings.

## Actual Ratings to Cost of Debt

Let's start with the easy route first. For three of my companies, I have an actual rating. Disney has a single A rating, Deutsche has a single A, Vale has A-minus rating. I'm going to try to convert those actual ratings into default spreads. With a single A rating, in November of 2013, the default spread I got was 1%. I got that by looking at publicly-traded bonds with an A rating and the spreads that they were trading at relative to a risk-free rate.

Now, here's what I'm going to do to get a cost of debt. I take that 1%, that default spread and added onto my risk-free rate. In the case of Disney, that risk-free rate was 2.75%. Because I'm doing everything in U.S. dollars, I come up with a pre-tax cost of debt of 3.75%. In the case of Deutsche Bank, I have a little more work to do. I need a euro risk-free rate. And if that euro risk-free rate is 1.75%, I add 1% to it, I come up with a cost of debt of 2.75%. The case of Vale, with an A-minus rating, the default spread was slightly larger, 1.3%. And because I'm doing everything in U.S. dollars, I add the 1.3% to the 2.75% risk-free rate, I come up with the cost of debt of 4.05%. With the actual ratings, I have a cost of debt for each of these companies.

Incidentally, if for Vale you decide to do everything in nominal reals, not a big deal. Take the 4.05% cost of debt you get in U.S. dollar terms and either just add the 7%, which is the additional inflation you see in the real. Or if you want to do it right, compound it with that 7% additional inflation rate. You'll come up with the cost of debt of 11.19% in nominal real terms. So if you have a rating, estimating the cost of debt is fairly trivial.

## Synthetic Ratings via Interest Coverage

You think, "What if I don't have a rating?" Well then, you have to attach a rating to a company, and my suggestion is to keep it simple. You're not a fixed income trader, you're doing cost of capital and all you want is a rough estimate of the rating and the cost of debt. I actually build my rating of one ratio. It's called the interest coverage ratio. The interest coverage ratio is the operating income earnings before interest and taxes divided by interest expense. Think about this like a lender. If you're lending money to a company, you want that number to be as high as possible, because the higher it is, the more buffer you have.

I compute the interest coverage ratios for my four publicly-traded companies that are non-financial service companies and for Bookscape, the private business. I divide the operating income by the interest expense. And if you look at the ratios, you can see the spread in those numbers. With Disney, my interest coverage ratio is 22.57. For every dollar in interest expense, I've $22.57 in operating income. What does that tell me? If I'm lending money to Disney, I feel pretty secure. I've a big buffer. So if you look across companies, you can see that Tata Motors has the lowest interest coverage rate at 4.51, and Disney has the highest interest coverage ratio.

By itself, interest coverage ratios don't give me costs of debt. So if I can convert my interest coverage ratios into ratings, I'm home free, right? So here's what I'm going to do. I have a lookup table I've created that I update once every two years. In this lookup table, if you give me your interest coverage ratio, no matter what it is, I should be able to convert to a rating. But I give you a different rating depending on whether you're a small company or a large company, a safe company or a risky company. And I'm going to use some very simple cut-offs. If your market cap is greater than 5 billion, I'm going to assume you're a large company. If you are in an emerging market, I'm going to treat you as a risky company. I know those are very broad categorizations, but let's use those to come up with ratings for the five companies that we have interest coverage ratios for.

Let's start with Disney. Large market cap company in a developed market. It's 22.57% interest coverage ratio gives me a triple-A rating. Vale, large market cap company in an emerging market. Well, it's 11.67 interest coverage ratio, using the small cap or risky segmentation, gives me a double-A rating. Tata Motors, 4.51 interest coverage ratio. Large market cap company in an emerging market, 4.51 interest coverage ratio gives me an A-minus rating. Baidu, small market cap company in an emerging market. That interest coverage ratio of 23.72 gives me a triple-A rating. Finally Bookscape, private business. Of course, it's a small company. I use the risky company segment, I end up with a 5.16 interest coverage ratio giving me an A-minus rating. I've converted my interest coverage ratios into ratings, and those ratings can be used with default spreads to get a cost of debt for each of these companies.

## Actual vs Synthetic Ratings Comparison

Now, when you compute a synthetic rating for a company that does have an actual rating, it's natural to ask, how off was I with my synthetic rating? And I decide to ask that question of my three companies. With Disney, if you remember, the actual rating was single A, my synthetic rating was triple-A. And there's a reason for that. Rating agencies do not rate companies based on just last year's operating income, because operating income can go up and down. So Disney's high rating, part of it, can be explained by the fact… The triple-A rating can be explained by the fact that in the most recent year they had lots of operating income. The rating agencies also factor in what they call subjective factors, and those subjective factors might also explain some of that difference.

With Vale, my synthetic rating is double A. My actual rating is A-minus. And there, there is a very simple reason why. Vale's a Brazilian company. You think, "So what?" Well, rating's agencies take into account what market you're in when they give you a rating. And this might not sound fair to you, but if you're in an emerging market, they will give you a lower rating than if you are in a developed market. And finally, for Deutsche Bank which had a single A rating, I didn't compute a synthetic rating, I could do no comparison. So if your synthetic ratings are different from your actual ratings, there are usually good reasons. But if you have an actual rating, don't go looking for trouble. Just use the actual rating.

## After-Tax Cost of Debt

So now we're ready to compute the cost of debt. For those companies that have actual ratings, I'm going to stick with the actual rating. For those companies that do not have actual ratings like Bookscape, I'm going to use a synthetic rating. The synthetic ratings and the actual ratings get converted to default spreads. Added to the risk-free rate, I come up with the pre-tax cost of debt for each of my companies.

But remember, the tax code is tilted in favor of debt. When you make interest payments, that's tax deductible. So when you borrow money at 5% with that tax benefit built in, you're actually borrowing money at a much lower rate. To compute that tax benefit, I multiply my pre-tax cost of debt by one minus the tax rate. And what tax rate should I use? Here's a simple rule. Interest saves you taxes at the margin. What do I mean by that? If you have a $100 million in interest expenses and a $billion in income, you save taxes on the last hundred million dollars of your income. So what I do is I use the marginal tax rate for each of these companies. That varies depending on the country you're in, and it ranges from 36.1% for Disney to much lower tax rates in countries outside the U.S. I multiply the cost of debt pre-tax by one minus the tax rate. I end up with an after-tax cost of debt.

For Disney, that after-tax cost of debt is 2.4%. That's a 3.75% net of the tax benefit. For Deutsche, that after-tax cost of debt is 1.94%. For Vale, that after-tax cost of debt is 2.67%. For Bookscape, even though it's a private company, the same rules apply. Its after-tax cost of debt is 2.43%.

For Tata Motors, there's one extra step I need to take. Because I'm using a synthetic rating for Tata Motors, I'm coming up with a default spread for the company. But to get a total cost of debt for the company, I have to add a default spread for the country. Why? This is unfair again. But because Tata Motors is an Indian company, it carries that extra burden of risk. So to get to its cost of debt, I start with the risk-free rate in rupees, 6.57%, and add two default spreads. One for Tata Motors, based on its synthetic rating, and one for India, based on its default risk. The total cost of debt I end up with is a rupee cost of debt for Tata Motors that reflects both its company risk and its country risk. And the after-tax cost of debt for Tata Motors is 6.5%.

## Process Recap and Next Steps

So sequentially, start with the rating if you have one or come up with the rating if you don't, convert that rating into a default spread, add the default spread to the risk-free rate, multiply by one minus the tax rate, you got your after-tax cost of debt. Now, the default spreads you use will change over time. The default spreads you saw for my companies were as of November of 2013. I update this spreads once every year because I know that if I stay with old spreads, I'm going to get myself into trouble. So when you're doing your cost of debt, see if you can get the most updated default spreads you can for your company given its rating because you want a cost of debt as of right now.

So here's what I'd like you to do next. Now that you've defined how much debt your company has, assuming it does have debt, you want to come up with the cost of debt. If you have an actual rating, your job is pretty simple. If you have a synthetic rating, you have a little more work to do. But both cases you convert that rating into a default spread, come up with the cost of debt for your company, and complete the process by looking at what the after-tax cost of debt is.
