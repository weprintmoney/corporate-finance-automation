# Session 11 Part 1 — Bottom-Up Betas Across Companies

In the last session, we looked at how to estimate the beta for Disney by breaking it down to businesses and building up to a bottom-up beta. In this session, I'd like to extend that concept to the other companies that are part of my lab experiments. So let's think about the biggest selling points for using a bottom-up beta. They're more precise than a single regression beta, they're more forward-looking, and they're more dynamic. So given that rationale, I should be using a bottom-up beta for not just Disney but every company in my sample. So here's what I'm going to do next. I'm going to take each of my companies and go through the process I did with Disney, but in less detail.

## Vale — Multi-Business Bottom-Up Beta

Let's start with Vale. Vale is a mining company, but it breaks itself down into four businesses. One is metals and mining, the second is iron ore, the third is fertilizers, and the fourth is logistics. It gives you revenues by business. And here's what I did. I looked for other publicly-traded companies in each of these businesses. And here, I didn't even bother looking at just Brazilian companies. Why? My sample size would be far too small if I focused just on Brazilian companies. So I looked at global companies in each of these groups. And when you look at the sample sizes, they reflect the advantage of going global. I get really big sample sizes. I look up the median beta, I adjust the beta for the debt-to-equity ratio, I adjust for cash, exactly what I did with Disney. I end up with an unlevered beta for each of these four businesses. I take the revenues that Vale provided me, and just as I did for Disney, I multiply those revenues by the EV to Sales Ratio. The same ratio I used to adjust revenues to value, I come up with values for each of the four businesses.

Based on my estimates, at least, here's what Vale looks like to me. It's about 76% iron ore, and the rest of the businesses make up the remaining 24%. I take a weighted average of my unlevered betas. And the weighted average I come up with is 0.844. That is my unlevered beta for Vale's operating assets. And just as I did for Disney, I can come up with the levered beta for each of the four businesses. I thought about allocating debt just like I did for Disney across the four businesses. But unlike Disney where the businesses are diverse and carry very different debt ratios, these are all infrastructure businesses that require debt. So I use the same debt-to-equity ratio, Vale's debt-to-equity ratio which I was able to estimate for each of the four businesses. I ended up with a levered beta for each business. That's it. Take the risk-free rate plus the beta times the risk premium. And remember, the equity risk premium for Vale reflects where it does business. I've got a cost of equity for Vale. I did do things in U.S. dollars for Vale because it's a commodity company and I have a U.S. dollar cost of equity for each of the four businesses. And I've a U.S. dollar cost of equity for Vale as a company. Same process that I did for Disney, just different businesses.

## Switching Currencies with an Inflation Differential

Incidentally, if at this stage in the process you change your mind. Change your mind in what sense? Let's assume you decide that you want to analyze Vale not in U.S. dollars, but in nominal reals. You could do everything from scratch. Start with the new risk-free rate and build up, but there is a simpler way for you to adjust each of those costs of equity to a nominal real cost of equity. Remember the trick we talked about when we talked about risk-free rates? That the differences between risk-free rates came from differences in expected inflation? If you can estimate an expected inflation rate in U.S. dollars and an expected inflation rate in nominal reals, you can adjust each of those dollar cost of equity very simply. For instance, let's assume that the inflation rate in U.S. dollars is 2% and the inflation rate in nominal reals is 9%. That's about a 7% differential. If you're in a hurry, just add 7% to each of the dollar cost of equity and you will have a nominal real cost of equity. Of course, if you wanted to do it right, you have to allow for the compounding effect of inflation. It gives you slightly higher numbers, but the net effect is still the same. Your nominal real cost of equity will be higher than your nominal dollar cost of equity because of that 7% inflation differential. That's Vale.

## Tata Motors and Baidu — Single-Business Companies

Let's think about the bottom-up betas and cost of equity for Tata Motors and Baidu. One of the nice things about both of these companies is they're both single business companies, at least from my perspective. I view Tata Motors as an automobile company. As with Vale, I'm not going to stay local. Looking at just the Indian automobile companies will not give me enough companies in my sample. So again, I went global. That actually does make sense because a big chunk of Tata Motors value comes from Jaguar Land Rover. I looked at 76 publicly-traded automobile companies across the globe. I came up with an unlevered beta across those companies. Doing exactly what I did with the Disney businesses, start with the regression beta, unlever, clean up for cash. That unlevered beta was 0.86. Then I used Tata Motors debt-to-equity ratio which was about 41.41% and the tax rate in India, 32.45%. And with those tax rates and that debt-to-equity ratio, I end up with a levered beta for Tata Motors of 1.10. Again, I'm almost home free. Because I want the cost of equity in rupee terms, I start with the rupee risk-free rate, 6.57% plus the beta that I just estimated of 1.10 multiplied by the equity risk premium for Tata Motors based on where it does business. My cost of equity in rupee terms for Tata Motors is 14.49%.

Repeating the process for Baidu. I look at the unlevered beta for online advertising companies because, after all, Baidu as a search engine gets its revenues from online advertising. I use that unlevered beta of 1.30 in conjunction with Baidu's tax rate and Baidu's debt-to-equity ratio to come up with a levered beta of 1.36, which in turn used with the renminbi risk-free rate. And the equity risk premium for Baidu will give me a cost of equity in renminbi of 12.91%. By now you should have a sense of the process. Break a company down into businesses. If it's in the single business, still go on. Come up with an unlevered beta for the business and then adjust for the debt-to-equity ratio in that business.

## Deutsche Bank — Why Banks Break the Levering Process

Let's see one final example. I wanted a beta and the cost of equity for Deutsche Bank. Deutsche Bank, from my perspective again, is in two businesses, commercial banking and investment banking. I went and looked up other commercial banks and other investment banks, came up with an average levered beta for each of those two businesses. Now, remember up till now when we've come up with a levered beta for a business, we've then unlevered using the debt-to-equity ratio for that business and re-levered using the debt-to-equity ratio of your company. I'm not going to do that for Deutsche, and here is why. Estimating the debt-to-equity ratio for a bank is not just pointless, it's frustrating because debt to a bank is not a source of capital, it's raw material. Levering and unlevering is a process I can't even go through, so I'm going to cheat. And here's how I'm going to cheat. I'm going to assume that because of regulatory constraints, banks have roughly similar debt-to-equity ratios. What that allows me to do is use the average levered beta for commercial banks and the average levered beta for investment banks as the levered beta for Deutsche's divisions. I use those betas, and in conjunction with the euro risk-free rate and the equity risk premium for Deutsche, I end up with a euro cost of equity for Deutsche's commercial banking business and Deutsche's investment banking business, and Deutsche overall. Notice that the cost of equity for investment banking is much higher than the cost of equity of commercial banking, and that should make sense. Investment banking might be a more lucrative business, but it's also a riskier business. So I have a levered beta and the cost of equity for Deutche's investment banking and commercial banking businesses and Deutsche as a company.

## Wrap-Up

So at this stage, if you want to estimate a bottom-up beta for a publicly-traded company, you know what you have to do. You have to get those unlevered betas by business, you have to get the weights, and if you want to come up with levered betas, the debt-to-equity ratios for each business. You now have a way of not having to use regression betas to come up with costs of equity and then hurdle rates for individual companies.
