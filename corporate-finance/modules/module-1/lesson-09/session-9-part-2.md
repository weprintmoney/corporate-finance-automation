# Session 9 Part 2 — The Three Determinants of Beta

In the last session, remember all those betas I listed out, Bulgari, Microsoft, G.E., and I told a story with each company trying to explain its beta. Here's what I'd like to do. I'd like to gather all those stories together and bring them together into the three determinants, the three variables. And there are only three that determine the beta for a company.

## Determinant 1: The Business You Are In

So let's start with the first determinant of betas. Tell me what you do, tell me what kind of product or service you provide and I can estimate what kind of beta you should have, a high beta or a low beta.

Let me back up and explain. If you're a cyclical company, I would expect you to have a higher beta than if you're a non-cyclical company, and here's why. Betas don't measure overall risk, they measure how you move with the market, and cyclical companies will tend to move more with the market than non-cyclical companies. That was the distinction I used to make until 20 years ago, but life has changed. Today we live in a world where tech companies and service companies dominate the market. So if you ask me, "Is Facebook a cyclical company?" I have no idea.

So let me give you my second contrast. The more discretionary your product or service is as a company, the higher your beta will be as a company. Let me explain what I mean by discretionary. If your customers can live without a product, they can delay buying it, they can defer buying it, I would expect you to have a higher beta than if you cannot do it.

And I'll give you a very simple sector to breakdown using this distinction. Say you take the retail sector and ask me, "What kind of beta would you expect a retail company to have?" Before I answer the question I want to ask you what kind of retail company are we talking about. And let's talk about four very different types of retail companies. The first is a luxury retailer, a Gucci or a Tiffany's. The second is a department store, a Macy's or a J.C. Penny's. The third is a discount retailer, a Walmart or a Costco. And the fourth is a grocery store.

If I asked you to think about, based on what we just talked about, which of these companies would have the highest beta and which will have the lowest one, think about it purely in terms of discretionary and non-discretionary. You'd expect the luxury retailers to have the highest betas, right? Their products and services are the most discretionary. And you'd expect grocery stores to have the lowest betas. Why? Because you can't live without food, even in bad times. Discount retailers will be close to grocery stores, a little higher than grocery stores, but still low. And department stores will fall somewhere between the luxury retailers and the discount retailers.

So there you go. When you look at your company, the first question you have to ask yourself is, "How discretionary is my product or service?" Incidentally if you remember Econ 101, you know what we're talking about? We're talking about elasticity of demand. The more elastic the demand for your product or service, the higher your beta. So that's your first stop. What kind of business am I in? What product or service do I provide?

## Determinant 2: Operating Leverage (Cost Structure)

Here's your second stop. Tell me something about your cost structure. The greater the proportion of your costs that are fixed costs, the higher your beta will be. And think of why. If you have lots of fixed costs in good times, you tend to make lots of money, in bad times, you tend to lose lots of money. Your mood swings become much more manic and much more depressive. You're a manic depressive, you have a lot of fixed cost and that manifests itself as a much higher beta. The higher your fixed cost, the more you have as an operating leverage, the higher your beta will be as a company. So sectors with high fixed costs, sectors like telecommunications, sectors like the airline business, where most of your costs are fixed, you'd expect to see much higher betas.

So that's your second stop. But here, let me take a tangent. If you ask me how much operating leverage your company has, the answer at first sight is pretty easy. You take a fixed cost and you divide by a variable cost. Easy, right? But try this in a real company. You take an income statement for a company, I challenge you to tell me how much of a company's costs are fixed and how much are variable because that's not how accountants breakdown costs. There are costs of goods sold, there are SGNA expenses. You could try to guess what's fixed and what's variable, but it's tough to do.

So I have a simple suggestion if you want to get an estimate of operating leverage. Look at the percentage change in operating income in your company for a given percentage change in revenues. And here's why. If you have lots of fixed cost, small changes in revenues would translate into big changes in operating leverage. Obviously, doing this in one year can be dangerous.

So what I did for Disney is I went back all the way to 1987 and I computed the percentage change in revenue, 11.79%, and the percentage change in operating income over the same period. You divide the percentage change in operating income, 11.91, by the percentage change in revenue, you come up with a measure of operating leverage of 1.01. And if you want to just focus on the post '96 time period, because Disney did change as a company after acquiring Cap Cities/ABC, the number you get is likely higher.

And your first reaction is, "Well, now that I have this number, what do I do with it?" I computed the average across all entertainment companies and Disney's measures, 1.01 or 1.25, depending on how you measured it, are both lower than that average. I know the difference is small and I want to draw too strong a conclusion, but to the extent that the direction matters, it looks like Disney has less fixed cost than the typical entertainment company and should therefore have a lower beta.

## Determinant 3: Financial Leverage (Debt)

So what business are you in? How much do you have in fixed costs? Which brings me my third and final stop. Tell me how much you've borrowed. Why does it matter? When you borrow money you create a fixed cost you did not have until you borrowed that money. That's interest expenses. You got to make it in good times and you have to make it in bad times. It makes your equity earnings much more volatile and through that, it makes your beta higher.

In fact, to illustrate how much debt affects betas, I'm going to go back and break betas down into two tabs. The first beta I'm going to call an unlevered beta. Think of that as the beta that comes from the business here and in your operating leverage, the first two determinants. And then I'm going to bring in leverage by measuring how much you borrowed through a debt to equity ratio, in market value terms. Here's what's going to happen. As you borrow more money, your levered beta, the beta for your equity will increase relative to your unlevered beta. You could take safe businesses and make them risky to your equity investors by borrowing enough money.

## Unlevering the Regression Beta: Disney Example

One final stop. When we're done with regressions and we came up with a regression beta, was that beta a levered beta or non-levered beta? Think about it for a moment. We never explicitly brought debt into the equation, right? It was a regression of returns in the stock against returns on a market index. The answer is, it's a levered beta, but the debt to equity ratio in that beta was the average debt to equity ratio during the period of the regression.

And that's what I did for Disney. I took the regression beta 1.25 and I computed the debt to equity ratio during the period, 19.44%. And I did something that might strike you as strange. I took out the effective debt from the beta. This is called un-levering a beta. I took the levered beta, 1.25, and I took out the effect of the 19.44% from that beta. Disney's marginal tax rate happened to be 36.1%, and the un-levered beta I come up with for Disney is 1.11. You're saying, "What does that even mean?" Given the businesses that Disney is in and its fixed costs, that's what its beta would be, if it had no debt. By borrowing money, Disney is magnifying that beta.

And here's the other advantage of knowing what that un-levered beta is. Could Disney change its debt equity ratio going forward? Absolutely. In fact in this table, I've computed what Disney's beta would be if its debt to equity ratio went to 11%, to 25%. In fact, I've even computed what the beta would be if you did a leverage buyout of Disney and made its debt to equity ratio 900%. Your beta will go to 7.51. And the last column, I've computed how much leverage is contributing to your beta.

## Bottom Line

Bottom line, when you look at the beta for your stock, remember it's a net effect of all three choices that your company's made. What business is it in? How much operating leverage does it have? How much did it choose to borrow? So once you've got your regression beta, don't just stop there. Look at how much your company borrowed during the period of the regression, compute a debt to equity ratio. And remember it has to be in market value terms. So you're looking at total debt and market equity each period. Then un-lever your beta. That's going to give you at least a rough sense of how risky your company's businesses are. You can have safe businesses with very risky equity.
