# Session 3 Part 2 — Market-Based Solutions and Self-Correction

## The Third Choice: Maximize Stock Price Subject to Constraints

So we've talked about replacing maximizing stock price over the different corporate governance mechanism, but we argued that that comes with a tradeoff. A cost is, that if you get systematic problems in an economy or markets, those systems don't deal with them very well. We also talked about replacing maximizing stock price with a different objective, but those intermediate objectives, revenue growth, market share, come with their own problems. You're saying, "Now, what?"

Let's talk about the third choice you have, which is to stay with the market-based solution. And I'll make a confession. I have a bias here. I want you to make this third choice if I can get you there. And if you decide third choice, here is what you're going to do. You're going to maximize stock prices subject to constraints. Here's what my biggest sales pitch for a market-based solution is, it's self-correcting. What do I mean by self-correcting? Remember those four linkages we talked about, of stockholders who've little power over managers, lenders who lend money and not protected or get ripped off, markets that are light do come up with prices very different from value, and finally, there are social costs and benefits. Each of these linkages, if you let the market play out, will create its own correction.

## Self-Correction Across the Four Linkages

Let's start with the first linkage. We talked about stockholders who've little power over managers and managers putting their interests over stockholder interest, right? Let's assume that stockholders continue to have little power, and managers continue to put their interests over stockholder interests. If you're a stockholder, you're getting angrier and angrier as this process continues, right? There will be a tipping point where if you get angry enough maybe there'll be a blowback. And that blowback can take a couple of forms. In companies where stockholders are angriest with managers, you start to see activists investors pop up. People like Carl Icahn and Bill Ackman, people who catered to the shareholders saying, "Hey those managers don't listen to you, sell your shares to us." In some cases, the anger can build up to a point where if a hostile acquirer approaches the company, you as a shareholder of the company are really willing to sell your shares to that acquirer because you don't like the managers. That's a correction mechanism when managers overreach and put their interest over stockholder interest.

Let's move on to lenders. Lenders, we said, if they lend money to a firm and don't protect themselves will get ripped off. I like to use the example of RJR Nabisco, a well-known consumer product company that in the 1980s essentially went through a leveraged buyout. What's a leveraged buyout? A private entity, in this case, KKR with a group of managers decided to borrow money and take the company private. You're saying, "What's wrong with that?" In the process, they essentially wiped out 22% of the value of bondholders in the company, because the bondholders were not protected. Why were they not protected? Because they didn't get it right in protections when they lent the money. Too late to protect the Nabisco bondholders, but post-Nabisco, you started to see corporate bond markets learn. They started a bonding [SP] clauses where if there were something like a leveraged buyout, a leveraged acquisition, that they were able to put their bonds back to the company and get back what they'd lent the company. Again, it happened too late to protect the Nabisco bondholders, but by putting in those clauses into bonds, bond markets essentially put in the corrective mechanism.

We also talked about how some companies hold back information. How other companies sometimes lie, and how, in return, markets can get irrational and inefficient. That said though, if companies take advantage of markets by lying to them, holding back information, there would be a day of reckoning when the truth comes out. And when the truth comes out, will these companies get punished? Absolutely. But it's worse. Not only will their stock prices drop when the truth comes out but they will stay down. Why? Because the managers will have lost all credibility with markets. Markets won't trust them beyond that point in time. So if you lie to markets, there is a blowback, and that blowback takes a form of long-term loss of value.

And finally, if you create social causes of company, there are consequences, right? Remember what happened with the tobacco companies when they were viewed as bad companies? Bad because they created health costs to society. You could tar them and feather them, and actually, you could tax just tobacco companies and get away with it because nobody came to their defense. If you're a company that creates a lot of social costs, at some point in time, there's going to be a blowback there as well. Left to their own devices, markets figure out ways to punish companies that essentially violate the rules. So let markets play through, and you will often find market-based mechanisms come up with more effective solutions than what you could come up with as a regulator, a government, or a court.

## Disney Case Study: Eisner's Imperial CEO Era

Now, let's take a look at Disney. Disney in 1997, that's when I showed you the board of directors that I described as the worst board of directors ever. It was a company run by an imperial CEO. What's an imperial CEO? This is a CEO who governs by edict, who has complete power. And that's what Michael Eisner was in '97. And he had earned that power by running Disney really well for a decade. Between '97 and 2003, Disney got into a lot of trouble. A lot of trouble that can be traced back to a big decision that Michael Eisner made in '96 to acquire Cap Cities/ABC, and say, "Trust me. I know what I'm doing." Well, it turned out he didn't. So over the following six years, Disney lost about half of its market cap, and shareholders got angrier. In fact, by 2003 or 2004, you could see the anger boiling up, not just in the annual meetings but in the form of a hostile acquisition bid by a company called Comcast. The bid didn't go through, but the die had been cast. Essentially, Comcast said, "Shareholders in this company don't like you, therefore, sell your shares back to us." And remember Roy Disney, the one individual on that list of 17 shareholders of the company that, in 2003, was not in the institution? Well, he'd resigned from the board of directors. And as he resigned with Stanley Gold, the other director who resigned with him, they said they were doing so because Michael Eisner was running the company as an imperial CEO, that he wasn't listening to the board. In effect, troubling was bubbling up to the surface. Eventually, the Comcast bid, the dissatisfaction by stockholders, led Eisner to step down in 2005.

## Disney Under Iger and the Recurrence of Imperial CEO Risk

And when he stepped down, he was replaced by Bob Iger. Bob Iger, in many ways, was the anti-Eisner, a man with a small ego willing to trash shareholders. And he created a board of directors that was much more independent. In 2008, for instance, when you looked at the board of directors for Disney, it was smaller and more independent than it was in either 2003 and 1997. In fact, Iger built on that good work. And by 2011, it could be argued that Disney was now at the top of the ranks in terms of corporate governance, a company with good corporate governance. Well, things started to go bad. In 2011, the board of directors, that now was Iger's board, decided that they wanted to make Iger again CEO and chair. Remember that's what Eisner was doing in 1997. In 2011, Iger announced that he was going to step down. Good sign, right? As CEO steps down, gets replaced by another CEO. Well, the board of directors went back to him and said, "You're too important to the company to step down." So in 2015, Iger reversed this decision and decided to stay on. That created a vacuum in the company and led top executives to leave the company. What's my bottom line? As Iger stayed on as CEO, as his tenure has lengthened, you're starting to see the imperial CEO components creep into how he behaves. Perhaps, perhaps we need 10 years that are limited for CEOs because the longer a CEO stays in power, the more danger there is of power accumulating.

## Assignment: Assess Your Company's Board

So here's what I'd like you to do with your company. Find out who sits on your board of directors. Find out whatever conflicts of interest you can get. For U.S. companies, that is relatively simple to do. There are many foreign companies, though, where it's more difficult to do but it's a start of assessing how much you can trust the managers of company.
