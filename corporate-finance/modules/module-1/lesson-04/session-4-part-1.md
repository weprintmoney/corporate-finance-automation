# Session 4 Part 1 — Risk, Hurdle Rates, and the CAPM Foundations

In this session, we're going to turn to the meat and potatoes part of corporate finance. We're going to start about talking about specifics. In particular, we're going to talk about how risk plays out in hurdle rates.

Now let's set the table. We said the investment principle requires that you come up with a hurdle rate, right? In fact, investment principle specifies the hurdle rate should be a function of the riskiness of the investment you're taking. So let's talk about what that means.

First, when you talk about a hurdle rate you're talking about a rate of return you need to make to break even on an investment, not even to be happy, to break even. And the way we think about hurdle rates, here is how we break it down. We start out by looking at the rate of return you would demand on a guaranteed investment, a riskless investment, and then we had a risk premium for an investment reflecting how much risk we see in that investment and how much higher the return would have to be given that risk. That's the search we're embarking on.

And to be able to do this, we need to be able to answer two questions. First, we need to be specific about risk. What exactly is risk? How do we measure risk? And second, once we've defined and measured risk, we've got to convert that risk into a risk premium. Everything we're going to do in this session is about answering those two questions.

## What Is Risk? Danger Plus Opportunity

So let's start with the first question. What is risk? I tell people that taking a corporate finance class ruins you for life, because your definition of risk becomes almost statistical. So I'm going to give you my favorite definition of risk and it does not come from a finance textbook. In fact, it's a Chinese symbol for risk. The Chinese symbol for risk or crisis which is a big risk is a combination of two symbols: danger plus opportunity. Risk is equal to danger plus opportunity.

I love this definition and let me explain why. It binds the two at the hip. It says if you want one, you got to live with the other, right? Who amongst us does not want opportunity? We all want to make 70% returns. What this definition reminds you is, if you want to make 70% returns, you better be willing to live with a lot of danger.

One way to think about risk and return models in finance is here's what we're trying to do. We're trying to measure the danger in an investment and convert that danger into question of, "Is there enough opportunity in this investment to compensate me for the danger?" We're not bungee jumpers in corporate finance. We don't take on risk for the sake of taking on risk. We take on risk because we think it comes with opportunities. Danger plus opportunity.

## Ingredients of a Good Risk and Return Model

So let's think about the ingredients that would go into a good risk and return model. So if you were constructing a risk and return model, here are the pieces you'd like to be part of that model.

First, you need to come up with a measure of risk that is universal. In other words, I don't want a measure of risk that applies only to stocks or only to U.S. stocks or only to bonds. I want a measure of risk that I can use across all kinds of stocks, across all kinds of investments. I want a universal measure of risk.

Second, a good risk and return model should be very specific about what types of risk I will get rewarded for and what types of risk I will not be. Specifically, if there are some risks that I'm exposed to that I'm not going to be able to get a higher return for, my risk and return model should be able to separate those risks from the risks that matter.

Third, once you've defined risk and said, "This is a risk that matters," you need to be able to come up with a model that converts that risk into a standardized risk measure. What's a standardized risk measure? It has to be self-standing. When I give you the risk measure for a company or a stock or an investment, you should be able to look at the number that I gave you and say, "That's a high number. That's an average number. And that's a low number." I'll give you a measure of risk that's not standardized. If I told you that the standard deviation of a stock was 35%. Think about it. You're looking at the 35% asking, "Is that a high number? Is that an average number? Is that a low number?" It'll be much better if I could give you a risk measure, we could look at that number and say, "Hey, that's a high number," just by looking at it.

Fourth, you should be able to translate that risk measure, the standardized risk measure you came up with into a measure of expected return. Remember the end game here is you've got to come up with a hurdle rate, 12%, 15%, 18%. You need to be explicit about that expected return.

And finally, there's an unpleasant requirement with one that you have to meet anyway. If you have a good risk and return model, it should actually work. Over time, if you come up with the expected returns and you observe what investments actually deliver on average and over long time periods, the model should work.

## The CAPM Against the Five Requirements

Now, that's a lot of stuff you're requiring of a good model. But let's try this out on the oldest risk and return model in finance which is the capital asset pricing model, or the CAPM. In the CAPM, here's how we approach the five steps.

First, you define risk in terms of variance of actual returns around an expected return. I'll come back and be more explicit about what I mean by that. But risk is defined as variance.

Second, in the capital asset pricing model, only that portion of variance that you cannot diversify away as an investor will get rewarded. So that's what we're going to focus on.

Third, that non-diversifiable portion of variance is converted into a number called a Beta. A Beta is scaled around one, it's standardized around one. So if I told you that the beta for a stock is 1.2, you don't need to know anything else to conclude that this stock is riskier than the average stock because that number is greater than one.

Fourth, I will take that beta and come up with an expected return, and in the CAPM, the expected return for an investment is the riskfree rate plus the beta that I came up with in step three, times an equity risk premium. Inputs I need to estimate but I've converted my standardized risk measure into an expected return.

And here's the final piece, and this is the piece that I'm not that happy to talk about. The CAPM is a great model, but it's not that good a model at explaining what goes on out there. It's at least as good as the next best model. That's damning with faint praise, but that's all I can say in its support. In fact, it's a model that doesn't work that well but it works at least as well as the next best model. So at least for the moment, we're stuck with a capital asset pricing model even though I will offer you some more terms.
