# Session 1 Part 2 — Broad Themes of Corporate Finance

So let's use that big picture to extract some very broad themes that are going to run through this class all through the sessions.

## Theme 1: Corporate Finance is Common Sense

So here's the first theme. If you think about what I just said in the big picture, you probably saw that much of what I mentioned is common sense. Take the investment principle, for instance. I'm essentially saying that when you take projects, you want to make sure that you demand a return based on the risk of the project. And if that return, that hurdle rate, is 12%, I said, don't take a project if it makes eight, nine or 10%. And also, if you can find a mix of debt equity that minimizes that hurdle rate, go for it. You're saying, I knew that already. I don't have to take a corporate finance class to get that.

Well, that should come as no surprise. And here's why. Corporate finance as a discipline is only 60 years old. You can trace it back to a paper written by Merton Miller and Franco Modigliani in 1958. People have been running businesses for thousands of years. It is hubris on the part of corporate finance to think that until we came along and laid out the theory, people ran businesses randomly. Good business people through the ages have always understood these first principles because they are common sense.

## Theme 2: Corporate Finance is Focused

Here's a second theme. Corporate finance is focus. You know where the focus comes from? Remember I said the objective in corporate finance is to maximize the value of your business. One objective, a singular objective. Everything we do in corporate finance is viewed through that objective. That's if you look at a project and say, what's the best way to decide whether to invest? I look at whether that decision increases my value as a business. Everything we do in corporate finance is focused because we have a singular objective.

That's good news and bad news. It's good news because focus is good. It's bad news because to the extent that you disagree with that objective of maximizing the value of the business, nothing we do in corporate finance is going to make sense. So I'm going to spend some time defending that objective.

## Theme 3: Companies Age Like Human Beings

Let's move to a third theme. The third theme is that companies, just like human beings, age. You have startups like babies. You have young growth companies, they're like teenagers. Growth companies are the peak of your life. Then you start maturing and you go into decline. I'm going to argue that the emphasis you put on the different principles is going to shift as companies go from starts to being in decline.

Early in the process, the emphasis will be on the investment principle. After all, your value comes from future growth, you better make good decisions to increase the value of that growth. As companies mature, you're going to see the financing principle become more important, and as companies going to decline, you're going to start to see the dividend decision come to the forefront.

In this page, for instance, I have two companies. One is Cad, and the other is Linn. Ced is a mature company. Mature company in what sense? Most of its value comes from assets in place. Conned is a regulated utility that serves New York state, and as a consequence, there isn't much growth potential left in the company. So big decisions for Conned are financing decisions and dividend decisions, not much in terms of investment decisions.

The other company is Lincoln. Lincoln was a young growth company when I looked at it, and you can see that reflected in how much of its value comes from future growth. Not surprisingly, its value is going to be driven by how well it makes investment decisions, and it can't afford to borrow money. Why? Because it doesn't have the cash flows to service the debt, and it really isn't even thinking about dividends because it's not at that stage in its life cycle yet.

So you're going to see me use the life cycle construct as we go through this class to talk about why companies do very different things when it comes to each of these different principles.

## Theme 4: Corporate Finance is Universal

Let's move to the fourth theme. The fourth theme is that corporate finance is universal. In fact, I think it's unfortunate that we call it corporate finance. And here's why. Corporate suggests that everything we're going to do is designed for corporations, big, publicly traded companies. Nothing could be further from the truth. Corporate finance is something that every business, small or large, private or public, US or emerging market, has to face somewhere across the process. So no matter what kind of business you plan to join or start, the corporate finance principles that we're going to talk about will apply to you.

In fact, I'm going to argue that corporate finance principles probably matter more for young companies, small private companies, and emerging market companies than they do for large developed market corporations, and here's why. If you make a mistake, you might have no fallback if you're a small private business.

## Theme 5: Don't Violate First Principles

Now, here's the final thing. As I mentioned, corporate finance has some very broad first principles, the investment principle, the financing principle, and the dividend principle. The investment principle says, don't make investments that earn a return less than the hurdle rate. The financing principle says, find a mix of debt and equity that minimizes the hurdle rate. And the dividend principle says, if you cannot find investments to make the hurdle rate, don't try to take an investment, give the cash back.

Now, these first principles get violated almost on a daily basis at companies, and often they get violated by people who should know better. People who are MBAs and PhDs who think that they're too smart, too bright, too well educated for the first principles to apply to them. Now, one of the cautionary notes that I hope to come back to over and over in this class is if you violate first principles, I don't care who you are or where you went to school or how sophisticated your models are, somebody's going to pay a price for violating first principles.

So we will talk about violations of first principles, and in fact, let me give you a very simple example of how violating first principles can come back to bite you. Every year, you have companies that finance operations in one currency with financing in a different currency. And all too often, they're given advice by their bankers that this is okay. It's okay to borrow money in dollars to fund projects in Rupees or Rupa or Paso's. It's not okay. And in fact, all you need is one shock to the system for these companies to go under. So don't violate first principles.
