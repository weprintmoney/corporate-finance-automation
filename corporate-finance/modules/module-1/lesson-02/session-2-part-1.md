# Session 2 Part 1 — The Objective of Corporate Finance and the Utopian World of Stock Price Maximization

Before we start developing tools, techniques, and models in corporate finance, let's spend some time on the objective of corporate finance, the objective of maximizing value. So in this session, I'd like to lay out the assumptions that drive that objective and what can go wrong with those assumptions.

## What It Means to Maximize the Value of a Business

Now, remember the objective in corporate finance, the objective in corporate finance is to maximize the value of the business. What does that mean? We'll talk more about value later, but essentially everything we do in corporate finance, we're looking at what will it do to the value of a business. If it increases value, it's a good decision. If it decreases value, it's a bad decision, whether it's an investment decision, a financing decision or a dividend decision. I mentioned this gives corporate finance focus, but it's focus coming from that central objective.

So let's talk a little bit about what that objective even means. What does it mean to maximize the value of a business? To talk about this objective, I'm going to lay out that financial balance sheet that we talked about earlier. Remember in the financial balance sheet, you've got assets in place and growth assets on the assets side of the balance sheet and debt and equity on the other. When you talk about maximizing the value of the business, you're talking about maximizing the value of your assets, assets in place and growth assets. So if you're a company like Lincoln, a growth company, and you're the manager company, I'm going to measure you not just on how well you do on the existing investment you have on the ground, but on how well you're nurturing the value of your future growth.

## From Value of Business to Value of Equity to Stock Price

So, in theory, corporate finance is about maximizing the value of your business. Well, here's the catch though. The people who make decisions in companies, especially if they're publicly traded companies are managers. And those managers answer to stockholders. You're saying, so what? Well, if you're a manager and you're say, what do I need to make stockholders happy? You focus not on the value of the business, but in the value of equity. So often in practice, here's what you see happening. While people talk about maximizing the value of a business, they often are intent on maximizing the value of equity. You might wonder, what's the difference, but we'll come back and talk about the difference, but the focus has shifted from value or business to value equity.

Now if you think about maximizing the value of equity, you run into a second problem. Who's going to measure that value? You could hire a consulting firm or a bank to come in every year and measure the value of your equity. But that's subjective. You're saying, How do I know they're right? How do I know they're not biased? So here's the third link. If you're a publicly traded company, there is a market number for your value of equity, right? It's your market price. So in practice, here's what often happens rather than maximizing the value of equity, companies focus on maximizing the stock price.

So here's the sequence. You started trying to maximize the value of the business. You narrowed that down to maximizing the value of equity. You ended up focusing on maximizing stock prices. In many corporate finance textbooks, you'll see the objective stated as maximizing stock prices. That's not true, but you can see why in practice, it ends up being the objective that companies pay.

## The Utopian World and the Four Linkages

Now, if you pick that objective of maximizing stock price, is it okay? To answer that question, I'm going to lay out the Utopian world from which corporate finance is born. What do I mean? Well, there are some assumptions you need to make for maximizing stock prices to be the only objective you need as a company. And I'm going to list those assumptions in the form of four linkages.

The first is the linkage between stockholders and managers. After all, stockholders don't run the firm. The managers they hire run the firm. So I'm going to talk about the assumptions I need for that linkage to work. Then I'm going to talk about the linkage between lenders. People who have lent money to the firm and the company itself. Then I'm going to talk about the link between the company and financial markets, and I'm going to close by talking about the linkage between the company and society.

## The Four Assumptions Behind Stock Price Maximization

So let's list the assumptions you need to make for maximizing stock prices to be the only objective you need as a company.

You need to assume that stockholders have complete power over managers. In what form, through the Board of Directors and through the annual meeting, we assume that stockholders with such complete power over managers that managers are terrified of what stockholders will do to them, and they go out and maximize stock price.

Second, we're going to assume that lenders lend money to a firm, and even if they don't protect themselves, they don't get ripped off. So they could leave loopholes the size of a MAC truck, and the company doesn't exploit it. Why you might ask? Because it might worry about its reputation effect. It might worry about the fact that it might have to go back to these same lenders to borrow money. Whatever the reason, we assume that lenders are fully protected.

Third, we assume that companies reveal information about themselves honestly and on time to financial markets. Remember, I used the word utopian when I described the world that I was just going to define. Utopian in what sense? Well, it's unrealistic. I agree. But in this unrealistic world, we're going to assume that information gets to markets in time, and it's always honestly delivered. And markets react appropriately. We assume that markets are rational and cool about the way they assess that information and look at the impact and value.

Finally, in this Utopian world, there are no social costs or social benefits. Social costs are costs that businesses create for society that cannot be traced and charged back to the company. These could be environmental costs, they could be health costs, but they could in general be any cost that cannot be traced back to the company. We assume there are no social costs, and we assume there are no social benefits.

## Why These Assumptions Matter — The Only Way to Increase Stock Price

You wonder why I might need all these assumptions? Remember what I'm trying to do. I'm trying to make the world safe for stock price maximization. I'm essentially taking away all the bad ways in which you could increase your stock prices. And what could those include? They could include ripping off your bondholders. You could make your stockholders better off by making your bankers worse off. You could make your stock price increase by lying to financial markets by holding back information. I'm saying you can't do that. You could also increase your stock price by creating costs for society. But in this Utopian world, I've taken away that approach as well.

In this world, there's only one way to increase your stock price. It's the old fashioned way. You got to go, find a business need that's not been met, produce a product or service that people actually want and deliver it in a way that you can generate profits from doing so. That's tough to do. But in traditional corporate finance, if you lived in this Utopian world, that would be the only way in which you could increase your stock price.

So take a look at that objective, maximizing value. Start to understand the contrast between maximizing the value of a business, maximizing the value of equity, and maximizing the stock price. They may sound like they're the same thing, but they're actually three very different versions of that objective function.
