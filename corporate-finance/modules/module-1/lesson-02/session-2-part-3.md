# Session 2 Part 3 — Where the Power Lies: Shareholders, Managers, and Governance

So having looked at the utopian assumptions that drive maximizing stock price and what can go wrong with them, it is very clear that we need to assess where the power lies in a company. So let's start by looking at the choices. In the utopian world, the power lies entirely with shareholders, but it's a rare company where that is true. And there are some companies where the power lies with managers, still others where the power lies with the inside shareholders, some shareholders who have more power than others. In the case of Facebook, let's face it. Mark Zuckerberg has a lot more power than a Facebook shareholder like you or I. Power could also lie with the government, with lenders, with the regulators, depending on the environment you're in. Put differently, in every company, the start of the analysis is by looking at where the power lies.

## Disney in 2003: Institutional Investors and Manager Power

So let's look at some examples. One of the simplest ways to assess where the power lies in a company is to look at who owns shares in the company. So I'm going to start with Disney in 2003. This table lists the 17 largest shareholders in Disney. And as you look down the list, notice that almost every single one of these shareholders is a big institutional investor. You're saying, "So what?" Now, each of these companies that I'm going to show you, here's the question I'd like to answer. If I am a small shareholder in the company, what are the chances that there will be somebody fighting my fight for me? In the case of Disney, the answer is no one, and here's why. Institutional investors, when they don't like the way a company is run, tend to sell their shares and move on. They don't stand and fight. I don't think any of the institutional investors on this page are going to be inclined to fight a fight for me. Says a shareholder in Disney, "I have little or no power in the way this company is run." This is a company where I think managers are essentially going to run the company.

## Vale: Voting Class Shares and the Government's Golden Share

Let's move to a second case, Vale, the Brazilian mining company, or, really, the global mining company incorporated in Brazil. Vale has two class of shares, and already, you can see the opening. If you are a shareholder of the company, guess which class of shares you get. You get the shares with no voting rights. In fact, in Brazil, these shares are called common shares and preferred shares. Common shares are the shares with voting rights, and preferred shares are the shares without voting rights. Go figure. When you buy shares, you get the preferred shares. And if you look at the common shares, the voting shares, they're held by seven big entities, one of whom happens to be the Brazilian government. Individual shareholders in Vale have little power in the company because the seven entities that own the voting shares run the company. They appoint the board of directors. It gets worse. In the case of Vale, the Brazilian government has what's called a golden share. What's a golden share? It gives the government veto power over big decisions like acquisitions, like divestitures, big investments. In fact, in Vale, the management team serves as long as the government allows them to serve. This is a company where the government has the power, and you, as a shareholder, have to accept that sometimes, a company will do what's in the government's best interest, not necessarily what's in your best interest. Bottom line, as a shareholder in Vale, I know that this company is going to act, sometimes, in the best interest of the government.

## Tata Motors and Baidu: Family Groups and Cross-Border Shell Structures

Let's move to the third case, Tata Motors, which I described as a family group company. It's part of a group called the Tata Group, which has 100 plus companies in its fold. You're saying, "So what?" Take a look at the top 17 stockholders in Tata Motors. A significant segment of these 17 shareholders are other Tata companies. You're saying, "So what?" Well, they're going to vote the interests of the family group, and here's how it's going to play out. On decision after decision, if it becomes a choice between what's good for the family and what's good for shareholders in Tata Motors, the company is going to do what's right for the family group, not because it's a bad family, but that's what self-interest will lead them to do. So if I'm an investor in Tata Motors, I should expect them, sometimes, to act in the best interest of the group, even if it means that I'm hurt as a shareholder in the company.

Which brings me to my fourth and final example, Baidu. Baidu is a Chinese search engine, akin to Google. But Baidu, the shares are actually traded in the NASDAQ in the U.S. You're saying, "Why?" Because the Chinese government has restrictions on foreign investors buying shares in Chinese technology companies, so here's what Baidu has done. It's created a shell company in the Cayman Islands with an operating agreement with the Chinese company, and you're buying shares in the shell company. Think about it. You're a shareholder not in Baidu, the Chinese operating company, but in Baidu, the Cayman Islands shell company. You have little or no power in this company. The management and the board of directors are going to be determined by the Chinese operating company. And as a shareholder in Baidu, I've got to walk in with open eyes.

## Disney in 2009: How Steve Jobs Shifted the Power Balance

Now, if you look at Disney again a few years later... Remember I showed you Disney in 2003? This is Disney in 2009 just to illustrate that things can change. Take a look at the top 17 shareholders. Notice somebody new on the list? Steve Jobs has become the largest single stockholder in Disney by the time you get to 2009. Saying, "How did that happen?" Steve Jobs was the largest stockholder in Pixar when Disney bought Pixar, and Disney paid for Pixar with shares. And instantaneously, Steve Jobs became the largest stockholder in Disney. You're saying, "So what?" As a Disney shareholder, this actually makes me feel a little better because remember, what I want is somebody to fight the fight for me, somebody to contest the status quo, somebody to take on incumbent managers. And in 2009, I had an ally in Steve Jobs in making that a more even fight.

## Takeaway: Assess Power in Your Own Company

So when you look at your company, here's what I'd like you to start with. Take a look at the top shareholders in your company. You should be able to find that in the public domain. Or you have access to a dataset, you should be able to get to that data. Take a look at the top shareholders, and make your best judgment. And here's the judgment you're making. If I am a small shareholder in this company, I own 1,000 shares or 10,000 shares, what is the chance that my interests will be protected? And be honest with yourself because in almost every aspect of corporate finance, you're going to see this play out.
