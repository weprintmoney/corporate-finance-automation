# Session 17 Part 1 — The Financing Principle: Debt vs Equity

## Introducing the Financing Principle

So now that we've put the investment principle behind us, let's turn to the second principle in corporate finance, the financing principle. The financing principle, we're going to look at the right mix of debt and equity for a company and the right type of debt that it should be taking on.

So when you think about the right financing mix for a company, the choice you're making is between debt and equity. And I'm going to start up by arguing that every business, small or large, private or public, U.S. or emerging market, has to make this decision, and here's why. When you think about debt and equity, the form it takes will depend on what kind of business you're in. If you're a really small private business, equity will usually take the form of either sweat equity or equity you've invested in the business, your own money, and debt will usually take the form of a bank loan. As a company grows, that equity will become venture capital equity, and as it goes public, it might become common stock. Your bank debt over time might become corporate bonds, but ultimately, we're talking about debt and equity.

## Defining Debt and Equity by Characteristics

In fact, the best way to draw a contrast between debt and equity is not to look at what it's called but look at its characteristics. Remember the three criteria we used to define something as debt? A contractual commitment, tax deductibility, and loss of control? Well, if that makes debt, here's what makes equity. Equity is a residual claim, not a contractual claim. There is no tax benefit you get from that residual claim and you get to run the company. The closer you are to those criteria, the closer you are to the debt or equity end of the spectrum.

So as I talk about debt and equity, don't think of this as just a choice that publicly-traded companies have to make. This is a choice that every business has to make, and the trade-off we're going to talk about applies to each and every one of these businesses.

## Debt and Equity Choices Across the Lab Companies

So before we start looking at the right mix of debt and equity, let's look at the debt and equity choices of the four publicly-traded companies, the non-financial service companies that I'm running as lab experiments, Disney, Vale, Tata Motors, and Baidu. If you look at the debt they have, it varies widely. Baidu has very little debt, Disney, Vale, and Tata Motors have a significant amount of debt. Much of Disney's debt takes the form of corporate bonds, almost 92%. The remaining 8% is bank debt. But if you look at the other companies, they're tilted more towards bank debt. In fact, Baidu has only bank debt.

If you look at the maturity of the debt, Disney has a lot of short-term debt. About 49% of the debt is between 1 to 5 years, but they also have a segment of really long-term debt, more than 20 years. Vale has much more long-term debt as a median debt, 5 to 10 years, and as you go across, you're going to see shorter-term debt in the hands of Tata Motors and Baidu.

When you look at what currency the debt is in, Disney has about 95% dollar debt and about 5% foreign currency debt. And if you look across the companies, they all have a mix of both domestic and foreign currency debt, and that's going to be something we have to come back and examine, is that the right mix for them? And finally, when you look at fixed versus floating rate debt, floating rate debt is debt where the interest rate gets reset, it looks like Disney and Baidu have about 95% fixed rate debt and 5% floating rate debt, whereas Vale and Tata Motors have all fixed rate debt.

For the moment, what are we going to do with these numbers? Not much, but a little later in this process, we're going to come back and revisit these companies and ask a question, is this the right kind of debt for each of these companies?

## Debt Ratios Across the Corporate Life Cycle

So to get a sense of what the trade-off between debt and equity looks like, I'm going to go back to a device I introduced early on in this class. I talked about the corporate life cycle, I talked about how startups are young companies, then you have growth companies, then you mature, and then you go into decline. If you tell me where you are in the life cycle, I should be able to guess whether you should be using less debt or more debt, and here's why.

If you're a young startup, I would expect you to be funded almost entirely with equity, and here's why. You don't have the cash flows to service debt. As you grow as a company, I expect you to stay mostly equity funded, again, because you don't have the earnings to service the debt and you have a lot of risks. As you mature as a company, your debt capacity should open up because now you have more predictable earnings and cash flows and less need to put those cash flows into investments. In fact, as mature companies, you should have a significant capacity at least to borrow money. And finally, in decline, you should be able to pay down the debt and pay down the equity, and your debt ratio might actually go up.

If your firm is at somewhere in this lifecycle, you should be able to guess what kind of debt ratio it should have.

## Companies That Refuse to Act Their Age

That said though, one of the most common problems among corporations is corporations that refuse to act their age. These are young growth companies that have become mature companies but continue to fund themselves as if they were young growth companies. That's a mismatch. And part of the reason we look at this financing mix question is to remind those companies that they've mismatched their financing mix with where they are in the life cycle.

So perhaps the simplest way to think about the debt ratio is to think about where your company is in the corporate lifecycle. Is it a young company? Is it a mature company? Is it a declining company? Because if you can answer that question, you're already well on your way to assessing whether it should have very little debt, some debt, or a lot of debt. Try it, it works.
