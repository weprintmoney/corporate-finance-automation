# Session 14 Part 2 — Return on Invested Capital, Country Risk, and Company-Level Excess Returns

## Converting Accounting Earnings into a Return on Invested Capital

So I've got accounting earnings for my project, right? If I could somehow convert those earnings into a percentage return, you might be able to use that number to make a judgment on whether this is a good project or not. So that's what I'm going to try to do. To convert these accounting earnings, because they're dollar values in the very last line, into returns, I've got to scale them to something. And remember, these are accounting earnings. The logical thing to scale accounting earnings to is the accountant's measure of how much is invested in this project, and that's what I'm going to try to capture on this page.

I start with capital expenditures. Remember every time I make a capital expenditure, it goes into my book value. Every time I depreciate, that goes out of my book value. And if I work in capital, that adds to my book value. So every year, here's what I do. I keep track of the book value of capital that I've invested in this project. So that number starts at about 2.975 billion in year one and builds up over time before starting to level off.

So I now have a measure of what the accountants tell me I'm going to make on this project in the form of after-tax operating income, I have a measure of how much accountants tell me I've invested in this project in the form of a book value of capital. I divide the after-tax operating income by the book value of capital. I come up with the return on invested capital on this project each year. Now incidentally, I can compute that return on invested capital based on the book value of capital at the start of each year or I can use an average book value of capital. It doesn't seem to make much of a difference here because, on both measures, here's what I find. If I look at the average accounting return on capital on this project over the next 10 years, the average return on capital I get is about 4.1% to 4.2%.

## Choosing the Right Hurdle Rate

So here's a question I'm asking you. Would you invest in this project with a 4.1% or 4.2% return on capital? Is that enough for you? If you think about it. To make that judgment, you have to compare this return on capital to a hurdle rate, right? Should that hurdle rate be the cost of equity or the cost of capital?

Well, if you think about what I've just done, I've taken the after-tax operating income and divide it by invested capital. The answer should be pretty straightforward, but let's work through it. Clearly, you don't want to compare the return on capital to just a risk-free rate. This is not a risk-free investment. You shouldn't be comparing return on invested capital to cost of equity. If I computed return on equity, which would be net income and book equity-based, then I could have looked at cost of equity. So I should be comparing it to the cost of equity. So no risk-free rate, no cost of equity. So you think, "It should be cost of capital." But which cost of capital? The cost of capital for Disney as a company, which is 7.81%, or just of theme parks? And we've answered this question already in a prior session. It should be compared to the hurdle rate for the theme parks. Why? Because it's a theme park investment reflecting the risk of that business.

Four point one percent or 4.2% depending on how you compute return on invested capital is lower than the 6.61% theme park cost of capital. So you're ready to reject it, but there's one loose end that I still want to tie up here. The 6.61% theme park cost of capital that I have comes from looking at Disney's existing theme parks in 2013. And in 2013, here's where their existing theme parks were, Orlando, Anaheim, Tokyo, and Paris, all developed markets. You think, "So what?" The new theme park that I'm thinking about building is in Rio, in Brazil. So there is a potential that this could be a riskier investment, right?

## Adjusting for Country Risk in a Foreign Project

So here's a broader question I have for you. When a company invests in a foreign project, a project outside its domestic market, should I be adding a risk premium for that foreign project? The answer seems, "Of course." Well, that really depends on what kind of risk you're thinking about. There are two types of risk you're going to be exposed to when you invest overseas, in a foreign project. And here are the two types of risk.

The first is foreign exchange risk. When a U.S. company invests in Germany, it's exposed to the fact that the euro/U.S. dollar exchange rate can vary over time, right? Now, if you're a company like Disney, I'm going to argue that that type of risk should not create a risk premium, and here's why. Remember those marginal investors in Disney, the State Streets, the Barclays, the Fidelities, the top investors in Disney? They can take care of themselves. They can hedge away or deal with exchange rate risk. I'm going to assume that exchange rate risk is diversifiable risk to that. So the only risk that Disney were exposed to is foreign exchange risk, I wouldn't care.

But when you invest in a project in Brazil, it's not just the foreign exchange risk you're worried about, you're worried about country risk, political risk, economic risk, that can undercut your project. For that, I think we need to add a premium. And there is a way in which you can adjust your cost of capital for the additional risk for the fact that this project is in Brazil, and here's what I'm going to do.

Remember we talked about country risk. We talked about how riskier country should have larger equity risk premiums. In fact, for Brazil, we estimate an additional risk premium of 3% based on the default spread for Brazil of 2% and the fact that equity in Brazil is much riskier than debt. So I'm going to use a higher equity risk premium for Brazil in computing my cost of equity. That higher equity risk premium pushes up my cost of equity to 9.16%. Notice I'm still using the U.S. dollar risk-free rate because I'm doing everything in dollars. And I'm still using the bait of a theme parks, that doesn't change, but because of a higher risk premium, I end up with a higher cost of equity. That higher cost of equity fed into my cost of capital gives me a cost of capital for this project of 8.5%.

So if you're looking at estimating a hurdle rate for a project for a company, not only do you need to know what business this project is in, you also need to know what country the project is in because they both play a role in what kind of hurdle rate you come up with for this project.

## The Project Decision and the 10-Year Cutoff Problem

So we're almost there. Here's what we have. We have a return on capital for this project. It's about 4.1% to 4.2% depending on how you measure the return on capital. That's lower than the cost of capital that we just estimated of 8.5%. That seems like an easy choice, right? Don't take the project.

But before you make that decision, here's something I'd like you to think about. That return on capital of 8.5% was based on projecting numbers for the next 10 years, but I stopped after year 10. You think, "So what? You've got to stop somewhere." This is a theme park. If you look at Disney's existing theme parks, Disneyland is more than 50 years old, Disney World is more than 40 years old, and they produce more cash flows for Disney now than they did at the start of their lives. Theme parks become cash cows after about 10 or 15 years. I'm being unfair to this project by stopping after 10 years. Not much I can do in an accounting return measure, but perhaps when I talk about cash flows, there is a way I can bring this in.

## Company-Wide Return on Invested Capital and Economic Value Added

We close this session with a tangent. I've just used return on capital to decide whether to take a project, right? By taking the after-tax operating income on the project and dividing my invested capital, I could compute a return on invested capital for an entire company. And here's all I need to do. If I take the after-tax operating income for Disney as a company and divide by the invested capital for the company, I come up with the return on invested capital for the entire company of 12.61%.

You think, "So what? What does that tell me?" To the extent that I trust my accountants, it looks like Disney is making a 12.61% return on its existing projects. Is that good enough? Remember the cost of capital for the entire company was 7.81%. It looks like Disney is generating a 4.8% excess return over and above its cost of capital to the extent that you trust that return on capital.

You can do this for each of the remaining companies. And you can see the numbers. You can see that Vale generates about a 2.22% excess return, Baidu generates about 18% excess return, Tata Motors is about a 9.5% excess return, and Bookscape actually generates a return on capital less than its cost of capital. Now, if you want to dig deeper, you can think about the comparative advantages that these companies have that allow them to generate their excess returns. But if you're analyzing a company, it might be worth it for you to try to compute what that excess return is for your company.

Incidentally, if I take that excess return I just computed, the 4.8% for Disney, and multiply it by the invested capital, I come up with a measure called the Economic Value Added, a very widely used measure about a decade or two decades ago, but one that really adds very little to the discussion because all it does is it takes a percentage spread and makes it into a dollar spread.

So here's what I'd like you to try for your company. I know that you might not be able to compute a return on invested capital for an individual project but you should be able to compute a return on invested capital for your entire company, the after-tax operating income divided by the invested capital. Compute that number, compare it to your cost of capital. See if your company generates a return greater than its cost of capital, roughly equal to its cost of capital or less than its cost of capital, and then follow up by asking why might that be.
