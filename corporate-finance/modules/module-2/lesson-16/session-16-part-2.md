# Session 16 Part 2 — Dealing with Uncertainty in Investment Analysis

So I introduced the notion of uncertainty as I ended the last part of the last session, and I talked about things you could do to make yourself more comfortable. Let me be more explicit about things you can do after you've done a valuation or a capital budgeting to make yourself more comfortable.

## Payback: The Simplest Response to Uncertainty

Here's the first and the oldest way that analysts have figured out how to deal with uncertainty. Think about the Rio Disney theme park and what worries you the most. It's a big infrastructure investment, right? Disney is spending almost $5 billion building these theme parks, and you're worried that if theme parks go bad that's a lot of money to lose.

So here's a simple question you might want to ask before you embark on the analysis or the final judgment. You might want to ask a question, "How long will it take me to make my initial investment back?" I know that's not enough that you want to make a return on your investment, but wouldn't it make you feel much better if you were able to make your $5 billion back and then you could think about everything else as icing on the cake?

There's a simple way you can get an answer to that question. Just add up your cash flows, your incremental cash flows until you get to your $5 billion. That's basically what I've done on this particular table. I've looked to see how long it will take Disney to make its initial investment back. It's called payback, and by counting the numbers, the answer I get is 10.3 years. Now, if you want to set the standards higher and say you want to make your initial investment back plus your acquired rate of return, it'll take you about 17 years.

You're saying, "What do I do with this?" Many companies impose what's called a payback constraint, and here's how it works. They will accept a project with a positive net present value, but only if the payback is less than some specified number of years. Five years or three years. Effectively, it's their way of constraining themselves from the risk that occurs when your estimates go wrong. So payback is the simplest way of thinking and dealing with uncertainty.

## What-If Analysis and Presenting Results

There's a slightly more sophisticated way of dealing with uncertainty. You can ask what if questions. What if questions about pretty much every assumption I've made in this analysis. So what if my revenue judgments are wrong? What if my revenues come in at less than expected? What if my cost estimates are wrong? What if my cost of capital is wrong? And you could effectively look at what will happen to your net present value as you change each assumption holding all as constant. That's called a what if analysis, and the net effect of that word of analysis is pretty predictable. You're going to get a range of net present values depending on the choices you make on individual variables.

A couple of caveats or warnings that I would suggest when you do what if analysis. I'm going to say something very strange, but I'll back it up. I think it's become far too easy to ask what if questions, far too easy relative to when? Relative to 20 or 30 years ago when doing a what if analysis required a lot of work. Today with Excel spreadsheets and advanced models, you can change inputs and get an answer almost immediately. So people are tempted to ask what if questions about pretty much every assumption they make in an analysis. My suggestion is keep it focused, pick two or three variables, the key variables, and ask what if questions about them, and here's my follow-up. Once you've asked the what if questions rather than give me table after table of analysis with different numbers, paint me a picture, show me how those what if questions might or might not change your analysis.

You're saying what kind of picture? I'm going to give you one of my favorite pictures of all time. This is actually from a book that's about 150 years old, it actually is a graph that shows Napoleon's ill-fated expedition to invade Russia in the early 1800s. So here's the best way to read this graph. Napoleon leaves Paris with a huge army and the breadth of the graph, the brown portion of the graph tells a picture of Napoleon marching across Europe towards Russia. Notice that the broad band becomes narrower and narrower as it gets and further and further into Russia, that's him losing army soldiers. Why? Because it's getting colder. He gets to the outskirts of Russia, and he decides, "It's really cold. I want to go home." So there's where the black bar picks up. There's Napoleon's army picking up from Moscow marching all the way back to France. By the time he ends up in Paris it's Napoleon carried home by three guys. That tells you the story of Napoleon's expedition to invade Russia far better than an extended novel would have. That is a good graph. You and I face a much simpler task, all we have to explain is how the net present value will change. As margins change or revenue growth changes, let's be a little more creative in the way we present our results. So that's a second way you can deal with uncertainty, ask what if questions.

## Monte Carlo Simulations

There's a third and perhaps more sophisticated way of dealing with the uncertainty. If you think about it, here's what's making you uncomfortable. When I asked you what the revenues were on this theme park, you were allowed to give me just one number of point estimate. When I asked you what the costs were, you were allowed to give me only one number, 60%. In reality, though, there is uncertainty about these numbers. Your revenues could be much higher or lower than expected, your cost could be 60%, or they could be 70% or 50%. In fact, in the real world, when we think about assumptions, reality is that each of these assumptions comes from a distribution. And normally, there's not much you can do about it, but there is a technique that allows you to bring this uncertainty into analysis. It's called a Monte Carlo simulation.

Let me use the Rio Disney theme park to illustrate how it would work. Remember those revenue estimates I gave for the theme park? Let's assume that I'm uncertain about them and that the actual revenues could be 20% higher or 20% lower and I'm not quite sure where we'll end up. So that's called a uniform distribution, and you see that as the first distribution I feed in. The second distribution I have is about expenses being 60% of revenues of the theme parks. Let's say I'm not certain about that because it's a real theme park. It could be much higher than 60%, perhaps as high as 75%, or as low as 45%, and there what I think the most likely number 60%, what's called a triangular distribution with an expected value of 60%. Let's take the cost of capital, a big part of the cost of capital is the country risk prem for Brazil. Brazil is a volatile country. Country risk prems go up and down so even though I have an expected value of 3% the actual number could be much higher or much lower, so I put it in a distribution, a normal distribution. I now have three distributions for three big assumptions.

Here's how a simulation works, I go pick an outcome out of the revenue distribution. Now, that number could be higher than my base number or lower. I keep it. Then I go to my cost distribution, pick a number. That number could be higher than 60 or lower than 60. I keep it, and then I pick a number out of the country risk distribution, higher than 3% or lower than 3%. I keep it. Then I compute the net present value of this project using those outcomes, and I repeat this over, and over, and over again. How often? In a typical simulation, you could run this distribution 10,000, 100,000 times, and you're saying to what end? At the end of the process, you're ending up with a net present value for the project, you end up with the distribution of net present values. Because each simulation gives you a different net present value.

You're saying, "What do I do with this?" The median of this entire distribution is about $3.3 billion, very close to what we got with the base case using all that work to get to the same number. But here's what you learn in addition, there's a 12% chance that the net present value will be negative. In other words, there's a 12% chance that the project could be a bad project. You get your best case and your worst case outcomes here. Your best case is your net present value could be $8.5 million, your worse case, it could be minus a billion. You're learning a lot more about the project than you did than when you run a best case analysis. I think your decisions will be enriched by having this distribution rather than a single number. Of course, to run a simulation, you need to review basic statistics, and you do need a simulation add on. I use a program called Crystal Ball that I add on to Excel that allows me to do this on pretty much any analysis that I have in Excel. Give it a shot.

## Risk Hedging: When to Get Rid of Risk

So at the end of this process you have some ways of dealing with uncertainty, but the reality is you still have to make a decision. There's one final question I want to answer. When you look at risk, one of the instincts that you have is to get rid of risk. Makes you feel more comfortable, but be careful.

Let's take an example. In this case, Disney, when it invests in a real theme park, is going to be exposed to exchange rate risk, right? Because [inaudible] our U.S. dollar exchange rate could do things that you didn't predict. Could you hedge those exchange rate risks? Absolutely. Should you? That's a different question, and here's why.

At first sight, you might say what's the harm in getting rid of risk? Well, there's no harm if it costs you nothing. So if it costs you nothing to get rid of a risk, if you can hedge risk costlessly, go ahead. Nobody's hurting. You could potentially be helped. But let's say it costs you money, as is usually the case to hedge a risk. You have a follow-up question to ask. Does hedging that risk create a benefit that is greater than the cost? Those benefits can be tax benefits. They can be benefits coming from the fact that you make better decisions. They might even be benefits in the form of lower discount rates, but you have to weigh the benefits against the cost. If the benefits are less than the cost, even though it makes you feel more comfortable getting rid of the risk, don't. If the benefits are greater than the cost, then you have a follow-up question to ask. Is it cheaper for you to get rid of the risk? Or is it cheaper for your investors to get rid of the risk?

Let me take exchange rates again. It is true Disney could hedge every exchange rate risk it's exposed to, but it's also true that the investors in Disney, remember those margin investors like State Street and Barclays, could probably hedge that risk much more cheaply. Because all they have to do is hedge it across their stocks and their portfolio. It might be cheaper for your investors to hedge that risk, and if so, he should let the risk pass through. The way I think about risk management is you have to be careful about what risks you hedge, what risks you pass through, and what risks you seek out and exploit. That, to me, is good risk management.

## Side Costs and Side Benefits

There's one final lose end I want to tie up. You've taken the cash flows in the project in this country to come up with a net present value. But in doing all of this, you've acted like this is a standalone project. When an established company looks at a project, often there are side costs and side benefits, and they're often brought in after the numbers have been crunched and, in my view, that's a mistake. Because that allows you to overcome the numbers and essentially do whatever you wanted.

So here's my suggestion, when there are side costs and side benefits, it's your job to bring those side costs and side benefits into the cash flows or into the discount ring. The side costs can include the costs associated with the resources you already own, an employee who already works for you or distribution center that you might already own. But there is a cost with using those resources. A side benefit might be something like synergy, when Disney opens at Rio theme park, maybe, maybe more people in Brazil will watch Disney movies. That's a side benefit. Those benefits and costs can be quantified, can be brought into cash flows and can be shown in your returns. And that's what I think you should be doing.

Now, in this part of the analysis there's not much you might be able to do because you need access to the individual projects that companies analyze. But that shouldn't stop you from creating hypothetical projects based on what you know about a company. Think about what the pattern of cash flows will be. Think about potential side cause and side benefits, but most critically, start thinking about the healthy ways in which you can deal with uncertainty.
