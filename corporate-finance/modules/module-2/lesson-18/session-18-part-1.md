# Session 18 Part 1 — Cost of Capital Approach to Optimal Debt Ratio

## From Trade-Off to a Specific Answer

In the last session, we talked about the trade-off when debt versus equity. Debt, we said, has tax advantages and, perhaps, makes companies more disciplined, but it comes with bankruptcy costs, agency costs, and lost flexibility. But at that stage, if I asked you what the right debt ratio for your company is, what the right financing mix is, you could not give me a specific answer. In this session, we're going to fill in that gap.

So let's talk about the first approach to estimating the right mix of debt and equity for your company. I'm actually going to use a tool we've talked about already, the cost of capital. If you remember, the cost of capital is a hurdled rate that we used to decide whether to take investments or not, and it does serve that purpose. But there's another role that the cost of capital has that makes it useful in answering the question, "What's the right mix of debt and equity for my company?"

## Why Minimizing Cost of Capital Maximizes Firm Value

And here's what it is. If you asked me to value your entire business, here's how I'd do it. I would take your cash flows as a business...these are pre-debt cash flows...and discount them back at a cost of capital. The present value is the value of the business. Take a look at that equation. In the numerator, you got the cash flows. In the denominator, you've got the cost of capital.

Now, let's hold the cash flows fixed and lower the cost of capital. What's going to happen? Obviously, the value's going to go up. In fact, at the limit, if I minimize my cost of capital, I've maximized my value as a business, right? You're saying, "So what?" Well, that is the objective in corporate finance is to maximize the value of the business. And if I stay true to that objective, perhaps, perhaps, if I can find a mix of debt and equity that minimizes my cost of capital, I've found my optimal debt ratio.

Now, if you think about the mechanics of doing this, here's what I would need to put this approach into practice. I would need to tell you what your cost of capital will be at every debt ratio from 0% to 99%. You're saying, "What's the big deal?" Well, remember, as my debt ratio goes up, my cost of equity will go up. Why? Because my equity in the same company is going to get riskier at higher debt ratios. As my debt ratio goes up, my cost of debt will also go up. Why? Because debt gets riskier as I borrow more money. My cost of capital is a weighted average of those two numbers.

## XYZ Widget Company: The Abstract Example

So let's start with a very simple abstract example, XYZ Widget Company, so to speak. This is a table that gives you the entire cost of capital schedule for that company so that every debt ratio is in my cost of equity, and I've stuck true to my story. As my debt ratio goes up, my cost of equity goes up. As my debt ratio goes up, my cost of debt goes up. But as my debt ratio goes up, notice initially what happens to my cost of capital. It starts at 10.5%, and it keeps dropping until I get to about 40% debt. After that, it reverses itself and starts rising. The optimal debt ratio for this company, if I stay true to the notion of minimizing cost of capital, is 40% debt.

In fact, in the last column, I've computed the value of this business, assuming it's a mature company, by changing just the cost of capital. And not surprisingly, if I minimize my cost of capital, my value as a business is maximized. That sounds simple, right? In fact, if all you had to do was take the schedule and find the optimal debt ratio, computing your optimal debt ratio for a company would be trivial if you had the entire schedule. In fact, if you could convert that into a table, not surprising, the table reveals exactly the same thing. My cost of capital takes on a U-shape. It bottoms out at your optimal, in this case, 40%, then rises. My value as a firm reverses that U-shape. It's maximized where my cost of capital is minimized.

## From One Point to a Full Schedule: The Disney Problem

Now, could it be this easy? If I gave you a real company and asked you to draw this cost of capital graph, could you draw the U-shaped graph? Well, think about it. I've talked a lot about Disney so far. In fact, I've computed a cost of capital for Disney, but I have only one cost of capital. At its existing debt ratio, I can tell you exactly what that cost of capital should be. The challenge here is I don't have a graph. I have a point. For most companies, what you have is one cost of capital at the existing debt ratio. You don't have an entire schedule like you did for XYZ Widget Company.

So here is the task you face in taking the cost of capital approach and applying it to a company. Let's take Disney. Let's get that one cost of capital that we already know for the company nailed down. This is actually a reproduction of something we did a few sessions ago as the hurdled rate for Disney. Disney is about 88.4% equity, 11.6% debt. Its cost of equity at its existing debt ratio, based on a bottom-up beta, is 8.52%. Its cost of debt based on its existing rating of A is 2.4%. Its weighted average cost of capital at its existing debt ratio is about 7.81%.

You're saying, "Now what?" One way to frame what I'm going to try to do for Disney next is to ask the question. If I change my mix of debt and equity from what it is today, the 88.4% equity, 11.6% debt to a different debt ratio, could I lower my cost of capital? Because if I could, you could argue that I should go to that debt ratio.

## Building Cost of Equity and Cost of Debt at Every Debt Ratio

So let's, at least, set up the steps that I will have to go through in order to get the cost of equity and the cost of debt for Disney at every conceivable debt ratio. In many ways, the way I've approached estimating these numbers is leading up to this process because it's going to lend itself pretty easily to what I'm going to do next.

First, I need a cost of equity at every debt ratio, right? I know what the cost of equity is at its existing debt ratio based on the levered beta that I came up with for Disney at that debt ratio. But the way I got that levered beta is I started with an unlevered beta and then levered it. I've given you an opening. As your debt ratio changes, you should be able to come up with a levered beta at every debt ratio by coming up with a new debt to equity ratio and a new beta. I should be able to give you a beta and a cost of equity at every debt ratio. That's easy have.

Let's talk about the cost of debt. The way I estimated the cost of debt for Disney at its existing debt ratio is I used the actual rating. If I changed the debt ratio, the rating's agency's not going to telegraph to me what the new rating for Disney will be at 20% debt, 30% debt, 40% debt. I have to estimate what Disney's rating will be as their debt ratio goes from 11% to 20% to 30% to 40%, all the way up to 90%. But there again, I've given you a mechanism you can use to come up with those ratings, right? Remember the synthetic ratings process where I computed an interest coverage ratio and computed the rating based on that interest coverage ratio? I'm going to use that process.

At every debt ratio, here's what I'm going to do. I'm going to compute the interest expense that Disney has at that debt ratio, and obviously, as I borrow more money, that interest expense will rise, and I'm going to use it to come up with the coverage ratio. Now, I'm going to use that coverage ratio to come up with the rating. As I borrow more money, my coverage ratio is going to decrease, my rating is going to fall, and as my rating falls, my cost of debt is going to go up. I'm also going to keep track of my interest expenses to make sure I can get my entire tax benefits, but when I'm done, I'm going to have a cost of equity and a cost of debt for Disney just like I did for XYZ Widget Company at every debt ratio. I'm going to look to see where I minimize my cost of capital, and I want to complete the process by telling you what Disney's value will be if they go to the optimal.
