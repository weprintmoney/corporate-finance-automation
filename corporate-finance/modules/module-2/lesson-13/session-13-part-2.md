# Session 13 Part 2 — Bringing Cost of Capital Together

## Disney: Bringing It All Together

So now we've talked about cost of equity in debt, we've talked about debt weights, let's bring it all together. Let's start with Disney. With Disney I have all the numbers I need to compute the cost of capital for the company.

Let's start with equity. The cost of equity for Disney is 8.52%. And we go through part by part in that calculation to see what the numbers are that drive that cost of equity. I started with the risk-free rate of 2.75%. That's a U.S. 10-year T-bond rate. Why? Not because Disney is a U.S. company, but because I'm doing my analysis in U.S. dollars. The beta that you see for Disney, 1.0013, is my bottom up beta for Disney, not the regression beta. And the equity risk premium for Disney is 5.76%, that's a weighted average based on where Disney does business. The compounded effect of all three numbers is the 8.52% that you see as my cost of equity. The market value of equity, because Disney is a publicly traded company, share price times number of shares is $121.9 billion.

Let's move to the debt. To get a cost of debt for Disney, I start with the exact same risk-free rate, 2.75%. But I had to default beta 1% based on the fact that I know Disney's rating. It's single A. I multiply that by 1 minus the marginal tax rate, 36.1% for Disney, I end up with the cost of debt after taxes for Disney of 2.4%. To get my market value of debt, I added the market value of the interest bearing debt, $13 billion, to the market value of leases, the debt component of leases of $2.9 billion. The total debt that I end up for Disney is $15.96 billion.

You take those two numbers, market value of equity and market value of debt, they convert into weights. The weights I get for equity and debt at Disney November of 2013 is 88.4% equity, 11.6% debt. The cost of capital is just a weighted average. The cost of equity and the cost of debt, 88.4% times 8.52% plus 11.6% times 2.4% gives me a cost of capital for Disney of 7.81%.

## Divisional Cost of Capital: Disney and Vale

Now, let's extend this process by looking at the divisional cost for Disney. Remember we computed a divisional cost of equity for the broadcasting business, the movie business, etc? Let's take those costs of equity, use the debt ratios we estimated for each of the businesses and come up with the cost of capital for each of the businesses. Process remains the same, but notice again how different the cost of capital are for different businesses. The cost of capital for the theme park business is 6.6%, but the cost of capital for the interactive gaming business is almost 9%. Disney overall has a cost of capital of 7.81%.

For Vale, again, I did exactly what I did with Disney. I took the cost of equity by business, brought in the cost of debt after taxes, and I do assume that all of the businesses carry the same cost of debt as a company because they all borrowed through the company. In the case of Vale, though, the weights remain the same, the debt weights remain the same across the companies, and I end up with the cost of capital in both U.S. dollars and nominal reals for each of the businesses that Vale is in.

## Tata Motors, Baidu, and Bookscape

So let's extend the concept to the other companies in my sample. To estimate the cost of capital for Tata Motors in Indian rupees, I started with the cost of equity, 14.49%, and the cost of debt of 6.5%. That cost of debt is an after tax cost of debt. The weights that I estimated for Tata Motors based on market values is about 29% debt, 71% equity. The weighted average cost of capital in rupees terms of Tata Motors is 12.15%.

For Baidu in renminbi, the cost of capital is 12.42%. And it's very close to cost of equity for a very simple reason. Baidu is about 95% equity, 5% debt. Its cost of equity is 12.9%. Its cost of debt after taxes is 3.45%.

For Bookscape, I computed two different cost of capital. The first was reflective of its true standing as private business. I used the total beta to come up with the cost of capital of 10.3%. That's because the total beta is high, the cost of equity is high, and the cost of capital reflects it. I then recomputed the cost of capital as if it were a public company, and ended up with a 6.57% of cost of capital. You're getting another view of that differential advantage that I said public companies have over private businesses. The public company here will have a much lower cost of capital than a private business.

## Cost of Equity vs. Cost of Capital as Hurdle Rate

So I'd like you to try to compute the cost of capital for your company using those market value weights you computed and the cost of equity and the cost of debt. That cost of capital is going to become a hurdle rate for your company.

At this stage, you might be puzzled, because earlier when we talked about cost of equity, I described the cost of equity as a hurdle rate. And now I'm talking about the cost of capital, and I'm calling the cost of capital a hurdle rate as well. You're saying, "How can they both be hurdle rates? After all, for Disney, the cost of equity for the company is 8.5%, the cost of capital is only 7.8%." They both can be hurdle rates, and which one you use will depend on how you compute your cash flows and how you measure returns.

That might sound mysterious, but let me give you the broad themes. If you estimate cash flows to equity investors, cash flows left over after debt payments, or you compute a return in equity, which looks at net income in the book equity, your hurdle rates should be the cost of equity. If you're looking at cash flows prior to debt payments, cash flows of the entire business, or return on overall capital, your hurdle rate should be the cost of capital. If you're still mystified, we'll come back and add some flesh to the statement, when we talk about investment analysis.

## Wrap-Up

So at this stage you have everything you need to compute the cost of capital for your company. You should be able to get a cost of equity based from a bottom up beta, a cost of debt based from the synthetic rating or an actual rating, and the weights based from market values. Come up with the cost of capital for your company, compare it if you want to your cost of equity for the moment. Hold on to both numbers because they might come in handy when you start thinking about which hurdle rates to use as you do investment analysis.
