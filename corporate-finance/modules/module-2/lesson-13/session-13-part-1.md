# Session 13 Part 1 — Weights for Cost of Capital

## From Cost of Equity and Debt to Cost of Capital

In the last few sessions, we've looked at how to estimate the cost of equity for your company and the cost of debt after taxes for your company. In this session, we're going to bring them together into a cost of capital. To convert the cost of equity and the cost of debt into a cost of capital, you need to attach weights to your debt and equity. Those weights will reflect how much of each you use in funding your business.

Now, the simple rule in all of corporate finance is those weights have to be market value weights, the market value of equity and the market value of debt. As opposed to what? As opposed to book value weights. The book value of equity and the book value of debt is what you get when you look at the balance sheet of your company, so the shareholder's equity that you see in a company is the book value of equity, and what you see as debt in the balance sheet is the book value of debt.

## Why Book Value Weights Don't Hold Up

Now, there are lots of companies that still insist on using book value weights when they compute cost of capital. And I've heard all kinds of reasons given for why this is right. None of them stand up to scrutiny, but let's take a look at a few.

The first reason I've heard is that the book value is more reliable than market value. Why? Because it doesn't change as much. Now, let's take the part of that statement that's true. It is true that book value does not change as much as market value, and we know why. Book value changes only when accountants revisit your books, which might be once every quarter if you're a public company or once every year if you're a private company. That doesn't make it more reliable. In fact, I would argue that the fact that book value does not change, it makes it less reliable.

The second rationale I've heard for using book value is it's just being conservative. The argument I've heard is, hey, the accounting numbers are more conservative. Let's use those numbers. Let's see if that's true. When I use book value weights for debt and equity, I'm going to come up with much higher weights for debt and much lower weights for equity for most companies, and here's why. The typical publicly traded company in the U.S. trades at about two and half times book value. The market value of equity is about two and a half times book value. But the book value of debt and market value of debt are pretty close to each other. So when I use market value weights, I'm going to weigh equity more and debt less. When I use book value weights, I'm going to weigh equity less and debt more. You think, "So what?" Remember that the cost of debt is much lower than the cost of equity? If I use book value weights, for most companies, I'm going to end up with a lower cost of capital for a company, not a higher cost of capital. That doesn't strike me as conservative.

So book value weights are not more reliable. They're not more conservative. What's the last argument? The last argument I've heard is a strange one. One of the numbers we compare the cost of capital to is the return you make on your invested capital as a rough proxy of how well a company is investing. Now, when you compute return on invested capital, we use book value numbers, and we'll talk about why in a few sessions. But the argument is because you use value numbers when you do return on invested capital, you should use book value weights for the cost of capital. That doesn't stand up to scrutiny. The market value of equity and debt is still what you would be raising if you went out and raised capital for this company today, no matter what its book value is. There is no good reason for using book value weights.

## Getting Market Values of Equity and Debt

So in order to compute a cost of capital, no matter how much you might want to do otherwise, you have to compute market values of equity and debt. Now, the publicly traded company getting the market value of equity should be relatively simple, right? Share price times number of shares. Getting the market value of debt, though, can be problematic, and here's why. Even if your company has bonds outstanding where you can look up the market price, a significant portion of a company's debt will be bank debt, debt that's not traded. Getting a market value for that debt is problematic, and that's why investment banks when they do cost of capital or consulting firms when they do cost of capital, when they get to this point in the process often assume that the book value of debt is equal to the market value of debt. Most of the time, you can get away with that assumption, but there's a simple way in which you can compute book value of debt to market value of debt that I'd like to take you through.

## Disney Example — Converting Book Debt to Market Debt

Let me use Disney to illustrate this process. Disney has a lot of debt on its books. In fact, if you look at its balance sheet, the book value of debt for Disney is about $14.3 billion. I'm going to try to convert that book value of debt into market value of debt using an approach that we use to compute the market price for a traded bond. Do you remember what the price of a bond is? It's the present value of the coupons and the face value discounted back at a fair market interest rate today, right? So I'm going to take all of Disney's debt and treat it as if it were a publicly traded bond.

So hang in there because here's what I'm going to do. I'm going to take the $14,288 million and make that the face value of my bond. I'm going to take the interest payments that Disney made in the most recent year which is the income statement of $349 million and make it the coupon payment on my bond. And finally, I'm going to go to the footnotes of Disney where they break their debt down by maturity and get an average maturity, a weighted average maturity, based on when the debt comes due of 7.92 years and make that the life of my bond.

So in effect, here's what I have. I have a bond with a face value of $14,288 million, a coupon payment of $349 million, and a maturity of 7.92 years. Remember I have a pre-tax cost of debt for Disney already based on its rating of 3.75%? I discount these cash flows back or I price this bond at a 3.75% rate. So what you have is a coupon of $349 million for 7.92 years and $14,288 at the end of the period. Discount it back at 3.75%, I'd get a market value of $13 billion. I have, in effect, converted my book debt to market debt. You can do this for most companies. So why waste your time assuming book debt is equal to market debt when you're just one step away from computing the market value of debt? So the $13 billion becomes the market value of the interest bearing debt.

## Adding Lease Commitments to Debt

You think, "What else is there?" Well, remember we said lease commitments are debt as well. So to compute the total debt at Disney, I have to convert those lease commitments into debt, and here's what I need to do to convert lease commitments to debt. I first need to find out what those commitments are for the future. These are not expected lease payments. These are actual contracts or commitments. U.S. companies are required to report these in a footnote, and Disney does as well. What they're required to do is reveal what their commitments are for the next five years and then give you a lump sum beyond the fifth year. So beyond the fifth year, Disney provides a lump sum of $1,784 million, and I could leave that all in year six, but that's unrealistic. Disney is unlikely to pay the entire amount in year six, so I spread it out as a five year lease commitment beyond year five. I end up with 10 years of lease commitments. I discount these lease commitments back to today using the 3.75%, the pre-tax cost of debt as my discount trade. Why the pre-tax cost of debt? Because these are pre-tax lease commitments. What I get is a present value which is $2.9 billion becomes the debt value of leases.

You add on the $2.9 billion to the market value of the interest bearing debt, you get the total debt outstanding at Disney. So the total debt for a company reflects not just its interest bearing debts but the lease commitments as well. The total market value of equity reflects, of course, all shares outstanding.

## Applying This to Your Company

The first step in computing a cost of capital is computing what the weights for debt and equity would be using those market values. So if you have a publicly traded company, try this. Start out with the easy number first. Getting a market value of equity should be easy for your company. Share price times number of shares or just look up the market capitalization. If you can, though, I would like you to compute the market value of your debt as well. If you have no leases, that requires converting your book data into market data. If you do have leases, you've got to bring in the lease commitment into your process and compute the debt value of leases. Those will become your weights for you cost of capital.
