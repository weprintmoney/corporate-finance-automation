 asset is the present value of the expected cash flows in the asset. You're trying to value business

 company. You can do a relative valuation or appraising. You know what you're trying to do here?
 You're trying to attach a number to a company based on how similar companies are being priced out

 growth is that it allows your earnings to grow over time. The earnings become larger. The bad side

 value of growth. What are your cash flows from existing assets? What's the value of growth? The
 third question I've to ask is how risky are your cash flows? And the place that shows up in your

 show up in your base year numbers. The value of growth shows up in the growth rate you use and the

 You focus on the cash flows equity investors get from the business and you discount those cash
 flows back at the rate of return that equity investors demand for investing in the business. In

 equity-focus, you can try to value the entire business. What does that mean? You look at the
 collective cash flow that equity investors and lenders get from the company. Equity investors get
 their cash flows to equity. Lenders get interest payments and principal payments. That collective
 cash flows called free cash flow to the firm. You discount that cash flow to weighted average of
 the cost of equity and the cost of debt which is the cost of capital. What you get as a present
 value is the value of the firm. You've got to make the decision early in the process. You have to
 decide when you sit down to do valuation, are you valuing just the equity or are you valuing an
 entire business? Now, you might wonder, "How do I make that choice?" I'll give you a simple rule.
 When you cannot estimate free cash flows to equity for whatever reason, you might have to focus on
 just dividends. All too often, that was what analysts used to do when they valued banks. They'd
 say, "Look, we cannot estimate free cash flow to equity. We're just going to discount dividends."
 If you can't estimate cash flows, then the choice becomes more complex because you can do cash flow

 changing debt ratios over time, estimating cash flow to equity becomes a pain because you got to
 estimate how much debt gets repaid, how much debt gets taken on. So, when you have shifting,
 unstable, or unpredictable debt ratios, you should be valuing the entire firm. If you have a
 predictable debt ratio that's not going to change over time, then you can do an equity valuation.
 Nine out of 10 discounted cash flow valuations that I do are firm valuations because it's very
 difficult to find a firm with a debt ratio you have today is the debt ratio that you're going to
 have forever. So, let's assume you've made that choice, equity or firm. Let's go back to the notion
