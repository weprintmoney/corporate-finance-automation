# Session 36 Part 2 — Corporate Finance First Principles Wrap-Up

## The Trade-Off on Debt

So now that we have a measure of the quality investment, what next? To look at what next, let's think about the trade-off on debt. On the trade-off on debt, I talked about the two significant benefits that debt brings into the game. The biggest is the tax benefit. Interest is tax deductible; cash flows to equity in most parts of the world are not. So when I borrow money, I create a tax benefit. The higher my marginal tax rate, the greater the tax benefit, the more I should borrow. There is a secondary benefit to debt. Sometimes, in some companies, borrowing money can make the company more disciplined in the way it picks projects. That can help you in terms of increasing value.

Those two benefits have to be weighed off against three significant costs. The first is a bankruptcy cost. When you borrow money, you increase the likelihood of bankruptcy. The expected bankruptcy cost is a product of that likelihood of probability of bankruptcy and the cost of bankruptcy. The cost of bankruptcy can be either direct cost, the cost occurred after you go bankrupt, or an indirect cost, which is a function of what happens to your company when people think you're in trouble. There is an agency cost. Every time you borrow money, you create a potential for conflicts, conflict between equity investors and lenders, and it's usually resolved by putting constraints on the company, covenants in loan agreements. As you borrow money, you're increasing those constraints, and that's got to be factored into your debt decision. And finally, every time you borrow money, you give up some future flexibility. So from a purely qualitative standpoint, if you ask me, should I borrow money, it's this trade-off that drives that decision.

## The Optimal Mix of Debt and Equity

Now, you can, of course, convert this trade-off into an actual choice. So when we look at the optimal mix of debt and equity, we bring that trade-off into play into the cost of capital. So here's what happens. As you borrow more money, you are replacing more expensive equity with cheaper debt. That's good news, but as you borrow more money, here's what you're doing to your cost of equity and your cost of debt. Your cost of equity is going to become higher because your equity investors bear more risk when you borrow more money. We show this as a higher beta and a higher cost of equity. Your lenders will also see more risk in you as you borrow more money, so the default spreads that they charge will go up, and with that, the cost of debt will go up. It's the net effect that drives your cost of capital. And as we saw, as you initially borrow money, your cost of capital might go down, but as you borrow more and more money, there's a tipping point at which your cost of capital starts to increase. Your optimum mix of debt and equity is the one that maximizes your value as a business by minimizing your cost of capital.

Now, in terms of getting to that optimal mix, we'd added some caveat. There are two basic questions you need to address once you have an optimal mix: how quickly do I need to move to that mix, and in what form? To answer the first question, we asked, how much urgency is there in the fact that your actual debt ratio is different from the optimal? For an under-levered firm, a firm with too little debt, the question we asked was, how likely is it that you'll be taken over? The greater the likelihood, the quicker the move has to be. With an over-levered firm, one with too much debt, the question we asked was, are you under bankruptcy threat? If you're under bankruptcy threat, you need to do something quickly. So the question of whether you move fast or slow to your optimal will depend on how much urgency there is in the process.

But once you've made the decision, you still have to decide how to move to the optimal, and there are two ways you can do it. One is you can recapitalize, change your mix of debt and equity. The second is you can take new projects. Which one you choose will depend on whether you've good projects. If you have good projects, my recommendation is you borrow the money to take those projects if you're under-levered, or raise equity to take those projects if you are over-levered. You'll get a double whammy. Not only do you move to your optimal mix of debt and equity and thus increase your value, but you also get a positive net present values of the projects you take.

Now, in terms of the right kind of debt for your company, the answer is very simple. Tell me what kind of assets you have as a company, the projects you take, and I'll give you the right kind of debt for your company. If your assets are long-term assets that generate cash flows in euros, the right kind of debt for you is long-term euro debt. By matching up your debt to your assets, here's what you do. You're able to borrow more money without exposing yourself to default risk. You get the best of both worlds. You get debt that behaves like equity. You get the tax advantages with all of the flexibility of equity.

## Dividend Policy and Valuation

In the context of dividend policy, the key number we talked about was potential dividend, the free cash flow to equity. The free cash to equity is the cash left over after you've met every conceivable need, CapEx, working capital, debt payments. That is what you can pay out. And remember, that free cash flow to equity can be a negative number. So let's start easy. If you have negative free cash flows to equity, you have no business returning cash to your stockholders. If you have positive free cash flows to equity, you can choose to return all of it or none of it. And if you decide to return none of it, your cash balance will increase. Is that a bad thing? Not necessarily if investors trust you with the cash. The key word I said in dividend policy is trust. To the extent that you've earned the trust of your investors, you can accumulate cash. If they don't trust you, they're going to demand their cash back, and who can blame them?

And finally, when you turn to valuation, make a decision just like you did in capital budgeting. Are you valuing just the equity in the business? In which case, here is what you need to do. Estimate the cash flows to equity, which can be in the simplest example dividends or in the more expensive example of free cash to equity, discount them back at the cost of equity, come up with the value of equity in the company. If you decide to value the whole business, the process gets more complex. You estimate pre-debt cash flows, free cash flows to the firm, you discount those back at the cost of capital, you come up with the value of the operating assets. Then tie up the loose ends. Add cash, add cross holdings, subtract debt, and if you have equity options, take care of those before you come up with a value per share.

## The Three First Principles of Corporate Finance

Overall of this, I've kept referring back to the big picture. So let me return and end this class with the same big picture that we started this class with. Let me repeat again the three principles that govern corporate finance and let's see if, at the end of 36 sessions, there's a little more heft to each of these principles.

The first principle is the investment principle. Here's what it says: "When you as a company go out and make investments, make sure you earn a return that is greater than your hurdle rate." But now, we have a basis for those returns. Those returns should be based on cash flows, should reflect the timing of those cash flows, and have all side effects built into them. That's investment return process. The hurdle rate should reflect the risk of the investments, and we've talked about what type of risk should be in the hurdle rate, and should also reflect where you get your money, the mix of debt and equity.

The financing principle has two sub-principles to it. The right financing mix for your company is the one that minimizes your hurdle rate and maximizes your value, so look for that mix. And the right kind of debt for your company is the one that best matches your assets.

And finally, there's a dividend principle. If you cannot find investments that make your hurdle rate, find a way to get the cash back to your stockholders. Whether you do it in the form of dividends or buybacks will depend on what kind of stockholders you have and what they prefer.

## Closing

So the first principles of corporate finance are simple. It is in the process of practicing them that we make them much too complex and we end up making mistakes. So when in doubt, go back to these first principles. It's amazing how often they can give you the right answer to even the most complicated questions you face in running a business.
