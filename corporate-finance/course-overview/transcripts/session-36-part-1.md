## Lesson 1: Corporate Governance and Conflicts of Interest

So let's summarize the 10 lessons that I hope you've taken out of this class. The first is we started with the notion of corporate governance, but if you boil it down to basics, we were talking about conflicts of interests. There are lots of different entities in a modern public corporation, and they're not all pulling in the same direction.

In fact, broadly speaking, there are at least four groups you got to keep happy in a company, and each has its own interests. The first are stockholders who want to maximize the value of their shares and get the highest returns they can. The second are bondholders who want to protect their money and make sure that they get paid their interest payments. The third are managers who might want to maximize their compensation and protect their jobs. And finally, the society, hanging out there. And if it's a publicly traded company of a fifth group, investors, who are making their best judgments in the company.

It should come as no surprise then that sometimes when you see decisions made by a company, they're made to advance the interests of one group at the expense of another, and that is at the heart of corporate governance. Not everyone will agree on every decision being the best decision from their interests.

## Lesson 2: Measuring Risk and the Marginal Investor

Second, we talked about how to measure risk, and if there's one broad lesson that I hope you got out of this is when you look at the risk in a company, it depends on who's looking at the risk. In fact, with publicly traded companies, we introduce the notion of a marginal investor, the investor at the margin who sets prices. And that marginal investor, we said, needs to meet two criteria. One, he or she needs to own a lot of shares and trade those shares.

Well, based on that assumption, with most companies especially if they're large to mid-cap companies, you can assume that the marginal investor's probably some kind of institution. Very few individuals have the amount of money that will allow them to be marginal investors. So what? You might ask. If that institutional investor, a mutual fund, a pension fund, is diversified, the way you measure risk in a company changes forever.

The risk we bring into the cost of equity for publicly traded companies reflects that assumption. The only risk we bring in to the cost of equity is the risk you cannot diversify away because we assume the marginal investor being diversified is going to get rid of the rest over their portfolio.

## Lesson 3: Beta and the Components of Cost of Equity

Third, we measured that risk with a beta, and that beta, of course, captures the risk that you cannot diversify it away. But once you start thinking about how to measure the risk, think about all the decisions that go into your cost of equity.

The first is the choice you make about what currency you do the analysis in. We talked about picking high inflation versus low inflation currencies, but more importantly, we said be consistent. Once you pick a currency, everything has to be done based on that currency.

In fact, if you break the cost of equity down into its components, each piece plays a role. Your choice of currency drives your risk-free rate, and that risk-free rate is embedded in it and expected inflation which you should also bring into your cash flows. Your beta reflects the business or businesses you're in, and we talked about bottom-up betas being much better choices than regression betas. That beta also reflects choices you make as a company as to what kind of business you're in, what your fixed costs are, and how much debt you carry. And finally, the equity risk premium reflects where you do business. If you are a developed market company but you operate in a lot of emerging markets, it is my job to make sure your risk premium carries that effect.

See, risk-free rate reflects the currency you do the analysis in. Your beta reflects the businesses you're in, your equity risk premium reflects where you do business.

## Cost of Capital and Cost of Debt

When we talked about cost of capital, we move past the cost of equity by bringing the cost of debt. And let me restate again what the cost of debt and the cost of capital is supposed to measure. It's a cost of borrowing money long-term today. Key words are long-term and today.

So to get to a cost of debt, we start with the same risk-free rate from which we start our cost of equity, but we add a default spread in sort of beta and in equity risk premium. The default spread measures your credit risk as a company, and to the extent that the tax code is tilted towards debt, we bring it into the cost of capital.

## Assessing the Quality of Investments

That cost of capital helps us assess the quality of investments. In fact, there are two ways you can answer the question, "Are my investments good investments?"

One is to where your equity had and focus on just equity investors. What does that mean? You look at the net income you make on the project, and you look at the equity invested in the project, you come up with a return in equity. You compare the return in equity to the cost of equity, you have a measure of the quality of your investments from an equity investor's standpoint. You could look at cash flows to equity, discounted the cost of equity come up with a value, a net present value based on equity investors in the project.

You could also look at the same projects from the perspective of the entire company. What does that mean? You measure your earnings as after-tax operating income. You measure what you've invested as invested capital: book equity plus debt minus cash. You return an invested capital that you get out of those two numbers, you compare to the cost of capital. Alternatively, you can focus on cash flows: cash flows to the entire business discounted back to the cost of capital.

So you can measure the quality of your investments either from the perspective of just the equity investors in the business, the entire business, and done right, you should get roughly the same conclusions.

## Summary of Lessons So Far

So let's summarize the lessons we've drawn so far. The first is if you look at a modern corporation, it's a web of conflicts of interest and you have to figure out whose interests are being served when you look at a particular decision.

Second, when you think about risk, it's not how much risk you and I see in investment that drives its cost of equity, it's what the marginal investors see in that.

Third, to convert that measure of risk into a cost of equity, you need a risk-free rate and an equity risk premium. That risk-free rate would reflect the currency you're working in. Your equity risk premium will reflect where your company does business.

And fourth, to make a judgment on the quality of your projects, decide whether you're looking at those projects as an equity investor or from the perspective of the entire business.
