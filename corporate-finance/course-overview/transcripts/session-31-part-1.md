# Session 31 Part 1 — Estimating Growth in Cash Flows

As we build up towards discounted cash flow valuations, we've talked about the first two inputs that drive value, cash flows and discount rates, in the last session. This session, we talk about that estimate that makes most of us uncomfortable, which is future growth. So let's talk about estimating the growth and cash flows for a company.

## Historical Growth

Now, if you look at most valuations and you look at where the growth rate in these valuations comes from, it comes from one of two sources. One is historical growth. Many analysts when they value companies look at the past. So if your company has grown 30% in the last 5 years, they project that as the growth rate for the next 5 years or the next 10 years. There's nothing wrong with that but there are a couple of things you might want to keep in mind.

The first is, historically, past growth has not been a very good predictor of future growth. You often see companies go from high growth rates to low growth rates overnight. The second is scaling up is hard to do. Let me explain what I mean. If you take a small company and you look at its history, it might have been able to grow at 75% a year for the last five years, but remember, in the process, it's become a much bigger company. Even if the characteristics of the company have not changed, the growth rate in the future is likely to be lower. So look at past growth, but don't let it be the driving force for your future growth.

## Outside Estimates: Analysts and Managers

The second way in which people get growth rates in valuation is they look at outside estimates. If you're an investor valuing a U.S. company, for instance, it is easy to get an expected growth in earnings for your company based on what analysts following this company are projecting for this company. It's called a consensus growth rate. You're saying, "What's wrong with that? After all, those analysts know a lot more about this company than you do." Well, that might be true, but if you use an analyst's estimate of growth, you've turned over your valuation, the most critical input, to someone else. Analysts are not that good at forecasting long-term growth. They're very good at forecasting short-term growth, growth in the next quarter, but that's not what should drive your valuation. So again, if you're valuing a company, look at what analysts are projecting as growth, but don't let that become your growth rate just because you trust them to do a good job.

The second way you can get an outside estimate of growth is to ask managers of a company what the expected growth in the company is going to be. Now, it's true. Managers know a lot more about the company than you and I do. But it's also true that managers cannot be objective. After all, it's their company. So my caution with outside estimates of growth is, by using them, we're turning over the most critical input in valuation to somebody else, and in the process, it's easy to lose control of your valuation.

## Fundamental Growth: Reinvestment and Return

So at this point, you're probably in the uncomfortable spot of saying, "How am I going to estimate growth if I don't use past growth and I don't ask other people what the growth rate is going to be?" There's a third way of estimating growth that I call a fundamental growth rate. And here's what drives the growth of a company. The growth of a company is not something that you and I can come up with or endow the company with. It's got to be earned. And here's what I mean about growth being earned. For a company to grow, it has to reinvest and reinvest well at the same time. In fact, algebraically, the growth in earnings for a company is a product of how much you reinvest and how well you reinvest.

Now, depending on whether you're doing an equity valuation or valuing an entire business, the way you measure how much you reinvest and how well you reinvest is going to be different. Let's start with equity earnings. If your focus is on equity earnings, you're going to measure how much you reinvest with the retention ratio. In the retention ratio, you look at the proportion of your net income that you put back into the business and you measure how well you reinvest with the return in equity, that's net income as a function of the book value of equity invested in a project. So if you have a 60% retention ratio and a 30% return on equity, your expected growth in equity earnings is going to be 18%.

Now, if you're focusing on operating income, your definitions of reinvestment and returns have to be different. The reinvestment rate, then, will be the percentage of your after-tax operating income that you put back in the business in the form of net CapEx and change in working capital. So if you take the net CapEx, CapEx minus depreciation, the change in working capital as a percentage of your after-tax operating income, that tells you how much of your after-tax operating income goes back into the business. And you measure how well you reinvest, not with the return on equity but with the return on invested capital. You look at after-tax operating income as a percentage of the total capital, not just the book equity, but the book equity plus the book debt minus cash invested in the project. Retention ratio times return on capital is expected growth in operating income.

The advantage of a fundamental growth approach to estimating growth is, when you want to increase growth, I'm going to hold you accountable. Accountable in what sense? I'm going to ask you, "Where is that increase in growth coming from? Is it coming from the company reinvesting more, which will mean less cash flows up front? Or is it coming from better investment?" In which case, you have to tell me where those better investments are coming from. It makes you think about the drivers of growth.

## Deutsche Bank Example and Normalization

So let's take a simple example, Deutsche, in 2008, to illustrate how this process have worked. In 2008, Deutsche had a return in equity of 19.45%. Its retention ratio was about 67%. Put differently, it held back 67% of its net income to put back into the business and was making roughly 19.45% as a return on equity in that investment. If we assume that Deutsche can maintain those numbers, because those are the numbers from the most recent year, Deutsche's expected growth rate will be about 13.04%. That's at the retention ratio times return on equity. It's as simple as that.

Now, you're saying, "Why assume that it can maintain those numbers? Well, 2007, the most recent year that I had data for, when I valued Deutsche in 2008, was a very good year for Deutsche. You could actually argue that, over time, those numbers would be difficult to maintain and you could replace the actual return on equity in 2007 with a normalized number. The way I normalized this number was I looked at the average return on equity between 2003 and 2007. And that number was lower, 11.81%. I also looked at the average retention ratio between 2003 and 2007, and that number was also lower, 46%. You take those normalized numbers, the product of those normalized numbers gives me an expected growth rate of 5.4%. Now, if I were valuing Deutsche in 2008, I would argue that that 5.4% is a much better estimate of growth than just using the last year, using historical growth, or using an analyst projection of growth. It's a number that's based on discipline. Discipline in what sense? It ties your growth to what the company does.

## Applying This to Your Company

So as you look at your company, here's what I'd like you to start thinking about. Look up the historical growth on your company or estimate it yourself. Look up what others are estimating as growth for the company, but that shouldn't be your end game. Then focus in on fundamentals. Look at how much of your earnings the company reinvested back into the business last year and how well it's reinvesting money. Those will become the foundation for your estimation of future growth.
