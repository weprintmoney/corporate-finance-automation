# Session 34 Part 1 — Stories, Numbers, and the Disney DCF Valuation

## Stories and Numbers: The Hidden Ingredient in Valuation

In these last few sessions, we've laid a foundations for the mechanics of discounted cash flow valuation: cash flows, growth rates, discount rates, terminal value, how to bring it all together by tying up the loose ends. In this session, I'd like to talk about going through the mechanics to actually valuing a company.

Now, when we talk about valuation, especially in the discounted cash flow sense, we tend to get caught up in the specifics, the mechanics of valuation. But I'd like to step back and talk about what I think is the hidden ingredient in good valuations. A good valuation is a bridge between stories and numbers. What I mean by that is, to value a company, you have to start with a story about the company. And that story has to become the numbers that you see in a valuation. In a good valuation, when you point to a number in a discounted cash flow valuation and you ask, "Why is that number what it is?" the answer should always be the story you tell about the company that explains that number. Good valuations connect stories to numbers. And people who are good at valuation tend to be good storytellers, can also do the number crunching.

Let me back up. Remember the Vale valuation I showed you? There was a story behind that valuation. The story I was telling about Vale was it was a mature company with significant comparative advantages. Mature company showing up in the low growth rate, the 2% growth rate in perpetuity, and a company with significant comparative advantages because I assumed it would earn a 17.25% return in capital in perpetuity.

## The Disney Story: Status Quo with Comparative Advantages

So let me set up the story I'm going to tell about Disney and convert that story into valuation. The story I'm going to tell about Disney is that it's a company that it's set in its ways. With the status quo, I'm going to assume that the company is going to continue to do what it does, which is, it earns more than its cost of capital because it has significant comparative advantages, reinvest fairly aggressively for a large company, about 54% a year. And because it is going to be able to grow at a growth rate faster than the growth rate of the economy, I'm going to assume a 10-year growth period. The story I'm telling about Disney here is an optimistic one. Perhaps not as optimistic as some people's stories, but more optimistic than others. You see that reflected in the numbers.

Over the 10-year growth period, here's how I came up with the numbers. For the first five years, I left the numbers at 2012, 2013 numbers. What do I mean by that? I left the return in capital and reinvestment rate at what they were in the most recent year. The story I'm telling is what company said in its words. Those numbers give me a growth rate of 6.8% a year for the first five years. I also assume that even though we have estimated that Disney has excess debt capacity, its existing management will continue to keep its debt ratio at what it is today, 11.6% debt ratio and a cost of capital of 7.81%. So for the next five years, the story I'm telling is a status quo story.

## The Transition Phase: Years 6 Through 10

What happens after year five? Well, I actually go to year 11 to look at what my mature Disney will look like, a lower return on capital of 10%, a lower growth rate of 2.5%, and a cost of capital of 7.29%. The year 6 through 10 that you see for Disney are really transition years.

So here's what I want my transition phase to do. I want to use my transition phase, year 6 through 10, to adjust my numbers from where they are in the first 5 years to where I think they will be after year 10. So let me take an example. Remember my growth rate for my first five years is 6.8%, reflecting my assumption that Disney will continue to do what they've done in the last year, 54% reinvestment rate and a return in capital of 12.6%? The growth rate I'm assuming after year 10 is 2.5%. Here's what happened between years 6 and 10. My growth rate decreases from 6.8% to 2.5% in linear increments. Why linear? This is the simplest way I could get the growth rate down. I do that with each and every one of my assumptions. My return on capital of 12.61% drops in linear increments to 10% by year 11. My cost of capital of 7.81% drops in linear increments to 7.29% after year 10. So year 6 through 10 are there just as a transition period to get me from high growth numbers to stable growth numbers.

## The Valuation Picture: Base Year and Cost of Capital

These numbers are what drive my valuation of a company. When I do valuations, I like to present my valuations not at spreadsheets but as pictures. And this is my valuation picture for Disney.

So let's take a look at the numbers. Let's start at the very top. I start with my base year numbers. My free cash flow to the firm in the most recent year reflects what Disney did in the most recent year. They earned a $6.92 billion after-tax operating income and they reinvested $3.732 billion of that $6.92 billion back into the company. That's what gave me a reinvestment rate of 54% and a return in capital of 12.61%. So those are my base year numbers. I've assumed, for the next five years, they will maintain those numbers. Maintaining a return on capital of 12.61% and a reinvestment rate of close to 54% gives me an expected growth rate of 6.8% a year for the next five years.

Now, if you go below the line, you see my existing cost of capital. And there, again, the number should be familiar because these are numbers we've revisited before. I start with the beta of 1.0013 that reflects the five businesses that Disney is in, the bottom-up beta. I used a risk-free rate of 2.75%. That's U.S. T-bond rate as of November of 2013. And I used a risk premium of 5.76%, reflecting where Disney does its business. The cost of equity I arrive at based on those inputs is 8.52%. The debt ratio of 88.5% equity, 11.5% debt, reflects Disney's choices in 2013, which I have assumed they will continue to make for the next five years. The cost of debt based on their rating is 2.4% after taxes. My cost of capital of 7.81% reflects Disney's existing policy on debt, which I assume they will continue for the next five years. What I'm trying to point out here is every number year can be traced back to an assumption that we've made about Disney at an earlier stage in the process.

## Terminal Value, Present Value, and Value Per Share

Between years 6 and 10, I bring the company down to mature growth. In mature growth, I give the company a 2.5% growth rate at 10% return on capital and a reinvestment rate of 25%. I also bring its cost of capital down to 7.29%. Those numbers give me a terminal value of close to a $190 billion. That's in year 10. I discount my cash flows, the cash flows for the next 5 years and beyond between year 6 and 10, and the terminal value back at the 7.81% for the first 5 years. But remember, after year five, my cost of capital also declines to 7.29%. This is a valuation where the numbers are in motion and you got to reflect that in your valuation.

What I get as a present value of all of my cash flows including my terminal value, is a $125.48 billion. I add cash. I also add the non-operating investment of cross-holding that Disney has, which is worth about $2.8 billion. I subtract out debt, and I subtract out a minority interest. This reflects the portion of a subsidiary that's been consolidated that doesn't belong to them. So the cross-holding effect shows up in two places. Once, as an add-on for a minority investment of cross-holding in another company that's a minority investment, and the second as a subtraction to reflect the consolidation of a subsidiary where Disney does not own a 100%. I get a value for the equity of $113.6 billion. I subtract out the value of the equity options that Disney has granted, and it's not a big number. Disney is a mature company and it's not a huge drag on its value. You subtract out the value of those equity options and you divide by the actual number of shares. Again, reminder, if you subtract other value of equity options, don't double-count by using a diluted count for the number of shares. The value per share that I get is $62.26.

## Holistic Valuation and Fair Value Judgment

This is an intrinsic valuation of Disney reflecting my status quo story that they would continue to do what they did in 2012 and 2013. Presenting a valuation as a picture is a good exercise because it forces you to see the numbers in your valuation and how they are all linked to each other. I don't like using this word but I will use it, nevertheless. A good valuation should be holistic. Every number should be connected to every other number. So when you want to change a number, you got to think through the consequences.

And based on my status quo story, the value per share that I have for Disney is $62.26. The stock was actually trading at about $68. That's within the margin of error. So my judgment, in November of 2013, would have been that Disney is close to fairly value.

So you have everything you need to bring your valuation to fruition now. Start with the story you're telling about your company. Connect that story to the numbers you've used in your valuation. Finish your valuation and see if you can present it as a picture. You're well on your way to being a valuation guru.
