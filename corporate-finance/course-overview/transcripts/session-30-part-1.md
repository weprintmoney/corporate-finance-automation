# Session 30 Part 1 — Cash Flows and Discount Rates in Valuation

## Three Definitions of Cash Flow to Equity

In the last session, we started our discussion on valuation by talking about two ways you can approach valuation. You can value just the equity in a business or value the whole business. And we talked about the inputs you need to value a company. In this session, I'd like to talk about two of those inputs: cash flows and discount rates.

When you're doing a discounted cash flow valuation of a company, you obviously need to estimate cash flows, but what are those cash flows? There are three definitions of cash flows you can get if you focused on equity investors. The first, and this is the oldest definition of cash flow, is to look at what investors actually get from the company. And equity investors get only one cash flow from the company, and that is dividends. So the oldest version of a discounted cash flow model, you discount dividends.

Increasingly over the last 20 years, as we saw in the section on dividends, companies have been returning cash not in the form of dividends but in the form of buybacks. So an expanded version of cash flow to equity investors looks at dividends plus buybacks, but you're still assuming that companies pay out what they can afford to do to their stockholders.

In the discussion of dividend policy, we also looked at potential dividends, where we estimate how much a company can pay out in dividends by looking at the cash flows left over after every conceivable need is met, reinvestment needs and taxes, and that cash flow is free cash flow to equity.

Three versions of cash flows for equity investors: dividends, dividends plus buybacks, and free cash flow to equity. That is, if you're valuing the equity in a company.

## Free Cash Flow to the Firm

But what if you're valuing the entire business? The cash flow you then have to estimate has to be a pre-debt cash flow, a cash flow before debt payments, but after taxes and after reinvestment needs. To estimate that cash flow, we're going to start with the operating income, not net income. We're going to act like we pay taxes on that operating income, and we're going to subtract out reinvestment needs. What we're left with is a free cash flow, but it's a free cash flow to the firm.

Note that the difference between free cash flow to the firm and free cash flow to equity is free cash flow to the firm is before debt payments, free cash flow to equity is after debt payments.

## Deutsche Bank: Dividends to Free Cash Flow to Equity

So let's talk about estimating these cash flows using the companies we've been talking about through this entire class. Let's start with Deutsche Bank. I valued Deutsche Bank twice, once in 2008 and once in 2013. In 2008, I valued Deutsche Bank the way I used to value all banks then. I valued based on dividends. Implicitly, I was assuming that dividends reflected the cash flows that Deutsche Bank could afford to pay out in 2008, and I did a dividend discount model.

By 2013, though, I was no longer comfortable discounting dividends, partly because Deutsche's dividends were in flux and partly because I'd lost faith in managers and banks paying out what they could afford to in dividends. So I estimated the free cash flow to equity. This is actually the computation I laid out when we talked about dividend policy. Would estimate the free cash flow to equity for a bank, I suggested you start with the net income and you subtract out the reinvestment in regulatory capital that the bank has to make.

Based on that measure of free cash flow, Deutsche's free cash flow to equity in the next year is negative. What does that mean? Not only does it say that Deutsche cannot afford to pay dividends, it says that Deutsche will have to raise equity over time to cover its requirements and reinvestment in regulatory capital. So free cash flow to equity for Deutsche in 2013 reflects the fact that I no longer trust the dividend payments to be what the cash flows of the bank are.

## Tata Motors and Disney: Estimating the Cash Flows

Now, let's expand this discussion to Tata Motors. To get the free cash flow to equity for Tata Motors, I followed the more conventional route. I started with net income. I subtracted out Net CapEx minus Depreciation. I subtracted out the change in working capital. And then I brought in the cash flows from and to debt, from debt in the form of new debt issues and to debt in the form of debt repayments. I got the free cash flows to equity every year. And you can see, the numbers shift over time and the equity reinvestment rate, which measures how much I'm reinvesting back of my net income, also shifts over time.

So free cash flow to equity is a cash flow left over after debt payments. Let's look at free cash flow to the firm, a pre-debt cash flow, and let's use Disney to illustrate this process. In the fiscal year ended September of 2013, here's what Disney's numbers looked like. It had operating income of $10,032 million. Notice the subtle shift away from net income. The effective tax rate that Disney paid across its income is 31%. Its CapEx for the year was $5.239 billion. That includes acquisitions, and its depreciation was $2,192 million.

To get to the free cash flow to the firm, here's what I'm going to do. I'm going to start with my after-tax operating income, which is $6.92 billion. From that, I'm going to subtract out my net CapEx, the difference between capital expenditures and depreciation, and I'm also going to subtract out my change in working capital because that drains my cash flows as well. My free cash flow to the firm for Disney is $3.188 billion. Note that these are cash flows before debt payments. Equity investors will get only a small slice of this, but it is an estimate of a pre-debt cash flow.

Incidentally, using those numbers, I can estimate my reinvestment rate, which will come in handy when we talk about growth. The reinvestment rate is my total net CapEx, which is $3,732 million, divided by my after-tax operating income of $6.92 billion. That gives me about 54%, and that is what Disney reinvested in the 2012-2013 financial year. So free cash flow to equity is after debt payments, free cash flow to the firm is before debt payments.

## Discount Rates: Cost of Equity and Cost of Capital

Let's turn our attention to discount rates. When you're doing a discounted cash flow valuation, are discount rates a critical ingredient in your valuation? Obviously. It is discounted cash flow valuation. That said though, I think we pay far too much attention to discount rates and valuation and too little attention to cash flows, but much of what we've said in the context of capital structure and investment analysis about discount rates is going to help us when we talk about discount rates when it comes to valuation.

Again, remember, if you're discounting cash flows to equity, the discount rate you need is a cost of equity. And for Deutsche Bank, I estimated the cost of equity in 2008 and 2013 using exactly the same techniques I used when I computed cost of equity for investment analysis. I started with the risk-free rate, and notice how much that shifted between 2008 and 2013. I used a beta that reflected a bottom-up beta, and that changed as well, and finally, an equity risk premium that reflected the price of risk. Put differently, everything in my inputs changed between 2008 and 2013, but my overall cost of equity for Deutsche Bank decreased slightly from 9.23% in 2008 to 8.8% in 2013.

Now, to compute the cost of equity for Tata Motors, I did my analysis entirely in repeat terms. Why? Because I'm going to do my valuation in repeat terms. I started with the risk-free rate in Indian rupees, 6.57%. I used the bottom-up beta for Tata Motors based on automobile companies. I multiplied that beta by an equity risk premium that reflects where Tata Motors operates, the 7.19%. Bringing those numbers together, my cost of equity for Tata Motors is 13.5%, exactly the same cost of equity I've been using for Tata Motors all the way through this analysis.

Finally, let's turn to Disney. Here, because I'm looking at a pre-debt cash flow, a cash flow to the firm, I need a cost of capital. To get to a cost of capital, I started with a cost of equity for Disney. The risk-free rate, the beta, and the risk premium that you see in that computation are the numbers we've already computed for Disney. The risk-free rate is a U.S. dollar risk-free rate. The beta is a bottom-up beta reflecting the five businesses that Disney was in in 2013. The equity risk premium reflects where Disney does its business. The 8.52% is my cost of equity.

To get to the cost of debt, I start with Disney's actual rating. I used a default spread, netted out the marginal tax rate. Remember that because the interest saves you taxes at the margin, you have to use the marginal tax rate, and the cost of debt after taxes I get is 2.4%. Looking at the weights for equity and debt in market value terms, I have 88.4% equity based on the market value of equity of $121.9 billion. And I have 11.6% debt ratio based on the market value of debt which includes leases of $15.96 billion. My cost of capital for Disney is 7.81%.

Incidentally, if that number looks familiar, it's a number that we used as a hurdle rate for Disney in investment analysis. It's also the number from which we started our discussion on optimal debt ratios for Disney in the Capital Structure section.

## Letting the Cost of Capital Change Over Time

The one final point I want to add when you talk about valuation that we did not talk about in the context of investment analysis and capital structure is in valuation, we forecast out cash flows for a company. And as we forecast out cash flows for a company, the characteristics of the company might change. In the case of Disney, for instance, when I value the company, I would start at a 7.81% cost of capital but I will let that cost of capital change over time. Why? Because I would expect the beta to change as Disney becomes a more mature company. I would expect the debt ratios to change as Disney becomes a more mature company. And as those two numbers change, my cost of capital which starts at 7.81% is going to end at 7.29%. So in valuation, the cost of capital can and should change over time as your company changes over time.

So think about doing this for your company. Start off by estimating cash flows in the most recent year. Don't get ambitious yet and try to forecast cash flows. Look at how much your company made and how much it reinvested. Also, if you've already got a cost of equity and cost of capital you've estimated for your company, get it ready because that's going to be the starting point for discount rates in your discounted cash flow valuation.
