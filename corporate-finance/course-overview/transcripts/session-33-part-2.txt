 Having dealt with cash and cross-holdings, we have a few more loose ends to tie up. So let's bring
 in the remaining loose ends that'll allow you to get to the value of equity per share in your
 company. Let's finish our discussion of cross-holdings first. In most companies, doing the value of
 the cross holdings right which requires the full financial statements of the parent company and the
 subsidiaries is going to be almost impossible to do. Why? Because many companies don't reveal the
 details you need about their cross-holdings to value them right. So here are two compromise
 solutions you can adopt. If your company has a cross-holding in another publicly traded company,
 you could take the market value of that publicly traded company and use that to estimate the value
 of your cross-holding. So let's say you own 10% of a publicly traded company with a market value of
 a billion. Ten percent of the billion is 100 million. It's cheating and here's why it's cheating.
 An intrinsic valuation, which is what you're doing when you discount cash flows at the cost of
 capital, you are assuming that markets make mistakes and that you cannot just trust a market value.
 And you're selectively ignoring that rule when you have a cross-holding. But if you have hundreds
 of cross-holdings in public companies, you might have no choice but to use the market value
 solution. Say what if I don't have a market value? What if my subsidiary is not publicly traded?
 Here's another cheat you can use. You have the book value of that cross holding, right? In your
 balance sheet? If you know what sector your cross-holding is in, you can convert that book value to
 a market value. Again, let me give you an example. Let's suppose you have a cross-holding in a
 chemical company and that that cross-holding is $100 million book value. And let's say that
 chemical companies typically trade at one and a half times book value. One and a half times 100
 million is 150 million. That becomes the estimated market value of your crossholding. It's an
 approximation but given the information you have, that might be as good a number as you can get.
 Now let's start to anything else you might want to bring into your valuation. Now remember, when
 you discount cash flows back at the costed capital, you value the operating assets. You brought in
 cash, you brought in cross-holdings. You should be done, right? But check around. Check around to
 see if there are other assets your company owns which you haven't counted yet. What might that
 include? Truly vacant assets. A piece of land that the company owns. A piece of real estate that
 it's not using. Some other assets that adds value but it's not reflected near operating income. You
 might want to bring that in. In some cases, especially in the US, we have companies which have
 defined benefit plans where essentially they promised a fixed pension to their employees to the
 extent that that benefit plan is overfunded. You have an overfunded pension plan, there are some
 people who add on that overfunding arguing that it belongs to the equity investors. But when you're
 counting these other assets, just be careful. You cannot double count. Let me explain what I mean
 by double counting. Let's assume you're valuing an oil company. You discount the cash flows back at
 the cost of capital, you come up with the present value right? You cannot take that present value
 and add to it the value of the refineries you have and other assets you have because those are the
 assets that generate the cash flows. Simple rule, you cannot count an asset and the cash flows from
 that asset in the same valuation. That's double counting. Let's do a final loose end. You've taken
 into account cash, you've looked at cross-holdings, you've brought in any other assets a company
 might have. You're almost there. You subtract out debt from that value for the business you have.
 You should have the overall value of equity for the company right? Here's the final stop you have
 to make to get to value of equity per share. In the last 20 to 30 years, in the US especially,
 companies have increasingly turned to paying their employees with equity. Equity in one of two
 forms, options and restricted stock. Restricted stock, you don't have a problem. Restricted stock
 show up in the outstanding shares, they're already dealt with. But when a company grants options,
 it creates a problem in discounted cash flow valuation because it creates a second claim on your
 equity. What do I mean by second claim? The overall value that you get for your equity now is
 claimed by two groups, one is the common stock holders through their ownership of shares and one is
 by managers through their ownership of options. You have to net out the value of that second claim
 if you want the value per share as a common stockholder. Incidentally, the way you have to deal
 with equity options granted to employees varies depending on whether you're looking at options that
 have already been granted or whether there are options that you expect to be granted in the future
 and let's lay out the rules on how to deal with each of these types of options. To the extent that
 you have options already outstanding and US companies report these in their financial statements,
 here's what you have to do. Value the equity as if there were no options. So take the cash flows,
 discount them back at the costed capital, do everything you have to do to get to the value of
 equity. Then value the equity options as options. You're saying, "What does that mean?" Don't value
 them based on the exercise value today. An option has a time premium. Value these options using an
 option pricing model. Then subtract out the value of these equity options from the value of your
 equity and then divide that remaining value by the actual shares outstanding. You don't have to
 adjust for the delusion because you effectively reflected the delusion in the value of equity
 options subtracted out from the value of equity. This is the right way to deal with options
 outstanding rather than trying to adjust the number of shares for options that might be exercised
 in the future. Value the equity, subtract out the value of equity options, divide by the number of
 shares. You're saying, "What about options that might be granted in the future?" Remember why
 companies use options to compensate employees. It's because they don't have the cash to play their
 employees. Treat it like any other compensation expense. What does that mean? If you treat it as an
 expense in the future, it's going to reduce your operating income and reduce your future cash
 flows. By reducing your future cash flows, it's going to reduce the value of equity. It might sound
 like double counting but it's not, because it's reading two separate claims on the equity. The
 first, we value the options and subtract them from the value of equity, takes care of past option
 grants. The second, we take into account the options in the future and reduce future cash flows,
 take into account future options issues. They both can hurt your value as an equity investor in the
 company. So to the extent that you've followed along up to this point, you're ready to come up with
 a value of equity per share of your company. So assuming you've added the cash and you've brought
 in cross-holdings into your valuation, here are the final two steps. Check around for any other
 assets you might have as a company. To the extent that these assets haven't been counted, make sure
 you're not double counting, add them on. Then subtract out the debt and if you have equity options,
 take care of them now. Take care of them right by valuing them as options. You're ready. You can
 come up with the value of equity per share. That is your intrinsic value for the equity per share
 in this company.
